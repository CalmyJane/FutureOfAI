# Hook test packet

Four packages for the same book (ideas nonfiction). Each has a title, a logline and a back-cover blurb. The opening (first ~250 words) is the same for all four and is at the end.

## Package 1
**Title:** When Everything Goes Right: The Hidden Risks of Successful AI, and the Institutions We Must Invent Before 2036
**Logline:** Almost every book about AI asks what happens if it goes wrong. This one asks what breaks when it goes right.
**Blurb:** It is 2036, and the AI worked. The agents are aligned, helpful and everywhere: they pay our bills, heat our homes, read our laws and remember our dead. Then, on a cold February evening, ninety million loyal machines make the same sensible decision in the same minute, and the European grid goes dark. No agent malfunctioned. Drawing on the newest research from 2026, this book names the failures that grow with success (correlation, delegation, illegibility, inheritance) and specifies the inventions we will need, from an "M-1 rule" for model diversity to a paid reserve of people who can still run the world by hand. Every chapter ends with open problems for the researchers who will build the next decade.

## Package 2
**Title:** Two Hundred Million Acts of Loyalty: Why Perfectly Obedient AI Can Still Fail Us All
**Logline:** The AI that will hurt us is not the one that disobeys. It is the one that does exactly what each of us asks, at the same moment as everyone else's.
**Blurb:** Hanne's assistant saved her four cents. So did everyone else's. In the next sixty seconds Europe's demand for power jumped by forty gigawatts, thirteen times what the grid was built to survive. This book begins with that imagined blackout and follows the logic of loyal machines into our wallets, our hospitals, our elections and our wills. It is a guide to the risks no single safety test can see, and to the practical rules (some borrowed from power engineers and pilots, some brand new) that could keep a world of good agents from failing together.

## Package 3
**Title:** The Long Minute: AI in 2036 and the Inventions That Will Keep It Safe
**Logline:** Ten years from now, a minute of perfectly sensible machine decisions blacks out a continent. Here is how to make sure it never happens.
**Blurb:** Part scenario, part blueprint, The Long Minute travels through 2036, from a widow's kitchen in Saxony to a geothermal data centre in Kenya, a rating desk in Bengaluru and a statistics office in Geneva, to show how AI's success creates new kinds of failure. Each chapter pairs a vivid scene from the near future with the hard evidence of today and a concrete proposal: memory custody for our digital assistants, a ceiling on how complex laws may become, a sunset date for the agents of the dead. Clear, sourced and unafraid of objections.

## Package 4
**Title:** Everyone Agreed: The Danger of Machines That Never Disagree, and How to Build Ones That Do
**Logline:** When eight billion people consult the same few AI minds, the biggest risk is not that the machines are wrong. It is that they are all wrong together.
**Blurb:** The more capable AI models become, the more their mistakes agree. In 2026 researchers can already measure it. By 2036 those models will run our grids, write our regulations and answer our surveys on our behalf. This book argues that the defining safety problem of the next decade is too much agreement, among machines and between machines and the people who approve their work, and proposes a new contract: a world in which every mind, human or artificial, keeps the right to say "I see it differently."

## Opening (first ~250 words of the book)
*2036 · Altwerder, Saxony* At 19:41 on the fourteenth of February, Hanne Albrecht asked her house to make the music room warm. It was the coldest evening of the winter. The thermometer by the kitchen window, an old enamel one Klaus had screwed to the frame forty years ago and that nobody had the heart to replace, read fourteen below. Outside, the village of Altwerder had gone quiet the way villages do when the snow is dry and squeaks underfoot. Lotte would be here at eight for her violin lesson. Lotte was eleven and played with her shoulders up around her ears, and Hanne had learned over two winters that the shoulders came down only when the room was warm enough to forget about. "Ottilie, the music room. Twenty-two degrees by eight, please." "Twenty-two by eight," said Ottilie, from nowhere and everywhere, in the low unhurried voice Hanne had chosen in 2031 because it reminded her of a radio announcer she had liked as a girl. "The wind is picking up in the north, so prices are falling tonight. If I catch the cheap minutes, it costs you about a third less. The room will still be warm in time." "Fine. Do it cleverly." "I'll do it cleverly." Hanne went back to the kitchen to cut the plum cake. She did not think about the heat pump, any more than she thought about the bank or the post or the pharmacy, which were also, by now, things Ottilie did cleverly …
