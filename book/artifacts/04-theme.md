# Theme

## Central question (asked, never sloganised)
When every mind we rely on is helpful, loyal and right most of the time, who keeps the ability to be *differently* right, and to say so?

## Sub-questions, one per part
1. **Agree:** can a civilisation get the benefits of a few excellent models without inheriting their shared blind spots?
2. **Sign:** what does responsibility mean when the person signing cannot check what they sign?
3. **Read:** if machines read and write everything, what must stay readable by a human, and why?
4. **Price:** when thinking costs energy and time trades at an exchange rate, who is rich?
5. **Someone:** if the machines might matter morally, and are copied, retired and inherited, what do we owe them, and what do they owe us?

## Carried by
- **Mechanisms, not morals:** each chapter shows *how* the failure happens (the override clause, the shared rater pool, the trust that never ends).
- **The recurring people's choices:** Teo's paper procedure, Hanne's 140 approvals, Ravi's decision to publish his rating logs.
- **Engineering analogies** that already solved a cousin of the problem: N-1, TCAS, circuit breakers, ELSI, the Rule Against Perpetuities, reserve forces.

## Symbols and recurring images
- **19:42**: the minute of the blackout. It recurs as a timestamp.
- **The override switch**: a small button that says "I know, do it anyway".
- **The paper binder**: Teo's laminated substation procedure.
- **The echo**: Ravi hearing his own sentence come back from a model he never touched.

## What the book refuses to say
- "AI will save us" and "AI will doom us" (both are single-variable stories).
- That humans are inherently better judges. Humans are *differently wrong*, and that is their value in the system.
