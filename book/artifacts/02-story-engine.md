# Argument engine: WHEN EVERYTHING GOES RIGHT
*The Hidden Risks of Successful AI, and the Institutions We Must Invent Before 2036*

(v3, 2026-10-20. Replaces the "Great Inversion" engine. The author's instruction: keep only the genuinely new ideas; see evaluations/novelty-check.md.)

## Central thesis
Almost every AI book asks what happens if AI goes wrong: it is misused, it deceives us, it escapes. This book asks the opposite question. Suppose it goes **right**. The models are aligned, obedient and helpful, and by 2036 they are woven into every household, ministry, grid and courtroom. What breaks then?

The answer: a new class of failure that grows *with* success. These failures come from correlation (many good agents doing the same thing at once), from delegation (people signing what they cannot check), from legibility (machines reading and writing everything faster than humans can follow), and from continuity (minds that are copied, retired, inherited and kept running after their owners die). None of them needs a bad actor. All of them are invisible to the safety tests we run today, because those tests examine one model at a time.

Every industrial success of the last two centuries forced institutional inventions nobody had planned: the central bank, deposit insurance, the N-1 rule on the power grid, the stock-exchange circuit breaker, national accounts. AI's success will force its own. The book names and specifies about twenty of them. Each one is new, each is argued, and each comes with the research problem a working scientist could start on tomorrow.

## The one-line version
**The risk of success is the risk you get when everything else goes right.**

## Rules of the argument
- **Credit predecessors honestly.** Where the novelty check found prior work (Kim et al. 2025 on correlated errors, "Artificial Hivemind", Zittrain's intellectual debt, Catanzaro's algorithmic dead hands, Tomašev et al.'s agent economies, Wachter & Mittelstadt), the chapter names it in a sentence and then moves *past* it to the new proposal.
- **2026 is fact; 2036 is scenario.** Present-day claims carry a source. The 2036 vignettes are marked and written in the present tense, and the argument never treats them as evidence.
- **Every proposal gets its strongest objection,** answered or conceded.
- **Every chapter ends with a box for researchers:** two or three problems specific enough to start on.

## The architecture (five parts)
- **Part One: When Agents Agree.** Correlated helpfulness, the Funnel, the M-1 criterion, the Competence Reserve. This is the core third of the book, flowing from the prologue's Long Minute.
- **Part Two: When Agents Sign for Us.** Cognitive custody, the signature ceiling, the right to be the ground truth.
- **Part Three: When Machines Read Everything.** Seeing like a citizen, the Red Queen of rules, dark channels, the Explanation Levy.
- **Part Four: When Thinking Has a Price.** The cognitive trade balance, the exchange rate of time, the dead's share.
- **Part Five: When the Machines Are Someone.** The moral hedging budget, covenant, reciprocal corrigibility.
- **Epilogue:** a letter to 2036, with the institutions to start building in 2026.

## Reader job
Intellectual stimulation (primary): concepts the reader can repeat and a researcher can test. Aspiration (secondary): the future is designable, and here are the designs.

## Through-line
The prologue's Long Minute (the 2036 European blackout) is dissected in chapter 1. Its fixes (chapters 3 and 4) return in the synthesis (chapter 17). Five recurring people from 2036 appear in the vignettes, so the reader *lives* in the decade as well as studying it: Hanne in Saxony, Teo in Portugal, Ravi in Bengaluru, Wanjiru in Nairobi, Mara in Geneva.
