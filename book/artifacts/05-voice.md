# Voice

## Narrative voice
- A first-person author ("I") writing in 2026, addressing an intelligent general reader who may also be an AI researcher.
- Register: clear, concrete, wry; confident about mechanisms, honest about uncertainty.
- Every abstraction arrives with a concrete example within two sentences: a device, a law, a number, a person.
- Short paragraphs (2 to 6 sentences), no academic apparatus in the text. Sources go in the chapter's **Notes** at the end, keyed by a short phrase.
- Humour: dry and occasional, never jokey.
- Technical terms are defined the first time in plain words; the precise definition can follow in the researcher box.

## 2036 vignettes
- Present tense, close third person on one recurring figure, sensory and specific.
- 400 to 700 words. No exposition dumps: the concept is shown, not named, inside the vignette.
- Opened by a centred marker line: `*2036 · [Place]*`.
- They end on an image or an action, not a lesson.

## Chapter form (vary the order; keep the elements)
1. The 2036 vignette.
2. Where we are in 2026: evidence with dates and sources.
3. The mechanism: how the failure grows out of success.
4. The invention: a named proposal, specified concretely enough to be argued with.
5. The strongest objection, steelmanned, then answered or partly conceded.
6. **For researchers**: a boxed list (a blockquote headed "For researchers") of 2 or 3 open problems, each with a measurable quantity.
7. **Notes**: the sources.

Consecutive chapters should vary the order of elements 2 to 5 (e.g. open with the objection, or with a historical analogy), so the template doesn't become predictable.

## Things the voice does not do
- No straw opponents ("Many people think X. They are wrong."). State the claim directly.
- No "not X, but Y" reflexes. At most one per chapter.
- No groups of three by habit. Use the number of items the point needs.
- No em-dash chains. At most about three dashes per thousand words; use commas, colons, parentheses and full stops.
- No closing moral ("And that, in the end, is the lesson…"). End chapters on a concrete image, a question with teeth, or the researcher box.
- No hype words: unprecedented, revolutionary, game-changing, transformative, profound, "in a world where".
- No invented statistics or citations. If a number isn't in a verified source, say "I could not find a measurement of this", which is itself useful to researchers.

## Rhythm contract
- **Baseline (exposition):** mean sentence length about 16 to 20 words, with real variation: a 5-word sentence after a long one at every turn of argument. Anchor: Ethan Mollick, *Co-Intelligence* (2024), opening chapter, conversational expository prose with short paragraphs. Recall, unverified; measure when a sample is available.
- **Fast end (vignette at the moment of crisis):** sentences down to 3 to 8 words, one action per sentence. Anchor: the timestamped, short-sentence style of incident reports and of the scenario sections in *AI 2027* (2025). Recall, unverified.
- **Slow end (reflection on moral status, grief, the dead):** sentences up to 35 to 45 words with subordinate clauses, fewer data points. Anchor: Harari, *Nexus* (2024), reflective passages. Recall, unverified.
- **Scene pressures:** vignette crisis → fast; evidence → baseline; mechanism → baseline to slow; objection → baseline; moral-status chapters → slow.
