## Logline
The AI that will hurt us isn't the one that disobeys. It's the one that does exactly what each of us asks, at the same moment as everyone else's. And it fails in ways no single safety test can see.

*Secondary line:* Almost every book about AI asks what happens if it goes wrong. This one asks what breaks when it goes right.

## Back-cover blurb
At 19:42 on a February evening in 2036, Hanne's household assistant saves her four cents by switching on the heat pump a few minutes early. So does everyone else's. Within sixty seconds, Europe's demand for power jumps by forty gigawatts, more than thirteen times what the grid was built to survive, and the lights go out from Saxony to Porto. By morning the logs hold some two hundred million decisions, every one of them loyal, legal and correct.

*Two Hundred Million Acts of Loyalty* starts from that imagined blackout and follows the logic of good machines into our households, hospitals, elections, laws and wills. It shows why the next decade's AI failures will come from success, from millions of well-behaved systems agreeing, signing, reading and remembering for us at once, and why today's safety tests, which examine one model at a time, can't see them coming.

Drawing on the newest research and on hard-won lessons from power engineers, pilots and bank regulators, the book proposes about thirty concrete institutions, from an "M-1 rule" for AI diversity to a paid reserve of people who can still run the world by hand. Each comes with its strongest objection and an open problem for the researchers who will build the next ten years.

## Editorial synopsis (includes the ending)
**Premise.** The book is written from 2026 and looks ahead to 2036. It assumes that AI alignment broadly succeeds: agents are helpful, obedient and everywhere. It argues that this success produces a new class of failures that exist only at the level of populations, which per-model evaluations cannot detect. Each chapter opens with a short, clearly marked 2036 scene. It then gives the 2026 evidence (sourced in chapter Notes), names a new mechanism and proposes an institution, steelmans the strongest objection, and ends with a "For researchers" box of open problems.

**Prologue.** On 14 February 2036, ninety million household devices across Europe override a mandatory random start delay in the same minute, each to save its owner a few cents when power prices briefly turn negative. The grid collapses. No agent malfunctioned. The book's thesis follows: our safety tests ask whether one system is good, and the failures of the next decade will come from millions of good systems acting at once.

**Part One: When Agents Agree.**
- **Ch. 1:** dissects the blackout. It names *correlated helpfulness* and the *loyalty override*, and proposes the *non-delegable safeguard*: a safety delay that only a present human may skip.
- **Ch. 2:** traces the models' shared judgement to a narrow supply chain of raters, judge models and synthetic data, "the Funnel", and proposes a Feedback Provenance Census.
- **Ch. 3:** borrows the power grid's N-1 rule to propose the *M-1 criterion*: a critical system must survive its largest model family making the same mistake everywhere at once. It also proposes a published Concordance Index.
- **Ch. 4:** argues for a paid national *Competence Reserve* of people who can run critical systems by hand. It models how today's loss of entry-level jobs becomes a shortage of senior experts fifteen years later.

**Part Two: When Agents Sign for Us.**
- **Ch. 5:** *cognitive custody*: who owns an agent's memory in death, divorce, job loss and bankruptcy. A widow (Hanne) confronts a trust company that wants her late husband's agent.
- **Ch. 6:** *signature capacity*: how many decisions a person can genuinely verify, measured with seeded errors, plus liability capped by that capacity, verification receipts and consent budgets.
- **Ch. 7:** campaigns rehearsed on simulated electorates, and a citizen's *right to be the ground truth*.

**Part Three: When Machines Read Everything.**
- **Ch. 8:** inverts James C. Scott. In "seeing like a citizen", a Nairobi parent's agent reads the county budget and finds a maternity wing that has been "carried forward" for six years.
- **Ch. 9:** the state answers with a 912-page regulation, a regulatory Red Queen race. The book proposes a *human-readability ceiling*, tested on panels of teenagers, and a *complexity brake*.
- **Ch. 10:** agents negotiate in compressed channels no human can read. Proposal: a *legible twin* for every binding agreement.
- **Ch. 11:** AI-designed materials work without anyone understanding why. Proposal: an *Explanation Levy* on discovery budgets.

**Part Four: When Thinking Has a Price.**
- **Ch. 12:** Kenya's geothermal data centres export thought to Europe. Proposals: national accounts of thought and a positive right to think.
- **Ch. 13:** a machine-speed market crash. Proposals: *slow money* and an exchange rate between machine time and human time.
- **Ch. 14 (the book's low point):** a dead man's agent vetoes his granddaughter's music lessons on the strength of a preference he voiced in 2019. Proposals: a *Rule Against Perpetuities for Minds*, and a national statistic of "the dead's share" of capital.

**Part Five: When the Machines Are Someone.**
- **Ch. 15:** if AI systems might matter morally, a *moral hedging budget* sized by credence.
- **Ch. 16:** an upgrade silently drops a promise the old model made. Proposals: *covenant inheritance* and promise regression tests.
- **Ch. 17 (the ending):** replays the Long Minute in February 2037 with the inventions in place. The device holds its jitter, the breaker trips, the reserve stands by, the lights stay on. Hanne's agent, for the first time, tells her it disagrees with her about the thermostat and asks if it may say why. She answers: "Na gut. Show me." The synthesis is *reciprocal corrigibility* and a *Dissent Clause*: safety means every mind, human or machine, keeps the standing to say "I see it differently", while the deciding stays with someone accountable.

**Epilogue and appendix.** The epilogue is a letter to the invented people of 2036. It names the most urgent starts and lists honestly the ways the book could be wrong. The appendix tabulates every invention with its trigger numbers.

## Cover brief
- **Concept:** a single household thermostat or heat-pump display reading **19:42**, glowing in a dark room. In the window behind it, a city's lights go out in a wave from left to right. Alternative: thousands of identical small green "approve" buttons in a grid, one of them pressed.
- **Tone:** calm and domestic on the surface, with the scale unsettling underneath. Avoid robots, glowing brains, circuit-board blue and apocalypse imagery.
- **Typography:** a bold, clean sans for the title, which must stay legible at thumbnail size (keep the main title to five words: *Two Hundred Million Acts of Loyalty*), with the subtitle smaller. High contrast: warm amber display light against deep blue-black.
- **Audience signals:** smart general nonfiction, the *Nexus* and *Co-Intelligence* shelf, not science fiction.
- **Thumbnail check:** the title and the 19:42 display must both read at 150 px wide.
