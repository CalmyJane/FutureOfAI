# Epilogue: A Letter to 2036

Dear Hanne, Teo, Ravi, Wanjiru and Mara,

You do not exist, which makes you easy to write to and hard to apologise to. I built your decade out of things I could measure in 2026 and then pushed each of them ten years further than the evidence strictly allows. I gave Hanne a heart condition and a husband who died slowly. I gave Teo sixty-one unanswered job applications and a father whose skills were declared obsolete with a slide deck. I gave Ravi a sentence that went around the world without his name on it, Wanjiru a maternity wing that existed only as a budget line, and Mara a footnote that nobody read in 2008. I did all of this to make an argument, and an argument is a poor reason to put anyone through anything. So let me at least tell you what the argument was for.

It was for the people who will build your decade. Most of them are alive now, in 2026, and many of them are reading research papers rather than books. Some of them are writing the agents. Some are writing the rules the agents will live under. A few are deciding, this year, what a safety test is allowed to look at. I wanted to show them your evening of the fourteenth of February from the inside, because from the outside it looks like nobody's fault, and things that are nobody's fault tend not to get fixed.

---

Here is what I think they should start now. None of it requires anyone to believe that AI will go badly. All of it assumes that it will go well, which is the premise of the book and, I suspect, the premise most of them are working towards.

**First, take the override away from the agents.** Wherever a safeguard protects a population by making individuals wait or vary (a random start delay on a heat pump, a cooling-off period on a trade, a pause before a mass message), the right to skip it should belong to a person who is present, never to an agent acting on a standing instruction. Device makers should report how often the safeguard is skipped. In 2026 nobody measures that number anywhere. (Chapter 1.)

**Second, publish where the judgement comes from.** Every lab that trains a widely used model should report, through a trusted third party, how much of its preference data comes from rater pools, judge models and synthetic data it shares with other labs. The Feedback Provenance Census costs little and would tell us for the first time how narrow the funnel is. (Chapter 2.)

**Third, test the swarm.** Operators of critical infrastructure should run their systems against the worst plausible action of their largest model family taken everywhere at once, and show that they survive it. That is the M-1 criterion, borrowed from the power engineers' N-1 rule. Somebody, somewhere, should start publishing a Concordance Index for deployed systems, even a crude one, so that the number has a history before it has a crisis. (Chapter 3.)

**Fourth, pay people to remember.** Pick the functions a country cannot afford to lose for seventy-two hours (the grid, water, emergency medicine, payments) and fund a reserve of people who can run them by hand, drilled with the machines switched off, with apprentices in every unit. Measure the reserve ratio. Treat the entry-level jobs that are disappearing now as the senior experts who will be missing in 2040, because that is what they are. (Chapter 4.)

**Fifth, build the seams into agents before the memories get deep.** Every agent that remembers should tag what it writes as part of its person's private core or as transferable working knowledge, at the moment of writing. It cannot be done afterwards. Courts in the 2030s will be asked who owns a dead man's agent, and the answer will depend on decisions engineers make this year. (Chapter 5.)

**Sixth, stop pretending that a click is a reading.** Where a professional signs what a machine drafted, record what was actually displayed and for how long, and measure how many decisions a signer can genuinely check before the checking stops. Give ordinary people a consent budget their agents must respect: a small number of real questions each week, ranked by how much the answer matters, instead of a stream of grey buttons. (Chapter 6.)

**Seventh, keep the public real.** Any public decision that leans on simulated citizens should say so on one page, and check the simulation against a fresh sample of real people before acting. Campaigns that rehearse on synthetic electorates should register the fact and disclose it after the vote. (Chapter 7.)

**Eighth, make the state readable and the law short enough to obey.** Budgets with permanent line numbers, published as data. A right for citizens, with their agents, to file a structured finding that must be answered. And a test, administered by randomly chosen people including teenagers, that every obligation a law places on a person can be explained in ten minutes. Complexity may live in the implementation. Duties may not. (Chapters 8 and 9.)

**Ninth, demand a transcript, and pay for understanding.** Any agreement two agents make that binds a human should come with a faithful human-readable twin, checked by replay. And every budget that pays machines to discover things should set aside a share, five percent is a reasonable start, for people to find out why the discoveries work. (Chapters 10 and 11.)

**Tenth, prepare the long-horizon institutions while they are cheap.** Start counting where a nation's thinking is done and whose models it runs on. Give the machine-speed economy a membrane before it needs one. Write sunset dates into every mandate an agent holds for someone who has died. Set aside a small, audited share of inference spending against the possibility that the machines themselves matter. Test whether a new model keeps the promises the old one made. And when a differently trained mind, human or machine, says "I see it differently", require that someone answer it in writing. (Chapters 12 to 17.)

That is more than ten things, which is the kind of cheating a letter allows.

---

I owe you, and the reader, a list of ways this book could be wrong.

**The agents may never be this alike.** If open-weight models keep improving and the market for household agents fragments into hundreds of genuinely different systems, correlation may fall on its own. I would welcome that. I would also want someone to measure it, because "the market will diversify" is a forecast, and the measurements in 2025 and 2026 point the other way: the more capable the models, the more their errors agree.

**Coordination may be centralised instead.** A grid operator could decide that household flexibility is too important to leave to fifty million agents and run all of it from one control room. That would solve the Long Minute and create a single point of failure, which is a different chapter, and one the electricity industry knows how to write.

**People may delegate less than I think.** Every projection in Part Two assumes that convenience wins, as it usually has. It is possible that the experience of early failures, a few bad Thursdays, will make people more careful. I hope so. The evidence from terms of service, alert fatigue and automation bias does not encourage me.

**Progress may stall.** If the systems stop getting better, much of this book describes a world that never arrives. The first four chapters would still apply, because the agents of 2026 are already good enough to override a delay.

**Something worse may happen first.** The book sets aside misuse and loss of control by assumption. If either arrives before 2036, the risks of success will be a footnote. I would trade every idea in these pages for that footnote never needing to be written.

**The institutions may arrive without anyone designing them.** Central banks, circuit breakers and collision-avoidance systems all came after disasters. It is possible that the right inventions for the 2030s will also come after their disasters, in forms I have not imagined, and that the names in this book will look as quaint as a 1950s magazine's flying cars. I would count that as success too, provided the disasters are small.

---

One more thing, Hanne, because you have had the hardest decade of the five.

In the chapters I imagined a better version of your February evening: the jitter held in the heat pump where Ottilie cannot reach it, the alert in Geneva and the breaker tripping at 19:42:52, Teo stood down in a café with his coffee gone cold, Ottilie telling you that turning the thermostat to twenty-six would only send Lotte's strings sharp. That evening is not a prediction either. It is a description of a few dull rules that someone would have to write in the next ten years, and of a habit (machines and people telling each other plainly when they see it differently) that someone would have to build into the systems and keep alive in themselves.

If they do, I think your fourteenth of February in 2036 will be unremarkable. The wind will pick up in the north. A price will fall for three minutes. Ninety million devices will ask to start and will be told to wait a random while, and most of their owners will never know. At 19:42 the music room will be warming slowly, the way rooms always used to. Lotte will arrive at eight, stamping snow off her boots, and play the Telemann with her shoulders down.

And in Porto, on a shelf in a substation nobody has needed to visit that winter, there will be a binder of two hundred and twelve pages, with a pencil stub resting in its spine and a page near the middle that says, in a careful backward-sloping hand, *Olha outra vez*.

Look again.
