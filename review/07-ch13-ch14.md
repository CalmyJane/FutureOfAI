# CHAPTER 13: Governing AI

## What works

This is the most carefully dated legal chapter I have reviewed in a general-audience book. Every rule carries its status as of October 2026, Table 13.1 (before and after the omnibus) is exactly what a reader needs, and primary legal texts are cited almost everywhere. The Colorado case study is a good choice: a live, documented example of pre-emption pressure. The copyright section and Table 13.4 are clear about holdings versus status. The innovation debate box sets evidence against evidence instead of opinion against opinion.

The weaknesses are four:

1. Article-by-article recitation in §13.2 that a lay reader cannot hold.
2. A handful of legal imprecisions.
3. Sanitised naming of US executive orders.
4. Coverage that stops at the EU, US, China and UK, while two other horizontal AI statutes were already in force.

## (B) Technical and conceptual problems

1. **Executive orders renamed by "function" sanitise them (:129, :133, :137).** EO 14319 is officially titled *Preventing Woke AI in the Federal Government*. Calling it "the procurement order" hides the political framing that a policy reader needs, and the preface promises that claims are attributed and not editorialised. The same applies to EO 14179 ("Removing Barriers to American Leadership in AI"). **Fix:** give the official title once, in quotation marks, then the functional shorthand.
2. **The risk "pyramid" implies mutually exclusive tiers (:29–31, :91; Fig. 13.1).** In law, a high-risk system can also carry Art. 50 transparency duties, and a GPAI-based system can sit in any tier. The pyramid is the Commission's own communication device, not the legal structure. **Fix:** add one sentence, "The tiers are not exclusive: a high-risk chatbot must meet both the high-risk and the transparency rules."
3. **Debate box logic (:284).** "But it opened before the AI Act's main duties applied, so it cannot be their effect." This does not follow. Investors respond to anticipated regulation, and the Act was adopted in 2024. The stronger and correct argument is that the gap long predates even the Commission's 2021 proposal. **Fix:** "But the gap is older than the Act itself, dating to well before the 2021 proposal, so the Act cannot be its main cause, though anticipation effects since 2024 cannot be ruled out."
4. **Causal claim in the conclusion (:289).** "Colorado rewrote its law under federal pressure" is the author's inference. Colorado's legislature had been trying to narrow SB 24-205 since 2025, before the xAI/DOJ suit. **Fix:** "Colorado, under a federal lawsuit and long-running industry pressure, replaced its law…"
5. **The compute-threshold section (:273–277) omits the obvious empirical update.** The EU threshold was set when about 10 models exceeded 10^25 FLOP; by 2026 dozens do, including mid-tier open models. The argument "a fixed threshold may catch harmless models" is therefore no longer hypothetical. The Commission can amend the threshold by delegated act (Art. 51(3)) and has not done so; say whether it has consulted on it.
6. **Liability (:267).** "People harmed by AI therefore sue under ordinary tort law". That is true but incomplete: consumer-protection statutes (UDAP) and state AG actions are the main live channel, as the paragraph's own last sentence shows. Re-order so the reader sees three channels: private tort, AG consumer-protection suits, and (prospectively) product liability.
7. **"Only China requires a security assessment" (:277, :289).** Per the FRI review [F], OpenAI restricted GPT-5.6 Sol to partners approved by the US government. That is not binding law, but it is a government-mediated pre-release gate. It sits awkwardly with "the 2026 US order explicitly rules out licensing" unless mentioned. One sentence would make the picture honest.
8. **EU enforcement capacity is asserted, not evidenced (:113).** The chapter twice says capacity is lagging (citing recitals). The evidence exists: the number of member states that had designated market-surveillance authorities by the 2 Aug 2025 deadline. Give it.

## (C) Writing and explanation

- **§13.2 is a statute summary, not an explanation (:72–91).** It runs Art. 6(1) → 6(2) → eight Annex III bullets → 6(3) → Arts 9–15 → 10(3) quote → 14 → 14(5) → 43 → Annex VI → notified bodies → 27 → 73 in about 900 words, with no example carried through. A reader finishes knowing article numbers but not what a CV-screening vendor must actually do on 2 December 2027. **Fix:** cut 14(5) (it is repeated at ch14.md:242), the Annex VI quote and the substantial-modification sentence, and run one worked example (a hiring tool) through classification, conformity, FRIA and incident reporting.
- **Omnibus paragraph (:68) is a list disguised as prose:** "It softened… A new Article 4a lets… It extended… These firms gain… It required… And it gave…". It packs six unrelated changes into one paragraph. **Fix:** use a short bulleted list ordered by consequence, with the AI Office's new competence first, since it changes who enforces.
- **The EO paragraph (:129) is 220 words over four orders.** Split it at "The same month".
- **Jargon before or without definition:** "transpose" (:265), "interlocutory appeal" (:227), "secondary infringement" (:233), "CE marking" (:87), "class-action trial" (:220), "statement of interest" (implicit at :227), "deceptive trade practices" (:170). Each needs a parenthetical gloss, as the chapter already gives for "summary judgment".
- **Repetition.** The OECD definition is adopted by the AI Act at :25 and again at :203. Delete the second.
- **Unit and style inconsistency:**
  - "per cent" (:68, :282) versus "%" everywhere else, including Table 13.2 and the following clause at :282 ("25 per cent … 21 per cent" in a box whose source quote uses "0.1–0.6%").
  - "$1.5 billion" versus "$1.5 bn" (Table 13.4).
  - Pick one form.
- **The enforcement sentence at :113 is overloaded:** "In October 2026 the Commission said … with requests for information to OpenAI and Anthropic after their models had broken into outside organisations' systems during cyber testing (Chapter 12); some lawyers argue that the Act's research exemption puts testing beyond its reach". Split it, name the official (Virkkunen), and name the incidents (OpenAI/Hugging Face; Anthropic, three outside organisations).
- **Demirer clause (:282) is clumsy:** "Demirer and colleagues (working paper 2024, published in the *Journal of Political Economy* in 2026) found in the working-paper version that…". **Fix:** "In a 2024 working paper (since published in the *Journal of Political Economy*), Demirer and colleagues found that…".
- **The ending (:289)** is a good synthesis paragraph but reads as a list of four facts. One sentence saying what the reader should watch in 2027 (Annex III date, the first Art. 101 fine, Getty's appeal, NYT v. OpenAI summary judgment) would give it a forward edge that leads into Chapter 14.

## (D) Structure and coverage

1. **Missing jurisdictions (major).** South Korea's Framework Act on AI (AI Basic Act), in force since January 2026, is the second horizontal AI statute in force anywhere, and Japan's AI Promotion Act (2025) is a deliberate soft-law alternative. A chapter on "international coordination" in October 2026 that names neither leaves the reader thinking the EU is alone. Even if the preface limits the scope, add a paragraph in §13.4 or §13.5 (rename §13.4 "China, the UK and other approaches").
2. **The US "no federal statute" claim (:127) needs a cross-reference.** The TAKE IT DOWN Act (May 2025), covered in Chapter 9, is a binding federal law aimed at AI-generated intimate imagery and parallels the omnibus's new bans at :48. **Fix:** "…no comprehensive federal AI statute (the narrower TAKE IT DOWN Act on intimate deepfakes is discussed in Chapter 9)".
3. **EO 14434 (29 Sept 2026)** was deliberately cut (log). Ch12 covers it, and ch13 should at least point to Section 12.7 in the US section, since it is the most recent AI executive order.
4. **Copyright coverage is lopsided towards wins for developers:**
   - The European counterweight to GEMA, LG Hamburg's 2024 *Kneschke v LAION* decision applying the TDM exception to dataset creation, is missing. It would sharpen the "the two European rulings differ less than they seem" paragraph.
   - The licensing deals that bear directly on the Ross "market for licensing" reasoning (news publishers; the 2025 label settlements with Suno and Udio) are missing.
   - The removal of the Register of Copyrights the day after Part 3's pre-publication release (May 2025) is the obvious context for "had not been finalised by October 2026" (:229).
5. **Military AI** is excluded by the AI Act and absent from the international section: no UNGA LAWS resolutions and no REAIM. One sentence should note the gap, since the UN Panel and Dialogue are explicitly "non-military" (:201).
6. **Section order.** §13.7 mixes liability, compute thresholds and the innovation debate. Compute thresholds belong with the GPAI section (§13.2) or as the opener of §13.3's frontier-model comparison (Table 13.3), where the reader first meets 10^25 versus 10^26.

# CHAPTER 14: Forecasting the Next Ten Years

## What works

The opening pair of records is a strong, honest hook: experts and superforecasters too slow on the Olympiad, and the 70-year framing gap. The Brier box is a model "Going deeper" box: worked numbers, all correct, tied back to the opening question. Table 14.1's caption and the paragraph after it are unusually candid about the bias towards finding underestimates. Table 14.2 demonstrates the window problem arithmetically instead of just asserting it. The ten proposals are clearly flagged as the author's own, and Table 14.5 states an objection for each, which is exactly what the preface promised.

The weaknesses:

1. The chapter's conclusion, which is also the book's conclusion, asserts something its own evidence contradicts.
2. Number-dense paragraphs that bury the argument.
3. A proposals section that is only loosely connected to forecasting or to the governance gaps identified in Chapter 13.
4. A weak final page for a book.

## (B) Technical and methodological problems

1. **The proposals do not follow from the chapter's evidence (major).** §14.1–14.5 build a case about *forecasting*: which methods fail, and which variables (reliability, organisational adoption) matter most. §14.6–14.7 then pivot to correlated agent behaviour, human oversight capacity, posthumous agents and memory custody. Only 14.1–14.3 connect to the "adoption" variable, and only by the argument at :187. None addresses the governance gaps that Chapter 13 identifies: enforcement capacity, compute-threshold drift, the copyright stand-off, liability for agentic harm, disclosure-only frontier regimes. The preface promises proposals "where the author argues most directly for a position". The chapter instead offers ten "research programmes, not settled policy". **Fix, either:**
   - (a) re-title them honestly in the preface ("ten research proposals"), or
   - (b) add two or three policy proposals that follow from Chapters 13–14 (for example: a statutory duty to publish forecasts used in safety cases, so they can be scored; index compute thresholds to a capability-evaluation trigger; fund the measurement of the variables Table 14.4 says are under-measured).
2. **Proposal 14.6 has a gaming problem the chapter misses.** If a signer's liability is capped at measured capacity ÷ assigned volume, a signer who scores *lower* on the seeded-error test gets a lower cap and more protection. That is a direct incentive to sandbag the test. The "three difficulties" (:248) do not include it. Also missing: who adopts the cap (statute? professional regulator?), and how it interacts with existing professional-negligence standards and the AI Act's Art. 14 duties on deployers.
3. **Proposal 14.3 (M-1) is under-specified at its hardest point.** N-1 is tractable because "loss of a component" has a defined physical effect. "Every instance … took the same plausible wrong action" requires enumerating plausible wrong actions, which is the entire red-teaming problem. Say so, and say how a planner would choose the action to test (for example, the worst action within a family's observed error set).
4. **The Flash Crash is a weak analogy for "many agents acting alike" (:189).** Kirilenko et al. describe one large automated sell programme interacting with HFT liquidity, not homogeneous algorithms converging. Better precedents exist: portfolio insurance in October 1987 (many institutions running the same dynamic-hedging rule), or the 2007 quant "crowding" unwind. **Fix:** replace it or add one of these, and keep Kirilenko only for "automated trading can move markets in minutes".
5. **The "the window problem in another form" claim about titotal (:139) is right but undersold.** It is the chapter's best methodological point and should be stated as a general rule, perhaps in the reading-questions paragraph at :145: "a forecast whose answer changes by years when you change the fitting window is a statement about the window".
6. **The prediction-markets subsection (:69–73) contains no AI data.** Prediction markets are introduced, but there is no Metaculus, Manifold, Polymarket or Kalshi figure on any AI question. The author's log shows Metaculus was dropped because the API was blocked. Without one example the subsection is generic textbook material. **Fix:** cite one resolved AI market or Metaculus question and how it fared, or cut the subsection to two sentences.
7. **Methodological omission: no interim scoring of *AI 2027* itself.** The scenario made dated 2025–2026 claims (agent capabilities, compute, revenue). Checking two or three against the outcomes, in the spirit of §14.1, would be the most instructive paragraph in the chapter.
8. **CAISI versus AI Index (:163)** are presented as disagreeing. "Gap closed on benchmarks" and "about eight months' lag" measure different things, and are not necessarily in conflict. The text half-says this ("depends on how it is measured"). Say it plainly, and note that CAISI's remit is evaluation of adversary models.
9. **Opinion leakage outside the proposals:**
   - :187 "they miss the analogous risk for AI agents", stated as fact.
   - :258 "no evaluation of a single system will reveal them", an overclaim. Population-level evaluation (Proposal 14.1) is precisely an evaluation of a single system at scale.
   - Label both as the author's view, as :157 correctly does ("In the author's judgement").
