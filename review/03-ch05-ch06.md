# Review: Chapters 5 and 6

*Understanding AI: Technology, Trends and the Decade Ahead* (Göbel, October 2026). Reviewed 25 October 2026.

Scope: `chapters/ch05.md` (314 lines) and `chapters/ch06.md` (285 lines), both read in full, together with the preface's evidence-labelling rules, `bib/ch05.bib`, `bib/ch06.bib`, the figure specs, the SVG text layers, and the evidence logs (used only as leads).

Method:
- Every DOI was resolved against Crossref (55 DOIs) and every arXiv identifier against the arXiv API (36 IDs).
- Numbers and quotes were checked against primary full texts wherever reachable: arXiv HTML/PDF, Europe PMC/PMC, nature.com, publisher pages, official specifications, NVD, METR's raw YAML data and NHC's PDF.
- Line numbers refer to the chapter files as they stand.

Labels used below:
- **Verified wrong**: the source checked says something different.
- **Unsupported / overstated**: the source exists but does not carry the claim as written.
- **Outdated**: correct when written, but superseded by October 2026.
- **Couldn't verify**: the source was not reachable, or the claim rests on secondary reporting only.

# Chapter 5: From Chatbots to Agents

## What works

This is a strong chapter.
- The model–tools–loop–memory frame is introduced early and then really used.
- The reliability section (5.5) is the best explanation of compounding error and pass@k versus pass^k I have seen in a general book. Every worked number recomputes correctly (see below).
- The security section is balanced, current and well sourced (NCSC, Nasr et al., CaMeL, Beurer-Kellner).
- Project Vend gives a through-line that pays off.
- Benchmark caveats (saturation, contamination, harness dependence) are handled with unusual care.

The problems are mostly:
- currency (METR, OSWorld dates);
- a handful of mis-described studies (Cemri, the SWE-bench Pro cost-cap comparison, AgentDojo);
- density: some paragraphs read as spec sheets.

## (A) Factual / reference errors

**A5.1 — METR time horizon is outdated and given without its uncertainty (major, outdated).**
- ch05.md:162: "has doubled about every 129 days since 2023 and reached about 12 hours for Claude Opus 4.6 in February 2026".
- METR's data file `benchmark_results_1_1.yaml` (https://metr.org/assets/benchmark_results_1_1.yaml) gives Opus 4.6 a p50 of 718.8 min (≈12.0 h) ✔. However:
  - its 95% CI is **317–3,634 min (≈5.3–61 h)**;
  - a later model, `claude_mythos_preview_early` (release 7 Apr 2026, added 8 May 2026), is at **1,044.8 min ≈ 17.4 h** (CI ≈ 8.5–55 h);
  - the 128.7-day doubling (CI 104–158 days) is fitted "from 2023 on" and **excludes points whose central estimate exceeds 16 h**;
  - the page has said since 8 September 2026 that the series is "no longer actively updated".
- Why it matters: an October 2026 book quoting a February figure as the latest, with no interval, overstates precision and understates the trend.
- Fix: "…doubled roughly every four months (129 days, 95% CI 104–158) from 2023 to early 2026; the last model METR measured before it stopped updating the series, an early Claude Mythos Preview (April 2026), reached about 17 hours, with a confidence interval spanning roughly 8 to 55 hours."

**A5.2 — Table 5.1 misdates Claude Opus 4.5 (minor–major, verified wrong).**
- ch05.md:134 lists "Claude Opus 4.5 | early 2026 | OSWorld (version not stated) | 66.3%".
- Opus 4.5 was released on 24 November 2025 (METR's YAML gives release date 2025-11-24). "Early 2026" is the date of the AI Index report, not of the result.
- Secondary reports of the AI Index also note that vendor-reported OSWorld-Verified scores had passed the 72.4% human baseline by March 2026 (e.g. GPT-5.4 at a reported 75.0%). That makes ch05.md:140, "By the summer of 2026 the leaderboard's best results exceeded the student baseline", late by several months. This last point rests on secondary reports only; couldn't verify against the AI Index PDF.
- Fix: date the row "Nov 2025 (reported in AI Index 2026)", and say that self-reported scores crossed the baseline in early 2026, with the official re-run leaderboard following.

**A5.3 — Cemri et al. mis-described (major, verified wrong).**
- ch05.md:287: "Cemri and colleagues annotated more than 1,600 execution traces… Annotators agreed closely on the categories (Cohen's kappa… 0.88 on 150 traces)".
- In the paper (arXiv 2503.13657, NeurIPS 2025), humans analysed **150 traces** to build the taxonomy. The κ = 0.88 was reached by three annotators "iteratively label[ling] a total of **15 traces**". The 1,600+ traces of MAST-Data were then labelled by an **LLM-as-a-judge pipeline**.
- Why it matters: the claim "the failures are recognisable patterns" leans on human agreement that was measured on 15 traces, not 150. Calling 1,600 traces "annotated" by the researchers hides that the frequencies (e.g. step repetition 15.7%, which is correct and the most frequent mode) are LLM-judged.
- Fix: "Cemri and colleagues built a taxonomy of 14 failure modes from 150 human-analysed traces (three annotators reached κ = 0.88 on a calibration set), then used a language-model judge to label more than 1,600 traces from seven frameworks."

**A5.4 — SWE-bench Pro cost-cap comparison is constructed by the author, not made in the paper (major, unsupported).**
- ch05.md:150: "Capping spending at $2 per task as well changed the picture again: GPT-5 at its high reasoning setting fell from 41.8 to 25.9 per cent on the public set".
- In arXiv 2509.16941v2, 41.8% comes from Table 1, the v2 run with no stated cost cap. 25.9% comes from Table 5 (appendix), the original v1 run with a 50-turn, $2 cap. The paper never presents these as a controlled comparison, and other settings may differ between v1 and v2.
- The paper's own Table 2 caption also mislabels the commercial set "(N=731)".
- Fix: drop the "fell from… to" framing. Instead: "In an earlier run capped at $2 per task, GPT-5 (high) resolved 25.9 per cent of the public set." Then Kapoor's point stands without an implied causal estimate.

## (B) Technical / conceptual problems

**B5.1 — "Usually higher" should be "never lower" (ch05.md:188).**
- For true per-task success probabilities, E[p^k] ≥ (E[p])^k by Jensen's inequality (x^k is convex on [0,1]). The observed benchmark pass^k can therefore never sit below the "equal-difficulty" prediction except through sampling noise.
- Fix: "…is never lower than the average single-attempt rate raised to the power k (a consequence of averaging), and with mixed difficulty it is usually much higher."

**B5.2 — The chatbot/agent dichotomy is dated (ch05.md:11, 21).**
- "In a chat, a human stands between every model output and every action." This is no longer true of mainstream chat products in 2026: ChatGPT and Claude run web search, code and connectors inside a chat turn.
- The chapter's own Concept box says agency is a matter of degree.
- Fix: present a spectrum (single reply → tool-using chat turn → multi-step autonomous run) rather than a binary.

**B5.3 — "No close equivalent in conventional software" (ch05.md:220) contradicts 5.6's own framing.**
- The section then (rightly) uses the NCSC's confused-deputy framing, a 1988 conventional-security concept.
- Fix: "…a problem that resembles older confused-deputy flaws but, unlike SQL injection, has no clean separation of code and data to fall back on."

**B5.4 — Card payments (ch05.md:271).**
- "Card payments assume that a human clicks 'buy' on a trusted screen" ignores card-on-file, recurring and merchant-initiated transactions, which already let software charge cards without a click.
- The real novelty is *agent-initiated* transactions whose intent must be proven.
- Fix: "Card rules assume either a cardholder present at checkout or a merchant acting on a standing agreement; neither fits a third-party agent deciding what to buy."

**B5.5 — Table 5.1 mixes step budgets and harnesses.**
- Only the Claude 3.5 row says that more steps raised the score. CUA's 38.1% and Opus 4.5's 66.3% come with undisclosed or different step limits.
- Fix: add a "steps / harness" column, or a caption sentence that step limits vary from 15 to several hundred.

**B5.6 — Plan-then-execute (ch05.md:35) is presented as "the main alternative" to ReAct, citing a security paper.**
- In agent engineering more broadly, the commoner alternatives are orchestrator–worker and tool-chain workflows (5.1's "workflows").
- Fix: say "one alternative, favoured for security".

## (D) Structure and coverage

**D5.1 — Missing: what agents are actually used for in 2026.**
- The chapter has rich benchmarks but almost nothing on deployment: the mass adoption of coding agents (Claude Code, Codex, Cursor agents) during 2025–26, browser agents shipped to consumers, enterprise usage data, or costs per task.
- A reader expects at least one evidence-based paragraph on usage, ideally from usage reports rather than press releases. That paragraph would also give 5.4 the "real work" its title promises.

**D5.2 — Missing: governance of agents.**
- There is nothing on liability, the EU AI Act's treatment of general-purpose systems used agentically, or the standards work (NIST, the FIDO agent-authentication work beyond payments). Section 5.8 gestures at identity.

**D5.3 — Balance.**
- Most primary sources on design and capability are vendor posts (Anthropic ×7, OpenAI ×6, Google ×2). The chapter labels them well.
- But the "what works" side of agents rests almost entirely on vendor material, while the critical side is academic. Say so explicitly once (5.1 already does it for Project Vend), or add an independent positive result (e.g. a peer-reviewed field deployment).

# Chapter 6: AI for Science, Mathematics and Engineering

## What works

The chapter's organising idea is excellent and is carried through, from Fig. 6.1 to the closing paragraph: the four jobs (prediction, emulation, search, automation) and "the check that follows them".
- The AlphaFold section is technically precise and checks out to the decimal against Jumper et al.: 48 blocks, 0.96 vs 2.8 Å, 128 TPU v3 cores, 30 April 2018 cut-off, fewer than 30 alignment sequences.
- The weather section is the strongest independent-evaluation narrative in the book. Every GraphCast number recomputes against the paper (90%/1,380, 96.9%/1,280, 88.0%, 36.7M, 32 TPUs, 4 weeks).
- The GNoME/A-Lab treatment and the Si et al. ideation–execution study are exactly the kind of "showcase vs independent test" pairing the book promises.

The weaknesses are:
- currency in mathematics, where the "modest picture" from 2025 is no longer true;
- one-sidedness on MatterGen;
- an underpowered treatment of the biggest claim in the chapter (Navier–Stokes);
- a title that promises "engineering" and barely delivers it.

## (A) Factual / reference errors

**A6.1 — MatterGen presented without the published critique that its "new" material was already known and in the training data (major, one-sided / outdated).**
- ch06.md:145 reports the TaCr₂O₆ proof of concept and adds approvingly that "The paper also takes disorder into account…".
- The numbers are all correct against the Nature paper (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08628-5): 200 GPa target, 222 GPa DFT, 169 GPa max of four (158 ± 11), within 20%, 117,652 + 850,384 structures, "more than twice as likely".
- But Juelsholt, "Continued challenges in high-throughput materials predictions: MatterGen predicts compounds from the training dataset" (*Materials Horizons*, 2026; https://edoc.hu-berlin.de/18452/38430), argues that:
  - the synthesised compound corresponds to a phase reported around 1971–72;
  - that phase was present in MatterGen's training set;
  - the disordered sample is better described as a known rutile solid solution.
- Why it matters: this is the same disorder error the chapter documents for GNoME and A-Lab. Omitting it for MatterGen reverses the chapter's own lesson, and the subsection title is literally "the disorder lesson".
- Fix: add two sentences after "within 20% of the target": "A 2026 reanalysis argued that the compound made is a disordered phase first reported in the early 1970s and present in MatterGen's own training data, so the proof of concept showed property targeting, not discovery." Cite the critique.

**A6.2 — The "independent test" of public models is 18 months out of date (major, outdated).**
- ch06.md:178 (Table 6.2) and 182: "An independent test of publicly available models gave a more modest picture… only Gemini 2.5 Pro reached a non-trivial score of 25%".
- That is correct for USAMO 2025, tested in March 2025 (arXiv 2503.21934 abstract ✔). By 2026 it no longer holds.
  - Secondary reports of MathArena's USAMO 2026 evaluation put public models at roughly 95% (GPT-5.4) and 74% (Gemini 3.1 Pro). These are secondary sources; check matharena.ai.
  - The AFP report the chapter itself cites (ch06.md:184, `afp2026ai`) notes informal tests in which four public models scored 42/42 at IMO 2026.
- As written, the section implies that public models remain far behind experimental ones, which is no longer the state of the evidence.
- Fix: keep the 2025 study as history ("in March 2025…") and add the 2026 human-graded independent result, with a caveat on contamination and grading.

**A6.3 — The Navier–Stokes claim is stated as fact and sourced to news (major, mislabelling / news-for-primary).**
- ch06.md:210: "OpenAI announced that… [it] had produced a proof that the three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations… can develop a singularity in finite time". It cites *Quanta* (`kakaes2026ai`, whose headline is "AI Has Solved One of Math's $1 Million Millennium Prize Problems").
- Checked:
  - *Quanta* (8 Sep 2026): 10,000 agents, 88 h, ≈17 h for the Lean step ✔. It frames significance as "if the result holds up to further scrutiny".
  - *Scientific American* (13 Oct 2026): 166 pages; "no one fully does" understand it ✔. Experts now say the final proof "is a different approach" from the Córdoba–Martínez-Zoroa programme. Buckmaster alleges improper pressure from OpenAI.
  - *TNW*: "We do not intend to claim the Millennium Prize".
- Problems with the text:
  - The second clause reads as established mathematics.
  - The primary (OpenAI's announcement and the posted proof) is not cited.
  - "OpenAI nonetheless said it would not claim the prize" puts in a "nonetheless" the sources don't support. TNW suggests the forcing term is the likely reason, i.e. the opposite logic.
- Fix:
  - "OpenAI *claimed* a proof… The proof has not been peer-reviewed or accepted by the Clay Institute."
  - Cite OpenAI's own post or arXiv/preprint as primary, and *Quanta*/*SciAm* as reporting.

## (B) Technical / conceptual problems

**B6.1 — The Hao et al. "AI tools" are much broader than the reader will assume (ch06.md:260).**
- The study classifies "AI-augmented" papers across eras of AI, including classical machine learning, not just the generative tools discussed elsewhere in the chapter.
- Readers will map "scientists who used AI tools" onto LLM use, which is not what was measured.
- Fix: "…papers that use AI methods of any era, from classical machine learning to deep learning…"

**B6.2 — GNoME's graph edges are not bonds (ch06.md:127).**
- "atoms (nodes) linked by bonds (edges)". In GNoME, as in most crystal GNNs, edges connect atoms within a distance cutoff, whether or not they are chemically bonded.
- Fix: "linked to their near neighbours (edges)".

**B6.3 — "A protein structure is checked against blind experimental data" (ch06.md:252).**
- Only CASP targets are. The 214 million database predictions are mostly never checked, which is exactly Terwilliger's point.
- Fix: "A protein-structure *method* is checked against blind experimental data; individual predictions usually are not."

**B6.4 — Table 6.1's "Reported weakness" column mixes weaknesses and dependencies.**
- "Uses physics-based initial conditions" (AIFS row) is not a weakness ECMWF reports; it is a dependence the chapter discusses later.
- "Higher cost than deterministic ML models" (GenCast) is the author's inference.
- Fix: rename the column "Limitation (reported or noted)" and mark the author's own entries.

**B6.5 — The AIFS description is of a pre-operational version (ch06.md:87).**
- The 2024 preprint (`lang2024aifs`) describes AIFS v0.x. The operational AIFS Single v1 (Feb 2025) and v2 (May 2026) differ in resolution and training.
- Fix: "In a 2024 technical preprint describing an earlier version…"

**B6.6 — The physics Nobel and Chapter 2 (ch06.md:3).**
- "The physics prize honoured the ideas behind the networks described in Chapter 2" is loose. The citation is for Hopfield networks and the Boltzmann machine, ancestors of, but not the same as, the backpropagation-trained feed-forward networks of Chapter 2.
- Fix: "…honoured early ideas (Hopfield networks and Boltzmann machines) from which the networks of Chapter 2 descend."

**B6.7 — Fig. 6.1's mapping is idiosyncratic.**
- "Collect data → Predict: fill gaps" places AlphaFold-type prediction, the chapter's leading example, at data collection.
- "Form hypothesis → Search" places GNoME and FunSearch there, although the text treats them as producing results, not hypotheses.

The figure shows "Check sources / Expert judgement / Compare with reality / Test on held-out data / Read the code / Independent replication", with no formal proof and no synthesis.

**B6.8 — The cap-set gloss (ch06.md:192).**
