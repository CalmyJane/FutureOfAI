## (A) Factual / reference errors

**A3.1 (major, verified wrong / superseded). Size of the RL stage of DeepSeek-R1.**
- **Location:** l. 322, Figure 3.3 caption (l. 242), fig-03-03 spec ("growing share of compute").
- **The text says:** "Epoch AI … estimated in May 2025 that the reinforcement-learning stage of DeepSeek-R1 used about 6 × 10^23 operations, about 20 per cent of the cost of pre-training its base model."
- **What the primary source says:** The chapter itself cites the September 2025 *Nature* paper, and its Supplementary Information (§2.4.4; arXiv v2, Suppl. B.4.4) reports DeepSeek's own figures:
  - R1-Zero: 64×8 H800 GPUs for about 198 h, i.e. about 101K GPU-hours;
  - R1: about 80 h on the same 512 GPUs, i.e. about 41K GPU-hours;
  - SFT data: 5K GPU-hours;
  - total: about 147K GPU-hours, or $294K.
- **The arithmetic:** DeepSeek-V3 used about 2.79M GPU-hours, so the RL stage is about **5 %** of base-model training, not 20 %.
- **Why it matters:** The chapter uses the 20 % figure to argue "this stage is no longer a small add-on". It is citing a May 2025 secondary estimate that a primary source the chapter already cites has since overtaken.
- **Sources:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.12948 (Suppl. B.4.4); https://www.theregister.com/2025/09/19/deepseek_cost_train/
- **Fix:** "DeepSeek's *Nature* paper reports that the reinforcement-learning stages of R1-Zero and R1 took about 147,000 GPU-hours, roughly 5 per cent of the GPU time used to pre-train its base model. OpenAI has said that o3 used ten times o1's training compute, most likely in this stage [@you2025how]." Then soften the Figure 3.3 caption ("can use a substantial share" → "is growing").

**A3.2 (major, verified: material omission that changes the claim). Kalai et al.'s bound depends on calibration.**
- **Location:** l. 296–298.
- **The text says:** "a base model's generative error rate is at least roughly twice its misclassification rate … Wherever classification is bound to fail, generation will fail too." Then: "a base model should be expected to hallucinate on at least about 20 per cent."
- **What the paper says:** The bound has a calibration term (δ). The authors argue δ is small for *pre-trained* models because cross-entropy training makes them calibrated: "language models which do not err must not be calibrated" (Corollary 1; §3, PDF ll. 391–443). The 20 % singleton result likewise holds for calibrated models.
- **Why it matters:** Stated without the condition, the result reads as a law about all generators, which it is not. A model that abstains, or one that is miscalibrated (as post-trained models often are), escapes it.
- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.04664
- **Fix:** Add one clause: "for a base model that is calibrated (its confidence matches its accuracy, which pre-training tends to produce)…"
- **Also confirmed:** The "penalty 2" slip is real; the paper prints "t = 0.75 (penalty 2)". The parenthetical in the text is correct but belongs in a note, not the main text.

**A3.3 (minor). Number of heads in GPT-2 small.**

## (B) Technical / conceptual problems

**B3.1 (major). "Adds skills, not only style" is presented as settled; it is contested.**
- **Location:** l. 322.
- **The issue:** "The resulting gains in mathematics and coding suggest that post-training of this kind adds skills, not only style."
- **The counter-evidence:** Yue et al., "Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Model?" (NeurIPS 2025 oral, arXiv 2504.13837) found that:
  - RLVR models win at pass@1;
  - base models match or beat them at large k;
  - the authors read RLVR as mostly sharpening the sampling of reasoning paths the base model already has.
- **Why it matters:** A one-sided reading of an open question in the chapter's newest section.
- **Fix:** Present both readings in two sentences and cite Yue et al.

**B3.2 (major). "Never drafts and revises."**
- **Location:** l. 179.
- **The issue:** "Each token is committed once chosen; the model never drafts and revises." This conflicts with §3.7, where R1-Zero "began to check and revise its own work". It also ignores speculative decoding (draft-then-verify) and the diffusion LMs in production by 2026 (e.g. Mercury, Gemini Diffusion).
- **Fix:** "Each token, once emitted, stays: a standard autoregressive model cannot delete what it has written, only add text that corrects it, which is exactly what reasoning models learn to do (Section 3.7)."

**B3.3 (major). Attention heads described imprecisely.**
- **Location:** l. 122.
- **The issue:** "each with its own query, key and value matrices operating on a slice of the vector". Each head *projects the full* residual vector into a smaller subspace; it does not read a slice of it. Only the concatenated *outputs* correspond to slices.
- **Why it matters:** Readers will form the wrong picture, i.e. that heads see different parts of the token.
- **Fix:** "each with its own, smaller query, key and value matrices, which project the whole vector into a 64-number subspace".

**B3.4 (minor). "Nothing in the computation runs step by step."**
- **Location:** l. 104.
- **The issue:** Overstated. Layers run in sequence, and generation is sequential. The point is that no *position* waits for the previous position during training.
- **Fix:** Rephrase to that effect.

**B3.5 (minor). Layer normalisation is described but RMSNorm is not mentioned.**
- **Location:** l. 134.
- **The issue:** The text implies all LLMs use layer normalisation. Llama, Qwen and DeepSeek use RMSNorm, which only rescales and does not subtract the mean.
- **Fix:** One clause.

**B3.6 (minor). Tokenisation is not forced by arithmetic.**
- **Location:** l. 9.
- **The issue:** "A neural network can only do arithmetic, so a language model first splits text into tokens" is a non sequitur. Characters or bytes are numbers too; tokenisation is chosen for efficiency.
- **Fix:** "…so text must become numbers. Rather than feed in single characters, which would make sequences very long, models split text into tokens."

**B3.7 (minor). The √d_k rationale is a conjecture in the source.**
- **Location:** l. 102.
- **The issue:** The √d_k explanation is presented as established. Vaswani et al. write "*We suspect* that for large values of d_k…".
- **Fix:** Keep the explanation and add "the authors suspected"; the variance argument (dot-product variance grows with d_k) is easy to add in one clause.

**B3.8 (minor). The 3.1 nucleus comparison confounds model and prompt.**
- **Location:** l. 199.
- **The issue:** "3,068 tokens after GPT-2 XL's open-ended lecture prompt but only 103 after Qwen2.5-1.5B's factual one" changes model and prompt at once.
- **Fix:** Compare the same model on two prompts. GPT-2 XL on the lecture prompt (3,068) against GPT-2 XL on "The capital of France is" (I computed 807) makes the point cleanly.

**B3.9 (minor). DPO presented one-sidedly.**
- **Location:** l. 272–276.
- **The issue:** DPO is presented only through its authors' results. Later controlled comparisons (e.g. Xu et al., "Is DPO Superior to PPO for LLM Alignment?", ICML 2024) found well-tuned PPO can outperform DPO. Most frontier labs still use online RL.
- **Fix:** Add one sentence on this.

**B3.10 (minor). Uncited generalisation about post-training.**
- **Location:** l. 282.
- **The issue:** "It is generally thought to change mainly which of the base model's capabilities are expressed…" is uncited.
- **Fix:** Cite the "superficial alignment hypothesis" (Zhou et al., LIMA, NeurIPS 2023), which is the source of this view.

**B3.11 (minor). GRPO description incomplete.**
- **Location:** l. 318.
- **The issue:** "avoids training a separate value network" uses a term the chapter never defines. Readers are also not told that GRPO keeps PPO's clipping and a KL penalty to a reference model.
- **Fix:** Define value network ("a second network that predicts how good a partial answer is, used as a baseline") or drop the clause.

**B3.12 (minor). Jain & Wallace without the rebuttal.**
- **Location:** l. 124.
- **The issue:** Jain & Wallace is given the last word. Wiegreffe & Pinter, "Attention is not not Explanation" (EMNLP 2019), is the standard reply.
- **Fix:** Add one clause on the rebuttal.

**B3.13 (minor). InstructGPT result framed without its baseline.**
- **Location:** l. 268.
- **The issue:** "The effect was large." The 1.3B-vs-175B comparison is against *prompted base* GPT-3, a weak baseline.
- **Fix:** Say so, so the reader does not infer that alignment beats a 100× scale-up in general.

## (A) Factual / reference errors

**A4.1 (major, verified wrong). The METR task suite is misdescribed.**
- **Location:** l. 224.
- **The text says:** "170 software and research tasks, from actions taking a human 1–30 seconds to research-engineering problems rated at 8 hours".
- **What the source says:** Kwa et al. §3: "A subset of HCAST: 97 diverse software tasks ranging from 1 minute to **30 hours**". RE-Bench tasks are the ones fixed at 8 h, and they are not the longest.
- **Why it matters:** As written, the chapter contradicts itself. Two paragraphs later it says METR treats estimates "above 16 hours" as unreliable, which makes no sense if the longest task were 8 h.
- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.14499 (l. 175 of text extraction).
- **Fix:** "…from atomic actions taking a human 1–30 seconds to software tasks of up to about 30 hours".

**A4.2 (major, verified: material omission). The ARC-AGI-1 o3 result lacks its key caveat.**
- **Location:** l. 177; Table 4.4; debate box l. 243.
- **The omission:** The ARC Prize post states: "OpenAI shared they trained the o3 we tested on 75% of the Public Training set."
- **Why it matters:** The result is used as evidence about test-time scaling ("the price of the last few points"). Training on the benchmark's own training split is directly relevant to interpreting it, and especially so in a chapter that devotes a subsection to contamination.
- **Source:** https://arcprize.org/blog/oai-o3-pub-breakthrough
- **Fix:** Add "a version that, OpenAI said, had been trained on three-quarters of the benchmark's public training tasks".

**A4.3 (major, interpretive / misleading evidence). The pre-training slowdown argument rests on the wrong comparison.**
- **Location:** l. 236.
- **The text says:** "The strongest evidence of diminishing returns concerns pre-training. GPT-4.5 … scored 36.7% on AIME 2024 and 38.0% on SWE-bench Verified… o3-mini at its high setting scored 87.3% and 61.0%."
- **Why it fails:** Comparing a non-reasoning model with a reasoning model shows that RL post-training helps on maths and coding. It does not show that pre-training returns diminished. The same OpenAI table shows that GPT-4.5 *greatly* improved on its pre-trained predecessor GPT-4o: AIME 9.3 → 36.7 %, SWE-bench Verified 30.7 → 38.0 %, GPQA 53.6 → 71.4 %. That is evidence *against* the stated reading.
- **Fix:** Rebuild the paragraph around the evidence that does bear on returns to pre-training:
  - Epoch's observation that GPT-5 used less compute than GPT-4.5;
  - few models exceeding 3× GPT-4 compute in 2.5 years;
  - Llama 4 Behemoth;
  - the power-law arithmetic itself.

  Give the GPT-4o → GPT-4.5 gains for balance.

**A4.4 (minor, verified). Inconsistent ClockBench human baselines.**
