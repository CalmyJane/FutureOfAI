# CHAPTER 9: Information, Media and Democracy

## (A) Factual / reference errors

**A9.1. ch09.md:5. "the institutional response came from a telecoms regulator applying the Truth in Caller ID Act … not from any rule about synthetic media". Major.**
This is misleading. In February 2024, between the robocall and the Kramer fine, the FCC adopted a Declaratory Ruling that AI-generated voices count as "artificial" voices under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act. That is a rule about synthetic voices, and it was adopted *because of* this incident. The criminal case was also brought under New Hampshire voter-suppression and candidate-impersonation law, not caller-ID law. Barrington et al., whom the chapter cites, also record that Lingo Telecom was penalised (PMC11958761: "Kramer was fined $6 million … while the telecommunications company, Lingo Telecom, rec[eived]…").
*Fix:* "The first federal response came from a telecoms regulator. The FCC declared that AI-cloned voices fall under existing robocall rules and fined Kramer for spoofing caller ID. No law yet addressed synthetic media as such."

**A9.2. ch09.md:23. Goldstein et al. (2023) "located four points where intervention is possible". Minor (labelling only).**
The four points are correct. The paragraph then argues that "the evidence on the third and fourth links tempers the concern about the first two". The newest evidence the paragraph cites points the other way. Meta's H2 2026 report says AI use "has moved from an occasional tactic … to a near-standard component of influence operation tradecraft. Every one of the CIB networks we disrupted … incorporated some form of generative AI", and it describes "industrialized AI workflows" (meta26 pp. 34–35). Meta does keep its core assessment that "behavioral detection approaches remain effective". The chapter reports both halves in one clause, which flattens a real change in trend.
*Fix:* Say explicitly that the 2024 and 2026 Meta reports show adoption moving from marginal to universal, while *detection* still works.
*Also:* `meta2026adversarial` is cited from a copy hosted by combatantisemitism.org. The document is genuine (title page "AUGUST 2026 / SECOND HALF Adversarial Threat Report"), but the book should cite Meta's own transparency-centre URL. It is also a company report, and the preface's rule ("according to the company") is not applied to it, nor to `clegg2024what`.

**A9.3. ch09.md:23. OpenAI October 2026 operations, cited to Anadolu Agency and NPR. Minor.**
These are news reports of an OpenAI threat report. Per the preface, cite OpenAI's report and label it "according to the company". OPB/NPR confirms the gist ("those focused on placing false narratives in established media outlets appeared to have reached a wider audience").

**A9.4. ch09.md:23. International AI Safety Report "written by an independent expert panel". Minor.**
The report (arXiv 2602.21012) describes over 100 independent experts led by the Chair. The "Expert Advisory Panel" is made up of representatives *nominated by 29 nations, the UN, the OECD and the EU*. Calling the authors "an independent expert panel" conflates the two.
*Fix:* "written by more than 100 independent experts chaired by Yoshua Bengio".

**A9.5. ch09.md:19 and bib `wang2023neural`. VALL-E "described in a 2023 preprint". Minor (mislabelling).**
The bib entry is the peer-reviewed IEEE/ACM TASLP version (33:705–718, 2025, doi 10.1109/TASLPRO.2025.3530270, Crossref-verified), and its first author is Sanyuan **Chen**, not Wang. The key, the "2023 preprint" label and the bib therefore describe three different objects.
*Fix:* Either cite arXiv 2301.02111 (Wang et al. 2023, preprint), or keep the journal entry and write "described in 2023 and published in 2025". Rename the key to `chen2025neural`. The quote ("only a 3-second enrolled recording") is verified in the arXiv abstract.

**A9.6. ch09.md:63. "unless the attacker has another strong model at hand, in which case the watermarked one was unnecessary". Major (verified wrong as an argument).**
Zhang et al., cited two sentences later, argue the opposite. The quality and perturbation oracles "can be satisfied in practice by an attacker with weaker computational capabilities than the watermarked model itself" (arXiv 2311.04378 abstract). Paraphrasing is also a much easier task than generating, so a small paraphraser can launder a frontier model's output. Kirchenbauer et al. themselves used T5-Large, which is smaller than the OPT-1.3B generator.
*Fix:* Delete the clause, or replace it with: "A weaker paraphrasing model is enough for an attacker, because rewriting is easier than writing (Zhang et al.)."
For balance, the chapter also omits the authors' own follow-up ("On the Reliability of Watermarks for LLMs", ICLR 2024). It found that watermarks remain detectable after human and machine paraphrasing once passages are long enough (hundreds of tokens). That nuance belongs in the robustness paragraph.

**A9.7. ch09.md:87. Apple iPhone 18 Pro: "the announcement did not mention C2PA". Minor (true but misleading by omission).**
The Apple Newsroom release does not mention C2PA. It does say "Image metadata and upcoming support for the SynthID standard can also help users identify images generated or edited with AI". Section 9.3 is about how watermarking and provenance relate, so Apple's planned SynthID support is the more relevant fact. It also matches Kohli 2026 ("OpenAI, NVIDIA, Kakao, and soon, Apple").
*Fix:* Add "and announced support for Google's SynthID watermark".

**A9.8. ch09.md:146. "The effect was smaller, though still present, among those for whom the conspiracy was central to their worldview [@cornell2024ai]". Major (emphasis inverted; press release cited where the paper exists).**
Costello et al.'s abstract (Science 385:eadq1814) says the effect "occurred even among participants with deeply entrenched beliefs". The chapter replaces the paper's headline robustness claim with a hedge sourced to a university press release.
*Fix:* Cite the paper: "the effect held even among participants whose beliefs were deeply entrenched, though it was somewhat smaller for them". Give the moderation coefficient from the paper if the chapter wants the attenuation.

**A9.9. ch09.md:173. "Persuasiveness and accuracy pulled in opposite directions in both large studies." Major (overstated).**
This holds for Hackenburg et al. ("where they increased AI persuasiveness, they also systematically decreased factual accuracy"). It does not hold for Lin et al. Lin found that facts and evidence *drive* persuasion: removing them cut the effect by 58% in Canada and 78% in Poland. The inaccuracy Lin documents is a partisan asymmetry ("AI models advocating for candidates on the political right made more inaccurate claims"), not a trade-off between persuasiveness and accuracy.
*Fix:* "In the Hackenburg study the most persuasive configurations were the least accurate; in Lin's, persuasion ran on facts, but the facts offered for right-leaning candidates were less accurate."

**A9.10. ch09.md:152 versus ch09.md:148. Salvi (personalisation helps) against Hackenburg and Lin (personalisation adds almost nothing). Major (unreconciled contradiction).**
Salvi et al. found GPT-4 *with* personal data more persuasive than humans in 64.4% of non-tied pairs (verified). Hackenburg (+0.43 pp, "never exceeds +1pp", verified) and Lin (Poland: no difference for the non-personalised condition, BF₀₁ = 25.3) found personalisation nearly irrelevant. The chapter reports all three without comment, and the reader cannot tell which to believe.
*Fix:* Add a sentence. Salvi compares AI with *humans* in a debate format with 900 participants, so its comparison is "AI with data against humans", not "AI with data against AI without data". The two large studies isolate personalisation directly and find it small.

**A9.11. ch09.md:177. Argyle et al.: "In the preprint version of the study, the model's predicted Republican vote share…" while citing the published *Political Analysis* article. Minor.**

## (B) Technical / conceptual problems

**B9.1. ch09.md:13. Diffusion is presented as *the* generation method.** "Many current image generators are diffusion models" is true but dated by October 2026. Leading image systems now include autoregressive (GPT-4o-style) and flow-matching models (rectified flow, as in SD3 and FLUX), and video generators are diffusion *transformers*. One sentence acknowledging this would stop the section from reading as frozen in 2022.

**B9.2. ch09.md:63. Watermark robustness is summarised in AUC.** Deployed detectors work at a fixed threshold (z = 4). An AUC of 0.954 at ε = 0.3 hides a much larger drop in true-positive rate at that threshold (Kirchenbauer's Table 8 gives it). The text's "replacing 30% or more degraded it markedly" is true of TPR@z=4, not obviously of an AUC of 0.954. Readers looking at Figure 9.1 will see a curve that barely moves to 0.3 and wonder what "markedly" refers to.
*Fix:* Quote the TPR at z = 4 for ε = 0.3, or say "the authors describe a high level of degradation at ε = 0.3, at a cost of about 15 perplexity points".

**B9.3. ch09.md:112. The Kapoor & Narayanan cost argument is reported uncritically.** It has two methodological limits a media researcher would flag:
- The WIRED database is a convenience sample of *detected* uses, so undetected deepfakes are by construction excluded.
- "Could be made without AI for a few hundred dollars" prices one item, not a campaign of thousands of personalised items. Scale is precisely what Goldstein et al. (2023) worried about.

The Debate box should carry at least the first point.

**B9.4. ch09.md:136 and Figure 9.3. Kalla & Broockman used as a baseline.** Their zero is for *field* contact on *vote choice* in *general* elections. Survey-experiment effects on 0–100 attitude scales are not on the same scale. The figure draws the K&B "≈0" line on the same axis as Hackenburg's attitude points and Lin's preference gaps, which invites the very comparison the caption says is invalid.
Kalla & Broockman also report *non-zero* effects in primaries and ballot measures. That fits Lin's large Massachusetts ballot effect, and the chapter could make the point.

**B9.5. ch09.md:193. "The two sets of findings are compatible. Removing the algorithm wholesale changed little …, while targeted changes … had measurable short-term effects."** Nyhan et al. was itself a *targeted* change (cutting like-minded sources by a third) and found nothing. The real difference from Piccardi is *what* was targeted (affective, antidemocratic content against ideological source) and *what* was measured (immediate feeling thermometer against three-month attitudes).
*Fix:* "Changing exposure to like-minded sources did not move attitudes; changing exposure to hostile, antidemocratic content moved feelings towards the other party, at least over ten days."

**B9.6. ch09.md:171. External-validity paragraph.**
- "few voters choose to spend that long discussing politics with a chatbot" is unsourced.
- The chapter misses the stronger point: experiments measure effects on *people who were assigned* to converse. Real-world effects depend on who *self-selects* into political chatbot use, which is likely the already engaged.
- The Lin debriefing argument ("so that any lasting effects would cancel out") also deserves a line on why it matters: it means that the persistence estimate is measured *after* debriefing, so it is probably a lower bound.

**B9.7. ch09.md:23. Missing mechanism: AI in the *distribution* layer.** The chapter's thesis is that "production was never the binding constraint". It then mentions only in passing that generative models are now embedded in search and feeds. The most important 2025–26 development for that thesis is chatbots *as* the distribution channel: data-poisoning ("LLM grooming") campaigns such as the Pravda network seeding chatbots, and chatbots giving wrong election information (AI Forensics and Proof News audits in 2024). These undercut the "supply doesn't matter" argument and should be addressed in 9.1 or 9.7.

**B9.8. ch09.md:240. Grok box: "the gap that the AI Act's new prohibition … is intended to close: the platform rules applied only after the images had spread".** The AI Act prohibition is also enforced after the fact. The real difference is that it binds the *provider of the generating system* (xAI) rather than the platform. Say that.

**B9.9. ch09.md:223. The DSA debate is asymmetric.** The critics' only cited source is labelled "a partisan document", while the defenders are represented by the statutory text. That is not "the strongest evidence on each side" (preface). There is serious non-partisan criticism, for example from free-expression scholars and NGOs (Article 19, EFF, academic work on "systemic risk" vagueness), and the debate should cite at least one such source. Separately, the Kaushal et al. findings are only paraphrased: no abstract was available through Crossref, and I could not check the "131 million" figure or the quotes.

# CHAPTER 10: Education, Health and Everyday Life

## (A) Factual / reference errors

**A10.1. ch10.md:150. Folk & Dunn (2026) misreported. Major (verified wrong).**
The text says: "emotional isolation predicted later use of social chatbots, and use predicted further isolation four months on".
The abstract (Psych Sci 37(4):276–286) says: "increased social chatbot use predicted increased loneliness, using a single-item measure of emotional isolation. When we used a broader and more stable measure of social connection, we found evidence that feeling less socially connected predicted subsequent increases in social chatbot use; however, chatbot use did not significantly predict decreases in social connection."
So the chapter assigns the wrong measure to the "isolation → use" direction and omits the null result on the more reliable measure, which is the result that matters most for the debate. The abstract also says "more than 2,000 adults from four Western countries". "English-speaking" is not in the abstract; check the paper.
*Fix:* "Chatbot use predicted later increases in a single-item measure of emotional isolation; on a broader, more reliable measure of social connection, lower connection predicted later chatbot use, but use did not predict lower connection. The authors call the analyses exploratory."

**A10.2. ch10.md:129. Vaccaro et al. reported one-sidedly. Major (verified omission).**
The text reports only g = −0.23 against the *better* of human or AI. The same paper reports: "when we compared the performance of the human–AI systems to … the humans alone — we found substantial evidence of human augmentation … g = 0.64 (95% CI 0.53 to 0.74)" (PMC11659167). For a chapter about clinicians *with* AI, augmentation relative to the clinician alone is at least as relevant as synergy.
*Fix:* Add "On average, though, human–AI pairs did clearly better than the humans alone (g = 0.64); the loss is relative to the AI working alone."

**A10.3. ch10.md:133. Penda Health trial (Agweyu et al. 2026): adjusted OR reported without the raw rates. Major (selective reporting).**
The abstract gives treatment failure of "102/4,693 patients (2.2%) in the intervention arm and 94/4,654 (2.0%) in the control arm (adjusted odds ratio 0.77, 95% CI 0.55 to 1.08)". The raw rates point the *other* way from the adjusted OR. The evidence log admits that the raw rates were omitted "because raw and adjusted directions differ". That is exactly the case in which a reader needs both.
*Fix:* "Treatment failure was 2.2% with the assistant and 2.0% without; after adjustment the odds ratio was 0.77 (95% CI 0.55–1.08). Neither difference is significant."

**A10.4. ch10.md:18. Kraft box: "By that yardstick, all the AI tutoring results below are large". Major (verified wrong against the chapter's own table).**
Table 10.1 includes Bastani's GPT Tutor arm (no significant change), GPT Base (−17%, a *harm*), and Liu et al. (−0.37 SD, *negative*, not significant in the full sample). Wang reports percentage points, not SD. Kraft's benchmarks are also for *standardised* achievement tests, while most of these trials use researcher-made tests.
*Fix:* "By that yardstick, the positive results below are large, often implausibly so for tests written by the researchers; two trials found harm."

**A10.5. ch10.md:153. Therabot "four weeks of access". Minor (verified imprecise).**
Per the NEJM AI abstract (via c4tbh.org), participants were "prompted daily for four weeks" and "retained access … for an additional four weeks without prompts". The chapter also relies on the Dartmouth press release's within-group percentages (51%, 31%) when the abstract states statistically significant *between-group* reductions at 4 and 8 weeks.
*Fix:* Lead with the between-group result from the paper. Report the press-release percentages, if at all, as secondary.

**A10.6. ch10.md:157. New York: "New York's law relies on repeated disclosure and efforts to detect self-harm [@nyassembly2026a10379]". Minor (wrong citation).**
The cited source is the *unpassed* minors bill (A10379-C), not the enacted law (S-3008C, Article 47 of the General Business Law). The enacted law is supported only by `gluck2025understanding` (FPF blog), which is cited in the table caption but not in this sentence.
*Fix:* Cite the statute, or FPF, for the enacted law, and A10379 only for the pending bill.

**A10.7. ch10.md:87. "a search of the September 2026 list found no ambient documentation tools [@fda2026artificial]". Minor (attribution).**
This is the author's own search, cited to the FDA. Say "the author's search of the list". More importantly, the absence is *expected*. Most ambient scribes are marketed as documentation tools that do not meet the device definition, or fall under the 21st Century Cures Act's non-device carve-outs. The sentence implies an evidence gap where there is a regulatory boundary. Explain the boundary.

**A10.8. ch10.md:29 and 38. Henkel et al. (Rori) included among "randomised studies". Minor.**
The arXiv abstract says "Each school was assigned to a treatment group or control group". It does not say randomly, and with 11 schools the balance is fragile. The table caption hedges ("random or matched"), but ch10.md:23 introduces the table as "the main randomised studies". Say how schools were assigned, or call it a "controlled study". Also check whether Rori in the evaluated year (2022–23) was LLM-based or largely scripted. If the latter, it does not belong in a table of "LLM-based tutoring". I could not verify this.

**A10.9. ch10.md:89. EU AI Act and medical devices: "AI that is part of a medical device covered by EU product-safety law is high-risk". Minor (imprecise law).**
Under Art. 6(1), it is high-risk only where the device must undergo *third-party* conformity assessment under the MDR or IVDR (in practice class IIa and above). Class I software is not caught.

**A10.10. ch10.md:89. PCCPs cited to the 2023/2025 "guiding principles".** The FDA issued *final guidance* on PCCPs for AI-enabled device software functions in December 2024. That is the operative document and should be cited.

**A10.11. ch10.md:11. "pre-school to year 12". Minor (en-GB mistranslation).**
US "PreK–12" ends at 12th grade (age 17–18), which is UK *Year 13*. Use "from pre-kindergarten to the end of secondary school".

**A10.12. ch10.md:5. "Students with an unrestricted chatbot solved more practice problems". Minor.**
Bastani measures the *share of points obtained* on practice problems (+48%), not the number solved. Use "scored higher on practice problems".

**A10.13. ch10.md:62. "In the Pew survey, 54% of US teens thought it acceptable…". Minor (ambiguous).**
Two Pew teen surveys have just been cited, and *both* contain a 54% figure (2025: used chatbots for schoolwork; 2024: research acceptable). The 54%/18% acceptability figures are from the **2024** survey (`sidoti2025quarter`).
*Fix:* "In Pew's 2024 survey…".

**A10.14. ch10.md:150. De Freitas et al.: "In five studies … found that a session with an AI companion reduced self-reported loneliness". Minor.**
Study 1 is correlational (app reviews), and the head-to-head comparison with human interaction is Study 2. "The case for relief rests on experiments" is mostly but not wholly true. Say "In a series of studies, mostly experiments…".

## (B) Technical / conceptual problems

**B10.1. ch10.md:23. Nigeria trial: differential attrition is understated.** 50% of controls (334/671) and 36% of treated students (235/657) missed the final test, a 14-point differential. That exceeds What Works Clearinghouse thresholds for "high attrition" at any overall rate. "The authors correct for this statistically" does not convey that the corrections (Lee bounds, IPW) rest on strong assumptions.
"1.5 to 2 years of ordinary schooling" from six weeks is an extrapolation that depends on an assumed annual learning gain for Nigerian secondary pupils. The chapter repeats it without saying so. These two points, plus the working-paper status, should be stated *together*, and in the effect-size box ("Attrition matters because…") the Nigerian case is the obvious worked example. The box never makes that link.

**B10.2. ch10.md:52. Liu et al. (Maryland): the effect is implausible as a direct effect.** An ITT effect of −0.37 SD with ~15% take-up implies a treatment-on-the-treated effect of about −2.5 SD, which is implausible. The chapter half-notices this ("may partly reflect how instructors' promotion of it changed other AI use"). It should say plainly that the grade effect is probably not a tutor effect at all, and that it appears only within same-course blocks, a specification chosen among several. Present this result with *more* caution than the Turkish trial, not as "pointing the same way".

**B10.3. ch10.md:36. Kestin crossover.** The chapter notes the ceiling and the authors' stake. It omits two further points: outcomes were *instructor-written* post-tests (the experimenter-made measure that Cheung & Slavin, discussed in the same chapter, flag as inflating effects); and each condition covered one lesson on one topic. Connect the dots: the box warns about exactly this, and the Harvard result is the chapter's largest positive effect.

**B10.4. ch10.md:9–11. Two human-tutoring effects with no explanation.** VanLehn's 0.79 and Nickow's 0.288 are presented without reconciliation. Readers will ask why "human tutoring" is 0.79 in one place and 0.29 "best current estimate" in the next. The difference is laboratory studies with narrow, proximal outcomes against school-based field RCTs, often on standardised tests. One sentence fixes it, and it is the same lesson the chapter teaches later.

**B10.5. ch10.md:64. Detector bias against non-native writers rests on one small study (91 essays).** A large-scale study on standardised-test essays found no such bias for a purpose-built detector (Jiang et al., *Computers & Education*, 2024, "Detecting ChatGPT-generated essays in a large-scale writing assessment: Is there a bias against non-native English speakers?"). Give the counter-evidence; the conclusion that detectors are unsafe for disciplinary use survives it.

**B10.6. ch10.md:122–124. Epic box: v1 against v2 AUC (0.63 against 0.82–0.92) is presented as "discrimination improved".** The 2021 study is one site, retrospective and hospitalisation-level. The 2026 study is four sites, prospective, with a different model version and possibly locally calibrated. These are not like-for-like. Note this, and give Epic's own claimed AUC (0.76–0.83) for the 2021 contrast. The gap between vendor claims and independent validation is the box's real lesson, and it is missing.

**B10.7. ch10.md:131–133. AMIE: company research not labelled consistently.** Funding is noted for Brodeur ("funded by Alphabet") but not for Tu and McDuff, which are by Google authors. OpenAI co-authorship is flagged for Fang, Phang and Chatterji. Apply one rule: flag company-authored studies at first mention.

**B10.8. ch10.md:133. Brodeur: denominators omitted.** PCPs returned surveys in only 60 of 98 cases. The "33 of 44 helpful" figure therefore comes from a self-selected 45% of visits. Say so.

**B10.9. ch10.md:137. Lukac: low uptake omitted.** The scribes were used in only 33.5% (DAX) and 29.5% (Nabla) of eligible visits (abstract). That matters for interpreting both the ITT effect and "fast-spreading".

**B10.10. ch10.md:113. MASAI "The most informative trial so far".** The superlative is defensible, but the chapter does not say that the comparator, double reading, is European practice. In the US (single reading plus CAD) the trade-off is different, so the result does not transfer directly. One clause is enough.

**B10.11. ch10.md:141, 143, 150. Survey and self-selection.** The chapter handles this well in the paragraph at line 145. That paragraph should come *before* the studies, not after, so that the reader reads the correlational findings with the warning already in mind.

## (D) Structure & coverage

- **"Everyday life" is thin.** Apart from companions, privacy and accessibility, the chapter says nothing about how ordinary people use AI for health information. The most relevant RCT is missing: Bean et al.'s Oxford study, in which about 1,300 members of the public using LLMs for medical scenarios identified relevant conditions *less* often than controls, even though the models alone scored highly (arXiv 2504.18919, 2025; check for the published version). It belongs right after ChatGPT Health (ch10.md:178), where the chapter cites OpenAI's 230 million weekly health users but no evidence on outcomes.
- **Teachers are absent.** The chapter treats education only through students. Teacher use of AI (lesson planning, marking, feedback) is the largest current deployment, and there is RCT evidence (e.g. the EEF ChatGPT lesson-preparation trial in England, 2024). The log says the EEF page was blocked; the report is widely mirrored.
- **10.6 mixes companions, therapy, lawsuits and legislation, and is the longest section.** Consider splitting it into "Companions and well-being: the evidence" and "Regulating companion chatbots".
- **Balance.** Health is treated with more methodological care than education. Education results from working papers and preprints (Nigeria, Rori, Tutor CoPilot, Maryland) get less scrutiny than the peer-reviewed medical trials. Reverse that.

## What works

**Chapter 9.** The chapter has a clear argument: cheapness changes production, not distribution. It carries that argument from the robocall opening to the closing synthesis. The quantitative work is unusually careful for a general book. The Kirchenbauer box's z-test, the base-rate example and the persuasion figures all check out against the primary sources to the decimal. The Debate box on 2024 is fair, and the chapter consistently distinguishes visible content from measured effects. The fixes are about balance (DSA critics, the AI-in-distribution channel, journalism coverage) and a handful of overstated reconciliations, not about the core.

**Chapter 10.** The chapter's central lesson is that design and outcome measure decide the verdict, and it teaches that lesson well. The Bastani material is an excellent anchor. The medical sections (the FDA pathway table, the evidence-ladder box, MASAI as a non-inferiority trial, the Epic box) are of a standard a clinical reader would respect, and the numbers check out. The weaknesses are uneven scrutiny: education working papers get gentler treatment than medical RCTs. There are also three verifiable misreports (Folk & Dunn, Vaccaro, Agweyu), and the closing synthesis is buried under the accessibility heading.
