## Chapter 7: Chips, Data Centres, Energy and Open Models

### What works

The physical-layer framing is the chapter's strongest idea. It works because the chapter keeps tying hardware facts back to policy, for example the H20 licence wording showing that "memory bandwidth can matter more than peak arithmetic" (l. 112). Its handling of energy uncertainty is better than almost anything in the popular literature:

- per-query figures with their system boundaries (Table 7.3);
- the market-based versus location-based emissions gap (l. 171);
- the IEA's warning about connection queues (l. 3).

The worked examples (ridge point, TPP, cost box) are correct and teach well. The closing synthesis paragraph (l. 299) is very good. The IEA-derived numbers are accurate to the source: I confirmed every Annex A value used in Figures 7.3 and 7.4 against the PDF.

### (A) Factual and reference errors

**A7-1. LBNL versus IEA comparison misleads the reader (verified wrong as framed). Severity: major.**
- **Where:** l. 140: "rose to 176 TWh (4.4% of US electricity) in 2023 … close to the IEA's figure for the following year."
- **What the source says:** IEA Annex A, Table A.4 gives the US at **154 TWh in 2023**, the same year as LBNL's 176 TWh. The two "independent" estimates differ by about 22 TWh (14%) for the same year. The text matches LBNL's 2023 figure to the IEA's 2024 figure (183), which hides that disagreement.
- **Why it matters:** this is exactly the boundary and method gap the section claims to teach.
- **Fix:** "…176 TWh (4.4%) in 2023, about 14% above the IEA's 154 TWh for the same year, a gap that reflects different boundaries and methods."

**A7-2. Arena gap expressed as a percentage. Severity: minor (technical).**
- **Where:** l. 254: "widened again to 49 points (3.4%)".
- **Problem:** Arena scores are Elo/Bradley–Terry ratings. Differences between them are meaningful; ratios are not, so 49/1,454 is a number without meaning. The AI Index's own highlights give 3.3%, using a different denominator, which shows how arbitrary the ratio is.
- **Fix:** drop the percentage and say what the gap means. A 49-point gap implies the closed model is preferred in roughly 57% of head-to-head votes: 1/(1+10^(−49/400)) ≈ 0.57.

**A7-3. GB200 NVL72 "1.4 exaflops of AI performance" given without its precision. Severity: minor.**
- **Where:** l. 49.
- **Problem:** this is NVIDIA's 4-bit, sparsity-assisted figure. Six lines earlier (l. 43) the chapter calls precision "the main trap in reading chip specifications" and criticises Google's Ironwood comparison for the same thing.
- **Fix:** "…1.4 exaflops of 4-bit 'AI performance' (a sparse figure, not comparable with the 16-bit numbers in Table 7.2)".

**A7-4. The 42 ms memory-bound example is internally inconsistent. Severity: minor (technical).**
- **Where:** l. 21. The text says a 70B model at 16 bits (140 GB) is "more than one H100 holds", then computes 42 ms per token using one H100's 3.35 TB/s.
- **Problem:** the model must be split across at least two GPUs, which halves the time per token, to about 21 ms.
- **Fix:** "reading those weights once at one H100's bandwidth would take about 42 ms; spread over two GPUs, about 21 ms per token for a single user."

**A7-5. The FP8 accumulation limit is attributed to the H800 as if it were an export-control artefact. Severity: minor.**
- **Where:** l. 41: "a limitation of their chip, the H800 … Its FP8 matrix units accumulated sums with only about 14 bits".
- **Problem:** the limited accumulation precision is a property of Hopper tensor cores and so applies to the H100 as well. The H800 differs mainly in its NVLink bandwidth. Placing the gloss here implies that DeepSeek was working around export-control crippling.
- **Fix:** "…a limitation of the Hopper tensor cores in their H800s (shared by the H100): FP8 products were accumulated with only about 14 bits of precision."

**A7-6. "The modern wave of open-weight language models began when Meta released Llama 2" is contestable. Severity: minor.**
- **Where:** l. 236.
- **Problem:** the claim contradicts l. 226, which cites GPT-NeoX (2022) and BLOOM (2022) as earlier fully open releases. It also ignores LLaMA 1 (February 2023, research release whose weights leaked) and Falcon (May 2023, Apache 2.0).
- **Fix:** "Open releases accelerated after Meta's LLaMA (February 2023) and especially Llama 2 (July 2023), the first from a major lab licensed for commercial use…"

**A7-7. Trade-truce attribution. Severity: minor.**
- **Where:** l. 118: "in September 2026 the US Treasury said the truce would run to 10 January 2027".
- **What the source says:** the cited AP report quotes Treasury Secretary Bessent in a Fox News interview saying "to Jan. 10". The report does not state the year and is not a Treasury statement (https://www.wxyz.com/politics/foreign-policy/trump-welcomes-chinas-xi-to-washington-with-planeside-ceremony-and-extension-of-trade-truce).
- **Datedness:** the chapter does not tell readers that the BIS affiliates-rule suspension it describes expires on 9 November 2026, two weeks after the book's cut-off.
- **Fix:** "Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said in September 2026 that the truce would be extended to 10 January; the one-year suspension of the affiliates rule was due to lapse on 9 November 2026 unless extended."

**A7-8. Broken cross-reference and unverified variant figure. Severity: minor.**
- **Where:** l. 66: "(Epoch's own dashboard gives 3.4 times [@epochai2026trends]; Section 4.4)".
- **Problem:** Section 4.4 contains no compute-stock or H100-equivalent discussion; it covers the growth of training compute (5× a year) and costs. Epoch's January 2026 data insight gives **3.3×** (90% CI 2.7–4.1) (https://epoch.ai/data-insights/ai-chip-production). I could not find the 3.4× figure.
- **Fix:** delete the parenthesis, or point it to Chapter 14 (the "Compute and investment" paragraph, ch14.md l. 151), which repeats the 3.3× figure.

**A7-9. "using chips made in a handful of factories with tools from one supplier" (l. 5). Severity: minor.**
- **Problem:** only EUV lithography has a single supplier. Deposition, etch and metrology come from Applied Materials, Lam, Tokyo Electron, KLA and others.
- **Fix:** "…with critical tools that, in the case of EUV lithography, only one company makes."

**A7-10. Evidence-labelling violations (preface rule: preprints labelled at first mention; opinions attributed). Severity: minor each, but systematic.**

**A7-11. Over-reliance on a party with a stake (one-sidedness). Severity: major.**

**A7-12. The cost box contradicts itself. Severity: minor.**
- **Where:** l. 196.
- **Problem:** the box says the amortised method "charges the run for its share of the hardware's working life", then says that "Neither includes … the cluster itself."

**A7-13. Overstated explanation for the water-figure spread. Severity: minor.**

**A7-14. Figure 7.2 caption overstates the change. Severity: minor.**

### (B) Technical and conceptual problems

1. **EDA is missing from the chokepoint story (major omission).** Chip-design software from Synopsys, Cadence and Siemens EDA is a classic US chokepoint and has actually been used as one:
   - controls on gate-all-around (GAAFET) EDA tools in August 2022;
   - sweeping EDA licence requirements on China imposed in May 2025 and lifted in July 2025 as part of trade bargaining.

   It belongs in the Concept box (l. 97) and in Figure 7.1.
2. **"Do the controls work?" (l. 120–122) is too thin to support its conclusion.** It has no evidence on:
   - China's domestic substitution: Huawei Ascend volumes, SMIC 7/5 nm yields, CXMT HBM;
   - smuggling and enforcement cases;
   - cloud access as a loophole.

   Without these the section cannot weigh "short of compute" against "workarounds". Add one paragraph with sourced numbers, labelled with uncertainty.
3. **Arithmetic intensity is explained but not named (l. 21).** Readers who meet "roofline" or "arithmetic intensity" elsewhere will not connect them to the chapter's explanation. Name the concept, and note that KV-cache reads add to the memory traffic in long-context serving. That would also link back to the "long inputs" point in §7.4.
4. **Table 7.2's "Chip-to-chip bandwidth" row compares unlike fabrics.** NVLink is an 8- or 72-GPU switched scale-up domain; Ironwood's ICI is a 3D torus over 9,216 chips. Add a footnote saying the numbers are per-chip aggregate link bandwidth and do not describe scale.
5. **Inference economics are absent from §7.5.** The section covers training costs and capex but not the price per token or inference unit economics, which is where cost is falling fastest and where most energy now goes.
6. **There is no debate on whether the capital spending can pay for itself, even though the chapter cites the evidence for one (l. 212).** It cites capex absorbing "almost all of their cash generation" and USD 200 bn of debt but never asks the question the October 2026 reader most wants answered: can revenue justify the spend? At minimum add the counter-view: revenue figures, circular vendor financing, and analyst or central-bank warnings, labelled as such. A Debate box here would earn its place more than the open-weight one, which is good but less timely.

## Chapter 8: Work, Productivity and the Economy

### What works

The organising chain from exposure to adoption, experiments, firm response and macro outcomes (Figure 8.1) is the right frame, and the chapter keeps to it. The opening contrast between METR and Noy & Zhang is an excellent hook that states the chapter's thesis. The Acemoglu versus Aghion & Bunel comparison (Table 8.4) is the best piece of economics teaching in either chapter; I confirmed all four Aghion & Bunel inputs against their PDF. The entry-level Debate box is balanced and up to date. The hidden-labour section adds a perspective most economics surveys omit. The effect sizes are accurate: I checked against Crossref abstracts for Noy & Zhang, Brynjolfsson et al., Dell'Acqua et al., Cui et al., Otis et al., Demirci et al., Vivalt et al., Hosseini Maasoum & Lichtinger, Bick et al. and Acemoglu (2025), and against arXiv for Eloundou and Becker.

### (A) Factual and reference errors

**A8-1. The J-curve inference is a category error (conceptual error). Severity: major.**
- **Where:** l. 220: "So is the gap between time savings of 1–3% of work hours in surveys and gains of 15% or more in many experiments."
- **Problem:** the survey figure is time saved as a share of *all* work hours, including non-users and non-exposed tasks (Bick et al.: "1.4% of total work hours"). The experimental figure is the saving *within treated tasks*. The gap is mostly arithmetic coverage (share of hours touched × per-task saving), not evidence of unmeasured intangible investment. Relatedly, "85% of users reallocating saved time to other work" does not by itself indicate a J-curve.
- **Fix:** delete the sentence, or replace it with: "The gap between survey time savings (1–3% of all hours) and experimental gains (15%+ within affected tasks) mostly reflects how few hours AI yet touches; it is not in itself evidence of a J-curve."

**A8-2. One-sided conclusion on learning; ignores the chapter's own flagship study. Severity: major.**
- **Where:** l. 252: "in the one study that tested learning directly, the same help reduced what they learned."
- **What the sources say:**
  - The abstract of Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, cited throughout the chapter, says: "AI assistance facilitates worker learning and improves English fluency" (https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae044).
  - Pedersen et al. (l. 248) tested deskilling and upskilling and found neither.
  - The Shen & Tamkin participants were not novices: they had more than a year of Python and were new only to the library.
- **Fix:** "The evidence on learning is mixed: in customer support, agents' gains persisted when the assistant was unavailable, suggesting learning [@brynjolfsson2025generative], whereas in a small coding experiment, help from AI reduced what programmers learned about a new library [@shen2026how]."

**A8-3. A St. Louis Fed result is presented as evidence it does not provide. Severity: major.**
- **Where:** l. 170, Debate box, "The case that it is": "attributes about a third of the rise in 18- to 24-year-olds' unemployment … to growing demand for AI skills in job postings".
- **What the source says:** the variable is the share of Lightcast postings requiring AI skills. The authors say it "captures changes in job content, not whether firms use AI to automate work or screen applicants" (https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2026/jun/how-shifts-labor-supply-demand-shape-outcomes-young-workers). That is evidence of skill mismatch, not of AI substituting for entry-level workers. It is also a Fed bank blog decomposition, not peer-reviewed.
- **Fix:** move it out of "the case that it is", or add: "…a measure of changing skill requirements rather than of automation, as the authors note."

**A8-4. Internal contradiction about what evidence is still needed. Severity: minor to major.**
- **Where:** l. 174: "Settling the question needs the Danish test, which links each firm's actual adoption to its hiring, repeated with US data".
- **Problem:** the chapter has already cited (l. 165 and in the box) Hosseini Maasoum & Lichtinger, who do exactly that with US résumé data on 65 million workers. The abstract confirms: "junior employment declines sharply in adopting firms relative to non-adopters", via slower hiring.
- **Fix:** "A US firm-level study (Hosseini Maasoum & Lichtinger) points the opposite way from the Danish one; settling the question needs administrative-quality US data linking adoption to hiring, observed after 2024."

### (B) Technical and conceptual problems

1. **"Automation" and "augmentation" mean different things in different sources, and the chapter never warns readers.**
   - Anthropic uses an LLM classification of conversation style.
   - The Census survey uses firm self-reports.
   - The Canaries paper uses Anthropic-based occupation shares.
   - BLS says its categories cannot distinguish the two.

   Add one sentence in §8.2 or the Concept box, because the Debate box leans on the distinction ("concentrated where AI substitutes").
2. **The inequality mechanism is asserted as fact.** l. 139: "Within a task they usually shrink inequality, because AI transfers the practices of strong performers to weak ones." Only Brynjolfsson et al. offer suggestive evidence for this mechanism; Noy & Zhang show compression without identifying why. Write "…possibly because…".
3. **Weak support for the Figure 8.1 claim "Evidence becomes scarcer at each step".** Firm and payroll data are plentiful; what is scarce is *causal* evidence linking AI to firm outcomes. The spec itself notes this. Say "causal evidence becomes scarcer".
4. **Hulten's theorem uses Domar weights.** l. 183 says "the task's share of GDP". Hulten's weights are sales over GDP and can sum to more than 1 with intermediate inputs. Acemoglu's application is fine, but a footnote would prevent an expert objection.
5. **The macro section omits the main argument for larger effects.** AI automating research itself (idea production) is the source of the large estimates (Aghion & Bunel flag it in their abstract; Davidson; Erdil & Besiroglu). One sentence plus a pointer to Chapter 14 would balance the section.

### (C) Writing and explanation

1. **§8.1 is overloaded with method detail before the reader has a reason to care.** Examples: the ILO's 29,753 tasks, 1,640 respondents, 52,558 ratings and 2,861 tasks; Gradient 4's "μ ≥ 0.6 and μ − σ ≥ 0.5"; the O*NET 27.2 counts; agreement of 80.8%/82.1%. Keep one sentence per method on *what is scored and by whom*, and move the rest to a Going deeper box or endnotes. The Concept box (l. 41–45) already does the summarising job well.
2. **Number dump in §8.2 (l. 57–59).** The paragraph holds about 13 percentages: 62, 45, 27, 10, 39.2, 5.7, 4.1, 1.6, 1.4, 6.3, 2.2, 1.3 and others. Lead with the contrast that matters (most adults use it; it touches about 6% of work hours; it saves about 2%) and put the series in Table 8.2.
3. **Version-history leakage (as in Chapter 7):**
   - l. 163: "(15% with data to July 2025; earlier versions headlined a regression-adjusted 13%)". This gives two numbers for the same data vintage without explaining that they come from different methods, which will confuse readers. Drop the parenthesis or explain it in one clause.
   - Table 8.2 caption: "which raised the August 2024 estimate from 39.4% to 44.6% (their February 2025 working paper had reported nearly 40% for late 2024)".
