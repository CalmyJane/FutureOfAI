# fig-14-01

- **type:** dot plot (two rows; a bar starting at an arbitrary year would give bar length no meaning)
- **purpose:** Show readers that the framing of a survey question moved AI researchers' aggregate forecasts far more than one year of new evidence did.
- **caption:** Figure 14.1: Aggregate year by which AI researchers gave a 50% chance to two milestones, in surveys run in 2022 and 2023. "High-level machine intelligence" asks when machines will do every task better and more cheaply than humans; "full automation of labour" asks when every occupation will be automatable. The gap between framings is larger than the change between years. Source: [@grace2025thousands].
- **content:**
  - Two rows, each with two dots (2022 survey: open circle; 2023 survey: filled circle) joined by a grey line.
  - Group 1, label "High-level machine intelligence (HLMI)": 2022 survey = 2060; 2023 survey = 2047. (The 2023 survey reports 2047, "13 years earlier" than the 2022 estimate; Table 6 of the source gives 2060 for 2022 directly.) Source: grace2025thousands.
  - Group 2, label "Full automation of labour (FAOL)": 2022 survey = 2164; 2023 survey = 2116. Source: grace2025thousands.
  - Axis: "Year at which aggregate forecast reaches 50% probability", linear scale, range 2020 to 2180, ticks every 20 years. Optional vertical reference line at 2026 labelled "today".
  - Legend: "2022 survey", "2023 survey"; note below: "n = number of respondents answering that question".
  - Value label above each dot; below each dot the number of respondents to that question, from grace2025thousands Table 6 (arXiv v3, appendix B.2): HLMI 2022 n = 461, 2023 n = 1,714; FAOL 2022 n = 202, 2023 n = 774. (Total respondents, 738 in 2022 and 2,778 in 2023, are not shown: each framing was put to only part of the sample, and the 738 figure is not in the cited source.)
