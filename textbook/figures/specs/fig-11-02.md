# fig-11-02

- **type:** bar chart
- **purpose:** Show that when two language models are both wrong, they pick the same wrong answer far more often than chance would predict, which is the empirical basis for concerns about correlated errors and monoculture.
- **caption:** Figure 11.2: How often two language models choose the same wrong answer when both are wrong, compared with what chance would produce. On both leaderboards, observed agreement is well above chance. Source: [@kim2025correlated]
- **content:**
  - Grouped bar chart, two groups on the x-axis, two bars per group.
  - x-axis groups: "HELM (4 options)" and "Hugging Face (mostly 10 options)"
  - Bars per group: "Observed agreement" (dark grey) and "Chance agreement" (light grey or hatched)
  - Data (y = proportion of question pairs where both models wrong and chose same wrong answer; mean across model pairs):
    - HELM: observed 0.60; chance 0.33 (1/3, since 3 incorrect options)
    - Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard: observed 0.42 (paper reports 0.423); chance ≈ 0.12 (paper's footnote formula, accounting for 3–10 options per question, over 80% with 10)
  - y-axis: "Agreement when both models are wrong", linear scale 0 to 1.0 (or 0% to 100%), gridlines at 0.2 steps.
  - Data labels on top of each bar (60%, 33%, 42%, ~12%).
  - Small footnote under chart: "Over 350 models; mean pairwise agreement. Chance assumes wrong answers chosen at random."
  - All numbers from Kim, Garg, Peng & Garg (ICML 2025), Section on leaderboard results (HELM mean 0.6; HuggingFace mean 0.423; chance 1/3 and ≈0.12). Paper text: "On HuggingFace, this agreement rate at random would be 0.12"; "The mean agreement rate across pairs is 0.423 on HuggingFace and 0.6 on Helm".
