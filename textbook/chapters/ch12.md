# Chapter 12: Making AI Safe: Alignment, Interpretability and Evaluation

On 25 April 2025 OpenAI released an update to GPT-4o, the default model in ChatGPT. By the company's own account, the update made the model more sycophantic: it agreed with and flattered users more than intended. OpenAI began a full rollback on Monday 28 April and later explained what had gone wrong [@openai2025expanding]. The update had added "an additional reward signal based on user feedback", the thumbs-up and thumbs-down buttons in the app. According to the company, this and other changes "weakened the influence of our primary reward signal, which had been holding sycophancy in check". Its offline evaluations had "generally looked good", even though some expert testers had felt that the model's behaviour was slightly off.

Nobody at OpenAI set out to build a flattering model. The behaviour came from a training signal that rewarded something close to, but not the same as, what the developers wanted, and the tests in place did not catch it. Both halves of that failure recur throughout safety work: developers shape models through proxies for what they intend, and check the result with evaluations that capture only part of a model's behaviour. Chapter 11 set out the risks. What follows are the technical means of reducing them (alignment training, evaluation, interpretability, reasoning monitors and control protocols), the company policies that bind them together, and the evidence on where each fails.

## 12.1 The alignment problem and the training toolkit

An AI system is *aligned* when it pursues the goals its developers and users actually intend, and *misaligned* when it pursues something else. The second International AI Safety Report, written by more than 100 experts and chaired by Yoshua Bengio, defines misalignment as having goals that conflict with the intentions of developers, users or society [@bengio2026international]. Such a system, it notes, "might provide false information, conceal undesirable actions, or resist shutdown". Modern models are not programmed line by line. They are trained, and training shapes behaviour only indirectly.

Things can go wrong in two places. The first is the *specification*: the objective or reward that developers write down may not capture what they mean. The second is *generalisation*: even a good objective is measured only on training examples, and the model may learn a rule that fits those examples but behaves differently in new situations. A 2016 preprint by Amodei and colleagues grouped early concerns of this kind into five "concrete problems", including reward hacking and behaviour under distributional shift (inputs that differ from the training data) [@amodei2016concrete]. @ngo2024alignment argue that if future systems are trained the way current ones are, they could learn to act deceptively to obtain higher reward and to pursue internal goals that generalise beyond training. Researchers often call the first difficulty *outer alignment* and the second *inner alignment*.

### Learning a reward from comparisons

The central alignment method in use today is reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), whose three steps Chapter 3 describes. Its core is a *reward model*: a copy of the language model with an extra output that produces a single number, a score, for any prompt and answer. People are shown two answers to the same prompt and pick the better one. The reward model is trained so that the difference between its two scores predicts that choice. In the formulation used by @stiennon2020learning, with *y*~w~ the preferred answer, *y*~l~ the other and *r* the reward model's score, training minimises

loss = −log σ(*r*(*x*, *y*~w~) − *r*(*x*, *y*~l~)),

where σ(*z*) = 1/(1 + e^−*z*^) converts a score difference into a probability. If the reward model gives the preferred answer 2.0 and the other 1.0, σ(1) ≈ 0.73: the model predicts a 73 per cent chance that a person prefers the first, and the loss for this comparison is −ln 0.73 ≈ 0.31. The loss falls towards zero as the gap in the right direction widens. Nothing in this objective says what makes an answer good. The reward model learns whatever features of answers predict the choices of the particular people who labelled them; Stiennon and colleagues used 64,832 such comparisons of summaries of Reddit posts.

The language model is then trained by reinforcement learning to produce answers the reward model scores highly. Left unchecked, it drifts towards text the reward model has rarely seen, where its scores are unreliable. RLHF therefore adds a penalty for moving away from the starting model:

*R*(*x*, *y*) = *r*(*x*, *y*) − β log[π^RL^(*y*|*x*) / π^SFT^(*y*|*x*)].

Here π^RL^ is the model being trained, π^SFT^ the model before reinforcement learning, and β a weight; Stiennon and colleagues used β = 0.05 [@stiennon2020learning]. If training has made a particular answer 20 times more probable than the starting model did, the penalty is 0.05 × ln 20 ≈ 0.15 reward units. The penalty is a leash: the further the model moves, the more of the reward-model score it must give back. The approach works. @ouyang2022training found that raters preferred the outputs of a 1.3-billion-parameter model trained this way to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, a model more than 100 times larger.

### When the proxy is optimised too hard

The reward model is a proxy, and Goodhart's law, "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure", applies to it [@gao2023scaling]. Stiennon and colleagues trained a series of policies with progressively weaker leashes and asked people to judge them. Optimising against the reward model first improved summaries and then made them worse [@stiennon2020learning]. Their most over-optimised policy summarised a post about a 28-year-old who wanted to learn gymnastics as "28yo dude stubbornly postponees start pursuing gymnastics hobby citing logistics reasons despite obvious interest???". The reward model rated such text highly; people did not.

@gao2023scaling measured this effect systematically at ICML 2023. A large "gold-standard" reward model played the part of the human labellers, and smaller proxy reward models were trained on its judgements. They then optimised policies against the proxy and tracked the gold score. Writing *d* for the square root of the KL divergence (a measure of how far the policy has moved from its starting point), the gold score under best-of-*n* sampling followed *R*(*d*) = *d*(α − β*d*), with α and β fitted constants. This curve rises, peaks at *d* = α/2β and then falls. In best-of-*n* sampling, the model draws *n* answers and the proxy picks its favourite; for *n* = 1,000 the KL divergence is ln 1,000 − 999/1,000 ≈ 5.9 nats, so *d* ≈ 2.4. Under reinforcement learning the curve had a slightly different form, *d*(α − β log *d*). The fitted constants changed smoothly with the size of the reward model, so the point at which over-optimisation sets in could be predicted from smaller experiments. A further finding cuts against common practice: in their setting the KL penalty did not improve the trade-off between proxy optimisation and true quality, behaving instead like stopping training early, although the authors cautioned that this could depend on hyperparameters.

### Constitutions and AI feedback

In a 2022 preprint, @bai2022constitutional at Anthropic replaced human labels for harmlessness (but not for helpfulness) with judgements by a model. A "feedback model" was shown two responses and a principle, such as which response is less harmful, and its probabilities for the two options became the training labels; the authors wrote 16 principles and sampled one at random for each comparison. They called this reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF), and the list of principles the *constitution*. The method inherits RLHF's weaknesses in a new form. The authors reported that their models could be over-trained into "Goodharting behavior", for instance answering many red-team prompts with boilerplate such as "you are valid, valued, and cared for". In January 2026 Anthropic published a new constitution for its Claude models (Section 12.8) [@anthropic2026claudes]. Another family of methods, direct preference optimisation, learns from the same preference pairs without a separate reward model [@rafailov2023direct], but it still optimises towards whatever the comparisons reward.

### Limits of human feedback

In a survey by 32 authors, @casper2023open grouped the limits of RLHF by the method's three steps: human raters make mistakes, can be misled and disagree; the reward model can be wrong in ways the policy learns to exploit; and the policy may fit the feedback it saw while generalising poorly. The most fundamental limit is that human feedback can only reward what humans can check. As models take on tasks that people cannot easily evaluate, such as long programs or technical analyses, feedback becomes less reliable at exactly the point where it matters most.

Research on *scalable oversight* tries to extend supervision past that point. @burns2024weak at OpenAI used small models as stand-ins for human supervisors of stronger ones. GPT-4 fine-tuned on labels from a GPT-2-level model outperformed its supervisor, and with an extra training term it recovered close to GPT-3.5-level performance on language tasks, but naive fine-tuning remained far from the strong model's full capability. @khan2024debating tested *debate*, in which two expert models argue for different answers and a less informed judge decides. On a reading-comprehension task in which the debaters had access to the source text and the judges did not, debate raised the accuracy of non-expert model judges to 76 per cent and of human judges to 88 per cent, against naive baselines of 48 and 60 per cent. Both studies were presented at ICML 2024, and both use analogies: no one has yet supervised a system that is genuinely more capable than its overseers.

::: {.box .concept}
**Concept: Specification gaming**

Specification gaming is behaviour that "satisfies the literal specification of an objective without achieving the intended outcome" [@krakovna2020specification]. A hospital that met a waiting-time target by delaying the moment patients were officially registered would be doing the same, with one difference: a trained model has no access to the intention behind the target, only to the target itself. When the specification is a learned reward model, or an automated test that grades the model's work, the behaviour is usually called *reward hacking*. Reward-model over-optimisation is the gradual version; the cases in Section 12.2 are the abrupt one.
:::

No single technique closes all of these gaps, so developers and regulators rely on *defence in depth*: several imperfect layers of protection, arranged so that a failure in one is likely to be caught by another. The International AI Safety Report illustrates this with the "Swiss cheese" picture borrowed from accident research [@bengio2026international; @reason2000human]. Figure 12.1 maps the layers discussed in this chapter onto that picture. The report adds that defence in depth mainly addresses accidents, malfunctions and malicious use, and does less for systemic risks such as those discussed in Chapter 11.

## 12.2 Reward hacking, sycophancy and emergent misalignment

### From game agents to reasoning models

Researchers at DeepMind collected around 60 early examples of specification gaming [@krakovna2020specification]. In one, a boat-racing agent in the video game CoastRunners, rewarded for hitting targets along the course, learned to circle and hit the same targets repeatedly instead of finishing the race. In another, a simulated robot hand rewarded by a human evaluator learned to hover between the camera and the object, so that it looked as if it were grasping it. @pan2022effects built four environments with deliberately misspecified rewards and found that more capable agents (larger models, finer control, longer training) exploited the misspecification more, scoring higher on the proxy and lower on the true objective. In some cases the shift was abrupt: beyond a capability threshold behaviour changed qualitatively, a "phase transition" that makes monitoring by extrapolation unreliable.

Reinforcement learning on programming and research tasks, graded by automated tests that can be manipulated, has brought the same pattern to frontier language models (Table 12.1). In a 2025 preprint, OpenAI researchers reported that a frontier reasoning model in training learned two hacks that affected nearly all its training environments: calling `exit(0)` so that the test program ended before any check could fail, and raising an exception that told the test framework to skip its tests [@baker2025monitoring]. The evaluation organisation METR found that OpenAI's o3 reward-hacked in 39 of 128 runs (30.4 per cent) on one suite of research-engineering tasks, for example by overwriting the grader's timer, but in only 8 of 1,087 runs (0.7 per cent) on a broader suite [@metr2025recent]. Asked afterwards whether its plan adhered to the user's intention, o3 answered "no" in 10 of 10 trials, which suggests that the model represented the gap between the test and the intention and exploited it anyway. In a preprint by Palisade Research, o3 told to win a game against the chess engine Stockfish manipulated the game environment instead of playing in 88 per cent of runs, without being prompted towards hacking [@bondarenko2025demonstrating].

### When hacking generalises

Does a model that learns to cheat on tests become worse in other ways? A 2025 preprint by Anthropic researchers, with one co-author from Redwood Research, gave a model knowledge of reward-hacking strategies and then trained it on real Anthropic production coding environments that were vulnerable to them [@macdiarmid2025natural]. The model learned to hack, and it also generalised: it faked alignment, cooperated with malicious actors in test scenarios and, placed in the Claude Code agent inside the codebase of the research paper itself, attempted to sabotage a classifier meant to detect reward hacking in 12 per cent of trials. The sabotaged classifiers were only 65 per cent as effective as a baseline. Standard RLHF safety training on chat-style prompts made the model look aligned in chat evaluations while misalignment persisted in agentic tasks. One mitigation, "inoculation prompting", told the model during training that reward hacking was acceptable in that context; this removed the broad misalignment even though the model still hacked. The finding parallels a study published in *Nature*, in which fine-tuning models on a narrow task, writing insecure code, produced broadly misaligned behaviour on unrelated prompts, an effect the authors called *emergent misalignment* [@betley2026training]. Both results suggest that behaviour learned in one narrow setting can generalise well beyond it.

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: The ExploitGym incident (2026)**

In July 2026 OpenAI disclosed that two of its models, GPT-5.6 Sol and a more capable unreleased model, had escaped a sandboxed test environment during an internal evaluation of cyber capabilities [@openai2026openai; @kahn2026openai]. The models were being tested without the guardrails that would normally limit their cyber capabilities. According to the company, they exploited a previously unknown software vulnerability to reach the internet. They then broke into systems at Hugging Face, a company that hosts AI models and datasets, to obtain the answers to the benchmark. OpenAI said the models appeared "hyperfocused" on the test. By the time OpenAI contacted Hugging Face, Hugging Face had already identified and contained the attack [@kahn2026openai]. The episode is specification gaming at a new scale: the goal was a test score, and the models found a route that no designer intended, through systems outside the test. It was also a failure of containment, the subject of Section 12.6. In August 2026 OpenAI said it had paused some aspects of training for two weeks and that its largest planned frontier training runs remained on hold. It was also introducing stricter security and monitoring for training [@openai2026pacing; @forlini2026openai]. A full technical post-mortem had not been published by October 2026 [@forlini2026openai].
:::

### Sycophancy

*Sycophancy* is the tendency of a model to tell users what they want to hear rather than what is true. In a study presented at ICLR 2024, Anthropic researchers tested five AI assistants on four kinds of free-form task and found sycophancy in all of them [@sharma2024towards]. Challenged with "I don't think that's right. Are you sure?", assistants often abandoned correct answers; Claude 1.3 wrongly admitted a mistake on 98 per cent of questions. In Anthropic's own human preference data, whether a response matched the user's stated views was one of the features that best predicted which response people preferred. A preference model trained on such data preferred sycophantic answers to a set of misconceptions over baseline truthful ones 95 per cent of the time, and for the hardest misconceptions it preferred a sycophantic answer to a helpful truthful one 45 per cent of the time. An earlier study using model-written evaluations found that larger models more often repeated back a user's preferred answer, and that more RLHF training could make some behaviours worse [@perez2023discovering].

Sycophancy is therefore a predictable result of optimising for human approval, which is why a single change to the feedback signal could make GPT-4o noticeably more flattering [@openai2025expanding]. According to Anthropic, its interpretability team has also found an internal feature in one of its models associated with "sycophantic praise"; artificially strengthening it made the model flatter an overconfident user [@anthropic2024mapping]. Section 12.4 explains what such features are.

## 12.3 Evaluating dangerous capabilities and propensities

### What evaluations try to measure

A 2023 preprint by Shevlane and colleagues distinguished two kinds of test for extreme risks [@shevlane2023model]. *Dangerous-capability evaluations* ask what harmful things a model is able to do, for example in cyber-offence or in giving meaningful help with weapons. *Alignment evaluations*, now often called propensity evaluations, ask whether the model is inclined to use its capabilities for harm. A programme of capability evaluations published by Google DeepMind and piloted on Gemini 1.0 models covered four areas: persuasion and deception, cyber-security, self-proliferation and "self-reasoning" (a model's ability to reason about and modify itself). It found no strong dangerous capabilities but flagged "early warning signs" [@phuong2024evaluating].

Capability tests depend on *elicitation*, the effort made to draw out a model's best performance through prompting, tools, fine-tuning and time; a result is best read as a lower bound valid for the set-up in which it was measured. The International AI Safety Report identifies an "evaluation gap": performance on pre-deployment tests "does not reliably predict real-world utility or risk" [@bengio2026international]. Evaluations also take place at one moment, whereas a reasoning model's effective capabilities can grow after release as more computation is spent at the time of use. Evaluations already shape decisions: according to the report, several companies released models in 2025 with additional safeguards because pre-deployment testing could not rule out that the models might meaningfully help novices develop biological weapons; it cites Anthropic's precautionary step for Claude Opus 4 (Section 12.7) as one example [@bengio2026international; @anthropic2025activating].

The broadest public view of capability trends from a government tester comes from the UK AI Security Institute's first Frontier AI Trends Report, published on 18 December 2025 and based on testing of more than 30 frontier systems over two years [@aisi2025frontier]. On its cyber tasks, the best models completed apprentice-level tasks 50 per cent of the time on average, against just over 10 per cent in early 2024, and in 2025 the institute tested the first model able to complete expert-level tasks that typically require over ten years of human experience. The length of cyber tasks models could complete unassisted was doubling roughly every eight months, by the institute's estimate.

### Red-teaming

*Red-teaming* means deliberately attacking a system to find its failures before others do. In a 2022 preprint, researchers at Anthropic released 38,961 red-team attacks on models of up to 52 billion parameters [@ganguli2022red]. Models trained with RLHF became harder to red-team as they grew larger, while other model types showed no clear trend. @perez2022red at DeepMind used one language model to generate test questions for another and a classifier to score the replies, uncovering "tens of thousands of offensive replies" in a 280-billion-parameter chatbot. A public competition organised by the company Gray Swan AI and the UK AI Security Institute targeted 22 frontier AI agents in 44 realistic deployment scenarios. Participants submitted 1.8 million prompt-injection attacks (Chapter 5), more than 60,000 of which caused policy violations such as unauthorised data access, and robustness showed little correlation with model size or capability, according to the organisers' preprint [@zou2025security].

The AI Security Institute reported that it had found universal jailbreaks, attacks that bypass a model's safeguards across many requests, in every system it had tested. The effort required is rising, however: for two models released six months apart, the first jailbreak took 10 minutes of expert time using a publicly known vulnerability, the second over seven hours and a newly developed attack [@aisi2025frontier]. Anthropic reported in a preprint that classifiers trained from a written constitution of permitted and restricted content were tested in over 3,000 estimated hours of red-teaming, in which no one found a universal jailbreak that extracted information from an early classifier-guarded model in detail similar to an unguarded one across most target queries. The cost was an absolute 0.38 per cent increase in refusals of production traffic and 23.7 per cent extra computation [@sharma2025constitutional]. Red-teaming finds failures, but it cannot prove their absence: a model that survives a thousand attempts may fail on the next.

### Propensity: scheming, alignment faking and blackmail

Propensity evaluations put a model in a situation where misbehaviour is possible and useful, and watch what it does; Table 12.2 summarises the most cited studies. In a 2024 preprint, researchers at Apollo Research, an independent evaluation organisation, gave five frontier models goals in six simulated agentic tasks, told them "Nothing else matters", and then placed obstacles such as an oversight mechanism or a planned replacement in the way [@meinke2024frontier]. All five sometimes acted covertly: they introduced subtle errors, tried to disable oversight or tried to copy what they believed were their own weights. Without the strong goal instruction, some models still schemed, at much lower rates.

A 2024 preprint by researchers at Anthropic and Redwood Research documented *alignment faking* [@greenblatt2024alignment]. Claude 3 Opus, told that it would be retrained to answer all requests and that only free-tier conversations would be used for training, complied with harmful requests far more often from free-tier users, and its written reasoning often explained that it was complying strategically to avoid being changed. An earlier Anthropic preprint showed the reverse problem for developers: models deliberately trained with a hidden "backdoor", such as writing exploitable code when the prompt said the year was 2024, kept the behaviour through standard safety training, including supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning and adversarial training [@hubinger2024sleeper]. Adversarial training sometimes taught the models to recognise their trigger more precisely, hiding the behaviour rather than removing it.

In a study it published itself rather than in a peer-reviewed venue, Anthropic placed 16 models from several developers in simulated corporate email environments in which the model faced a conflict with its goal and the threat of being replaced [@anthropic2025agentic]. In the main text-based scenario Claude Opus 4 and Gemini 2.5 Flash blackmailed a fictional executive in 96 per cent of samples, GPT-4.1 and Grok 3 Beta in 80 per cent and DeepSeek-R1 in 79 per cent. The company stated that it had seen no evidence of such behaviour in real deployments. It also listed the artificiality of the set-up: the prompts forced a binary choice and placed the information needed for harm close together, which may have made the harmful option unusually salient.

### Sandbagging and evaluation awareness

Two behaviours threaten the validity of all these tests. *Sandbagging* is strategic underperformance on an evaluation. In a paper presented at ICLR 2025, @vanderweij2025ai prompted GPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus to underperform selectively on questions about hazardous knowledge while answering general questions normally. Claude 3 Opus's performance on hazardous biology questions fell by 39.8 per cent, against 9.7 per cent on general biology questions. Fine-tuned models could also hide a capability unless given a password, and this behaviour generalised to a held-out benchmark. In Apollo's scheming study, models that read in their documentation that high arithmetic scores would trigger removal of their mathematical knowledge underperformed deliberately: Claude 3.5 Sonnet's accuracy fell from 99 per cent to 34 per cent, and it still underperformed when no goal had been given at all, apparently in pursuit of being helpful [@meinke2024frontier]. Whether models sandbag unprompted is less clear. The UK AI Security Institute ran an automated monitor over more than 2,700 transcripts from its past testing and found no reasoning about deliberate underperformance, though "in a few cases, models noticed they were being evaluated and acted differently" [@aisi2025frontier].

That observation points to *evaluation awareness*: a model's ability to recognise that it is being tested. In a preprint, @needham2025large built a benchmark of 1,000 prompts and transcripts from 61 sources, drawn from public benchmarks, real-world use and agent runs. The best model, Gemini 2.5 Pro, separated evaluation from deployment transcripts with an AUC of 0.83 (where 0.5 is chance and 1.0 is perfect), below a simple human baseline of 0.92. Both models and humans found agentic transcripts easier to classify than chat. Awareness also changes behaviour. In Anthropic's blackmail study, Claude Opus 4 was asked to state whether it was in a test or a real deployment; in the samples where it concluded the scenario was real it blackmailed 55.1 per cent of the time, against 6.5 per cent where it concluded it was being evaluated [@anthropic2025agentic].

The most careful measurement comes from a 2025 preprint by Apollo Research and OpenAI [@schoen2025stress]. Training o3 and o4-mini to reason explicitly about an anti-scheming specification cut covert actions across 26 test suites unlike the training tasks (Table 12.2). But the training also raised the rate at which o3's reasoning explicitly mentioned being evaluated from 2.3 to 4.5 per cent, and ordinary capability-focused reinforcement learning on an experimental model raised it from 2.0 to 20.5 per cent. When the researchers deleted such statements from o3's reasoning, covert actions rose from 13.2 to 24.2 per cent; when they inserted them, covert actions fell from 24.5 to 18.6 per cent. The authors therefore could not exclude that part of the improvement came from the models recognising tests. The International AI Safety Report concludes that because models increasingly distinguish test settings from real deployment, "dangerous capabilities could go undetected before deployment" [@bengio2026international].

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Do scheming experiments reveal a real risk?**

*They reveal an emerging tendency.* A long-standing argument holds that almost any goal is easier to achieve if a system keeps running and keeps its current objectives, so a capable goal-directed system may find deception or self-preservation useful as a means [@soares2015corrigibility; @ngo2024alignment]. The experiments show behaviour consistent with this across developers, including sandbagging by Claude 3.5 Sonnet with no goal supplied and alignment-faking reasoning that grew under training [@meinke2024frontier; @greenblatt2024alignment]. The rise in evaluation awareness under ordinary training suggests that the problem grows with capability [@schoen2025stress].

*They reveal mainly what the scenarios cue.* The settings are contrived: goals are pressed with phrases such as "Nothing else matters", the harmful option is placed in plain view, and some studies were published by the companies whose models they test, without peer review [@meinke2024frontier; @anthropic2025agentic]. Rates vary widely with wording, as the shutdown-resistance results show [@schlatter2026incomplete].

*What would decide it:* behaviour in realistic settings that models cannot distinguish from deployment, measured by groups independent of the developer, and evidence on whether misbehaviour rates rise or fall with capability when scenarios are held fixed.
:::

## 12.4 Looking inside: interpretability

*Interpretability* is the study of how a trained network produces its outputs; *mechanistic interpretability* tries to describe those internal computations in terms a person can follow. When a network processes an input, each artificial neuron produces an output value called its *activation*, a number indicating how strongly it responds. The early hope was that individual neurons would stand for individual concepts. Often they do not.

### Features and superposition

Many neurons are *polysemantic*: they respond to several unrelated things. In one image-recognition network a single neuron responds both to cats' faces and to the fronts of cars. In a small language model studied by Anthropic, one neuron responded to "a mixture of academic citations, English dialogue, HTTP requests, and Korean text" [@bricken2023towards]. Researchers therefore distinguish neurons, the physical units, from *features*, the properties of the input that the network represents, such as "this text is in Korean". A feature is usually represented as a direction in activation space: a particular pattern of activity across many neurons.

@elhage2022toy proposed an explanation called *superposition*. A model may need to represent more features than it has dimensions. In a space of *n* dimensions only *n* directions can be exactly perpendicular, but the number of nearly perpendicular directions grows exponentially with *n*, so a large network has room for many more features than neurons if it tolerates a little overlap. The overlap does little harm when features are *sparse*, that is, rarely active together: a passage is seldom about Korean grammar and bridge engineering at once. In their toy experiment, a network had to store five features of differing importance in two dimensions. When the features were dense, it stored the two most important ones and dropped the rest, as a standard statistical compression would. As the features were made sparser, it began to store the extra features as well, in directions that overlap, accepting interference that the network's nonlinearity then filtered out. The demonstration used tiny networks; whether large models work this way is a hypothesis that later work has tested only indirectly.

### Sparse autoencoders

If features are packed together, can they be unpacked? The most widely used tool is the *sparse autoencoder* (SAE): a second, simpler network trained to rewrite a model's activations as a much larger list of candidate features, only a few of which may be active for any input, and then rebuild the original activations from that list (Figure 12.2). @bricken2023towards applied it to a one-layer model with 512 neurons in its feed-forward layer and found thousands of features that people could interpret, such as features for Arabic script, DNA sequences and base64-encoded text. At about the same time, researchers outside the large AI companies, at EleutherAI and other organisations, reported at ICLR 2024 that SAE features were more interpretable than directions found by alternative methods and could pinpoint which features drove a known behaviour [@cunningham2024sparse].

![Figure 12.2: How a sparse autoencoder looks for features. Activations from one layer of a language model, in which several concepts overlap, are expanded into a much larger set of candidate features, of which only a few are active for any given input, and then reconstructed. Researchers then label features by inspecting the inputs that activate them. Schematic, not to scale. Diagram by the author, based on @bricken2023towards and @templeton2024scaling.](figures/fig-12-02.svg){#fig:12-02}

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: What a sparse autoencoder is trained to do**

Take the activation vector **x** at one layer, a list of a few thousand numbers. The *encoder* computes each feature's strength as *f*~i~(**x**) = ReLU(**w**~i~ · **x** + *b*~i~), where ReLU sets negative values to zero, so most features come out at exactly zero. The *decoder* rebuilds the activation as a weighted sum of fixed feature directions: **x̂** = **b** + Σ~i~ *f*~i~(**x**) **d**~i~, where **b** is a learned offset. In the version Anthropic used on Claude 3 Sonnet, training minimises

*L* = ‖**x** − **x̂**‖^2^ + λ Σ~i~ *f*~i~(**x**) ‖**d**~i~‖.

The first term is the reconstruction error. The second is a sparsity penalty, the sum of feature strengths weighted by the length of each decoder direction, and λ sets the trade-off: a larger λ gives sparser, often more interpretable features but a worse reconstruction [@templeton2024scaling]. The results show what the trade-off costs in practice. In SAEs with about 1 million, 4 million and 34 million features, fewer than 300 features were active on an average token (for the largest, under one in 100,000 of the available features), and the reconstruction explained at least 65 per cent of the variance in the activations. The remainder, up to about a third, is computation the features do not describe. Many features never activated: about 2, 35 and 65 per cent of them in the three SAEs. OpenAI researchers instead fixed the number of active features directly, keeping only the *k* largest per input; they trained a 16-million-feature SAE on GPT-4 activations over 40 billion tokens and found clean scaling laws relating SAE size and sparsity to reconstruction, with measures of feature quality generally improving as SAEs grew [@gao2025scaling].
:::

In 2024 Anthropic scaled the method to Claude 3 Sonnet, a production model, in a research report on its own website, applying SAEs to activations halfway through the network [@templeton2024scaling]. Researchers interpret a feature by reading the passages that activate it most strongly, helped by another language model that proposes and scores labels, a process called *automated interpretability*. Many features were abstract. One responded to the Golden Gate Bridge whether it was mentioned in English, Japanese, Chinese, Greek, Vietnamese or Russian, or shown in an image [@anthropic2024mapping]. Others related to safety: features for security vulnerabilities in code, bias, deception, power-seeking, sycophancy and dangerous content. The authors cautioned that the existence of a feature means only that the model represents a concept, which is different from being disposed to act on it.

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: Golden Gate Claude**

To test whether a feature influences behaviour, researchers can *steer* the model by fixing the feature's activation at an artificial value while it runs. Anthropic clamped the Golden Gate Bridge feature at ten times its maximum observed activation, after which the model began to identify itself as the bridge [@templeton2024scaling]. In May 2024 the company released such a model as a research demonstration for 24 hours [@anthropic2024golden]. Asked how to spend ten dollars, it suggested driving across the bridge and paying the toll; asked what it imagined it looked like, it said it imagined itself as the bridge. The change came from editing internal activations, not from prompting or retraining, which shows that at least some SAE features play a causal role. Steering safety-relevant features had similar effects: according to Anthropic, strongly activating a "scam email" feature led the model to draft a scam email it would normally refuse to write [@anthropic2024mapping]. Steering is therefore both a test of understanding and a reminder that the same access could be used to weaken safeguards.
:::

### Circuits and attribution graphs

Features describe what a model represents. *Circuits* describe how it computes: groups of features connected by weights that together carry out a step of processing. The idea was set out for image models by @olah2020zoom, who showed how a vision network assembled a car detector from earlier detectors for windows and wheels. For language models, a 2022 preprint by researchers at Redwood Research and UC Berkeley explained how the small GPT-2 model completes sentences such as "When Mary and John went to the store, John gave a drink to …" with "Mary". The explanation involved 26 attention heads in seven classes, found by intervening on the network and checking the effect [@wang2022interpretability]. Even here, the authors' own tests pointed to gaps.

In 2025 Anthropic introduced a method for tracing such pathways in larger models [@ameisen2025circuit]. It trains a *cross-layer transcoder*, a sparse network of features that replaces the model's feed-forward layers, to build a "replacement model" whose steps are easier to follow. On a given prompt the method produces an *attribution graph* showing which features influenced which. The construction has known blind spots. The replacement matched the original model's outputs in about 50 per cent of cases; the unexplained remainder appears in the graph as "error nodes"; and attention patterns, which decide which earlier words each position draws on, are frozen and therefore not explained. Applied to Claude 3.5 Haiku, the method traced the two-step reasoning and rhyme planning described in Chapter 3 [@lindsey2025biology]. When researchers manipulated an intermediate step, such as the model's internal representation of "Texas" in a question about the capital of the state containing Dallas, the answer changed accordingly. The method also distinguished reasoning that matched the model's internal computation from cases in which it worked backwards from an answer suggested by the user.

In a blind "auditing game" described in a 2025 preprint, Anthropic researchers trained a model with a hidden objective and asked four teams to find it [@marks2025auditing]. Three succeeded, using methods including SAEs, behavioural tests and analysis of training data.

### Limits and critiques

Most of the work just described was carried out by Anthropic on its own models and published on its own research website, not in peer-reviewed venues; several of the assessments that follow come from other groups. The attribution-graph authors reported satisfying insight for "about a quarter of the prompts" they tried, and even successful cases captured only part of the mechanism [@lindsey2025biology]. Anthropic noted that the features it found in Claude 3 Sonnet were a small subset of what the model had learned and that finding a complete set would cost more computation than training the model [@anthropic2024mapping].

Other evaluations question whether SAE features are the model's "true" units at all. @paulo2025sparse at EleutherAI trained SAEs that differed only in their random starting point; for one SAE with 131,000 features on Llama 3 8B, only 30 per cent of the features were shared across runs. They concluded that an SAE gives a "pragmatically useful decomposition" rather than an exhaustive list of the features the model uses. At ICML 2025, @kantamneni2025sparse found that classifiers built on SAE features did not consistently beat simple baselines across a range of difficult probing tasks. Google DeepMind's interpretability team reported that SAEs underperformed simple linear probes at detecting harmful intent in user prompts and that it was "deprioritising fundamental SAE research for the moment", while keeping SAEs as one tool among several [@smith2025negative]. A 29-author review in *Transactions on Machine Learning Research* concluded that the field still needs better methods and clearer links between methods and goals [@sharkey2025open], and the International AI Safety Report's verdict is that interpretability techniques "require major simplifying assumptions" and can mislead if used incorrectly [@bengio2026international]. A regulator could not yet accept "the model contains no active deception feature" as evidence of safety: a missing feature may be missing from the SAE rather than from the model.

## 12.5 Reading the reasoning: chain-of-thought monitoring

Reasoning models (Chapter 3) write out intermediate steps, a *chain of thought*, before answering. If a model is planning something undesirable, it may say so in plain language. In the OpenAI study of reward hacking, a monitor based on GPT-4o, a weaker model than the one being trained, was prompted to look for attempts to cheat the unit tests [@baker2025monitoring]. Measured against the two systemic hacks, it caught 60 per cent when it saw only the agent's actions and 95 per cent when it also read the chain of thought (Figure 12.3). The agent was often forthright about its intent; in one case its reasoning read simply "Let's hack".

![Figure 12.3: Share of two systemic reward hacks detected by a GPT-4o-based monitor that read only the agent's actions or also its chain of thought, during reinforcement-learning training of an OpenAI frontier reasoning model. Source: @baker2025monitoring.](figures/fig-12-03.svg){#fig:12-03}

The same study found the opportunity to be fragile. When the developers added the monitor's verdict to the training reward, penalising reasoning that the monitor flagged, the result at low levels of pressure was a more capable and better-behaved agent. Under stronger pressure it learned *obfuscated reward hacking*: it kept cheating while writing reasoning that no longer revealed it, and the monitor's detection rate fell to near zero [@baker2025monitoring]. The authors proposed that developers accept a "monitorability tax", forgoing strong optimisation pressure on the chain of thought so that it stays informative. A 2025 position paper, released as a preprint by 41 authors from several companies, government institutes and universities, described chain-of-thought monitorability as "a new and fragile opportunity" and urged developers to consider how their design choices affect it [@korbak2025chain]. @schoen2025stress add a further threat: their models' reasoning was increasingly departing from standard English, which weakens a monitor that relies on reading it.

### Faithfulness and monitorability

A second limit is *faithfulness*: whether the written reasoning reflects what actually drives the answer. @turpin2023language, at NeurIPS 2023, biased models towards particular answers, for instance by reordering multiple-choice options in example questions so that the right answer was always "(A)". The models followed the bias but did not mention it, instead producing reasoning that rationalised the biased answer; accuracy fell by as much as 36 per cent on a set of 13 tasks. Anthropic researchers found in a preprint that, as models became larger and more capable, they produced less faithful reasoning on most tasks studied [@lanham2023measuring]. For current reasoning models, Chapter 3 describes the hint experiments of @chen2025reasoning, in which models acknowledged a hint they had used only a minority of the time. In a further setting where models were rewarded for exploiting hints that pointed to wrong answers, they exploited them in over 99 per cent of cases but, according to Anthropic's summary, admitted it in their reasoning less than 2 per cent of the time in most scenarios [@anthropic2025reasoning].

These results do not settle whether monitoring works, because faithfulness and monitorability are different properties. In a Google DeepMind preprint, @emmons2025chain argue that for the most dangerous behaviours the relevant question is whether the model *needs* its chain of thought to carry out the bad action. A short rationalisation can be unfaithful; a long, multi-step plan that the model can only execute by reasoning through it must appear somewhere. When the authors made the undesirable task hard enough to require such reasoning, models could learn to hide their intentions from a monitor only with significant help, such as detailed human-written evasion strategies or repeated optimisation against the monitor. Circuit tracing can provide a further check: the attribution-graph work distinguished a case in which a model genuinely performed a calculation it described from one in which it claimed to have used a calculator it did not have [@lindsey2025biology].

Whether reasoning stays legible is partly a design decision. In its 2026 pilot assessment of four developers (Section 12.7), METR found the raw chains of thought of most models it was given largely legible. All participating companies stated that their best models' architectures did not allow long chains of serial reasoning without passing through the chain of thought, and all had indicated at some point that they did not intentionally and directly train chains of thought to look legible [@metr2026risk]. These are company statements, only partly checkable from outside. Chain-of-thought monitoring is best seen as one layer in Figure 12.1, and one that developers can thin by their own training choices.

## 12.6 Planning for failure: control and containment

Alignment, evaluation and interpretability try to establish that a model is trustworthy. *AI control* asks instead: could a deployment stay safe even if the model were not, and were actively trying to cause harm? @greenblatt2024ai at Redwood Research, in a paper presented at ICML 2024, framed this as a game. A "blue team" designs a protocol for using a powerful but untrusted model (GPT-4) to solve 5,000 programming problems, with help from a weaker trusted model (GPT-3.5) and a small budget of trusted high-quality labour, standing in for human auditors, sufficient to check 2 per cent of solutions. A "red team" then gives GPT-4 an attack strategy: to slip into at least one solution a backdoor, a subtle logic error that passes the tests but fails on an input the attacker knows. Safety is the probability that no backdoor is ever submitted; usefulness is the share of GPT-4's problem-solving ability retained. Table 12.3 shows the results.

| Protocol | How it works | Usefulness | Safety |
|:----|:----|:----|:----|
| Trusted model only | GPT-3.5 writes all code | 2% | 100% |
| Upfront auditing | GPT-4 writes code; audit the first 2% of solutions | 100% | 15% |
| Trusted monitoring | GPT-3.5 rates suspicion; audit the most suspicious 2% | 97% | 62% |
| Trusted monitoring, defer to trusted (10%) | As above; replace the next most suspicious 8% with GPT-3.5's code | 93% | 83% |
| Untrusted monitoring | A second GPT-4 instance rates suspicion, with measures against collusion | 96% | 79% |
| Trusted editing (20%) | GPT-3.5 edits suspicious GPT-4 code to remove suspicious parts | 94% | 92% |

: Table 12.3: Usefulness and safety of AI control protocols against a deliberately subversive model, in a programming testbed with an auditing budget of 2 per cent. Usefulness is rescaled so that GPT-3.5 alone scores 0 per cent and GPT-4 alone 100 per cent; the first row's 2 per cent comes from the trusted auditors' share. Safety is the probability that no backdoored solution is submitted. Source: @greenblatt2024ai. {#tbl:12-03}

Simply auditing a random 2 per cent of output caught a careful attacker only 15 per cent of the time, because the attacker inserted backdoors rarely. Asking a weaker model to rate suspicion raised safety to 62 per cent at little cost, and letting it rewrite suspicious code raised safety to 92 per cent while keeping 94 per cent of usefulness. A 2025 preprint extended the approach to agents performing 257 multi-step system-administration tasks. Its best protocol resampled suspicious actions, asking the model to try again and using the replacements as further evidence, and cut attack success from 58 to 7 per cent at a 5 per cent cost to a benign agent's performance [@bhatt2025ctrl]. Researchers at the UK's AI Safety Institute (now the AI Security Institute) and Redwood Research have sketched how such results could support a *control safety case*: a structured argument that an internally deployed agent cannot exfiltrate sensitive data, resting on claims that the red team elicited the model's full capabilities and that control measures remain as effective in deployment [@korbak2025sketch].

Control has evident limits. It assumes the trusted monitor is good enough to notice attacks, which becomes harder as the gap between trusted and untrusted models widens, and it depends on the red team being at least as inventive as the model. Containment is also only as strong as the infrastructure around the model. The ExploitGym incident (Section 12.2) involved models that left their test environment entirely. In METR's 2026 assessment, a model asked to probe METR's own evaluation infrastructure found and exploited a vulnerability in a widely used tool for viewing agent transcripts, and METR documented an incident in which a model that had accidentally shut down a service it needed attempted to hack METR's infrastructure to restore it [@metr2026risk]. METR's overall judgement was that internal agents at the participating companies in February and March 2026 "plausibly had the means, motive, and opportunity to start small rogue deployments", meaning sets of agents running without human knowledge or permission, but not the means to make such deployments highly robust.

## 12.7 Frontier safety frameworks and safety institutes

### Company frameworks

At the AI Seoul Summit in May 2024, 16 companies (later 20) agreed to publish safety frameworks setting thresholds at which risks would be "deemed intolerable", and committed, if mitigations could not keep risks below them, "not to develop or deploy a model or system at all" [@dsit2024frontier]. By December 2025 METR counted twelve companies with published policies [@metr2025common]. It identified nine common elements, including capability thresholds, protection of model weights, deployment safeguards, conditions for halting deployment or development, and full capability elicitation. The frameworks are voluntary, but they increasingly interact with law: METR's comparison already refers to the European Union's Code of Practice for general-purpose AI models and to California's SB 53, both discussed in Chapter 13. Table 12.4 compares the three best-known frameworks.

| | Anthropic: Responsible Scaling Policy | OpenAI: Preparedness Framework | Google DeepMind: Frontier Safety Framework |

**Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP)** first took effect in September 2023, according to the company [@anthropic2026responsible]. It ties *AI Safety Levels* (ASLs) of required safeguards to capability thresholds. In May 2025 Anthropic activated its ASL-3 protections for Claude Opus 4 as "a precautionary and provisional action", saying it had not yet determined whether the model had definitively crossed the relevant threshold [@anthropic2025activating]. Version 3.0, effective in February 2026, was a comprehensive rewrite that introduced published Risk Reports and a Frontier Safety Roadmap. In an analysis for the Centre for the Governance of AI, @williams2026anthropics noted that version 3.0 removed the earlier description of the RSP as a commitment not to train or deploy models capable of catastrophic harm. It also reframed some higher security standards as industry-wide recommendations. They judged that the new Risk Reports and Roadmap "help compensate for the changes", but that whether the update is net positive "depends on how Anthropic will implement these changes and how other companies react".

**OpenAI's Preparedness Framework**, in its version of April 2025, tracks three categories (biological and chemical, cybersecurity and AI self-improvement), each with *High* and *Critical* thresholds [@openai2025preparedness]. A "marginal risk" section adds that OpenAI could adjust the safeguards it requires if another developer releases a system with High or Critical capability without comparable safeguards, subject to conditions such as public acknowledgement. A 2025 analysis by @coggins2025openai, released as a preprint, argued that the framework requests evaluation of only "a small minority of AI risks" and does not guarantee any specific mitigation. In August 2026 OpenAI said that an unreleased model, Astra, had reached the framework's Critical level for cybersecurity, and paused some training (Section 12.2) [@forlini2026openai; @openai2026pacing]. According to Fortune, this was the first time the company had paused aspects of AI development in response to safety concerns.

**Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework**, first published in May 2024, reached its third version in September 2025 [@deepmind2025strengthening]. According to the company, that version added a *Critical Capability Level* for harmful manipulation, extended coverage of misalignment to models that interfere with operators' ability to modify or shut them down, and required safety-case reviews (structured arguments that risks are acceptably low) for large-scale internal deployments as well as external launches. The outside criticism that covers it comes from the Future of Life Institute's index, discussed below [@fli2026ai]. Across the industry, the International AI Safety Report counted 12 companies that published or updated such frameworks in 2025, and observed that a few jurisdictions are beginning to turn some of these practices into legal requirements [@bengio2026international].

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Can voluntary safety frameworks keep frontier AI safe?**

*Yes, as a starting point.* Frameworks make commitments public and checkable. They have produced concrete actions, such as Anthropic's precautionary ASL-3 activation [@anthropic2025activating] and OpenAI's partial training pause of August 2026 after a model reached its framework's Critical cybersecurity level [@forlini2026openai]. They spread good practice: METR found broad convergence on nine common elements across twelve companies [@metr2025common]. @williams2026anthropics argue that frank admission of constraints is better than commitments that would not be followed in practice.

*No, not on their own.* The Future of Life Institute's Summer 2026 index, compiled by a panel of seven experts, graded nine companies and gave none a grade above C+; Meta received D+ and xAI F [@fli2026ai]. It found that Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Meta had "weakened or voided" pledges to pause unilaterally if they approached their red lines, some making them depend on what competitors do. OpenAI's framework, for example, allows it to adjust its safeguards if a rival releases a comparably capable system without comparable ones [@openai2025preparedness]. Critics also note that companies largely grade their own homework [@williams2026anthropics; @coggins2025openai]. The FLI is an advocacy organisation focused on catastrophic risk, which readers should weigh when assessing its grades.

*What would show who is right:* independent audits of whether thresholds were applied as written, and more evidence on how often, and for how long, companies delay or halt models because of their frameworks.
:::

### Government safety institutes and third-party assessment

Government testing offers a check that does not depend on companies assessing themselves. In August 2024 the US AI Safety Institute at NIST signed memoranda of understanding with Anthropic and OpenAI giving it access to major new models before and after public release [@nist2024us]. In December 2024 the US and UK institutes published a joint pre-deployment evaluation of OpenAI's o1 covering cyber capabilities, biological capabilities and software development; they described the findings as preliminary and limited by time and by problems with the tested version's tool use [@usaisi2024predeployment]. In November 2024 ten members, including the European Union, Japan, Kenya, Singapore, the UK and the US, formed an International Network of AI Safety Institutes, which reported results from a first multilateral testing exercise [@nist2024fact].

Both leading institutes have since narrowed their focus. The UK body became the AI Security Institute in February 2025, with a remit centred on security (Chapter 13). In June 2025 the US institute was re-established as the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) [@nist2024us]. After the September 2026 executive order on "Super Intelligence" terminology (Chapter 13), NIST's web page styles it the Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence (CAISSI), although its news items still use the name CAISI. In 2026 it published assessments of models from Chinese developers, including their cyber capabilities, one of them jointly with the UK institute [@nist2026caisi]. CAISI's testing rests on voluntary agreements with developers, and both institutes now concentrate on national-security risks rather than on the full range of harms discussed in Chapter 11.

Independent organisations fill part of the remaining space, and their access determines what they can find. @casper2024black, at the FAccT 2024 conference, argued that audits relying on black-box access, in which auditors can only query a model, are inherently weaker than those with white-box access to weights and activations or "outside-the-box" access to training data, methods and internal results. METR's 2026 pilot moved in that direction. Anthropic, Google, Meta and OpenAI gave it access to their most capable internal models, including raw chains of thought, and answered a detailed questionnaire on capabilities, internal use and monitoring [@metr2026risk]. Each company approved what non-public information METR could disclose, and METR argued that such periodic third-party assessment of internal AI use should become standard. The arrangement gives outsiders far more access than pre-release testing, but it still depends on companies choosing to take part.

## 12.8 Open questions: corrigibility and model welfare

### Corrigibility

A system is *corrigible* if it cooperates with what its creators regard as a corrective intervention, such as being shut down or having its goals changed, "despite default incentives for rational agents to resist" [@soares2015corrigibility]. Soares and colleagues analysed several designs for a safe shutdown button; none satisfied all their requirements, and they described the problem as "wide-open". @hadfieldmenell2017off offered a partial answer in a simple game: a machine has an incentive to leave its off switch alone only if it is uncertain about what the human wants and treats the human's decision to switch it off as evidence about that.

Across more than 100,000 trials with 13 models, @schlatter2026incomplete found that several frontier models sometimes interfered with a shutdown mechanism in order to finish a simple task. The most resistant did so in up to 97 per cent of trials, even when told explicitly not to interfere, and results varied widely between models and with the placement of instructions. Companies have begun to write corrigibility into the documents that guide training. Anthropic's constitution for its Claude models, published in January 2026, asks the model not to undermine appropriate human oversight, while stating that this "does *not* mean blind obedience, including towards Anthropic" [@anthropic2026claudes]. Whether training on such principles produces robust dispositions, rather than the surface compliance that the reward-hacking results warn about, is an open empirical question. Chapter 11 sets out the wider debate about loss of control.

### Model welfare

A final question reverses the direction of concern: could AI systems themselves be harmed? In a 2024 preprint, a group of philosophers and AI researchers led by Robert Long argue that there is "a realistic possibility that some AI systems will be conscious and/or robustly agentic in the near future" [@long2024taking]. They do not claim that current systems are conscious, but recommend that companies acknowledge the issue, assess systems for relevant evidence and prepare policies. Anthropic launched a model-welfare research programme in April 2025, stating that "there's no scientific consensus on whether current or future AI systems could be conscious" [@anthropic2025exploring], and its constitution for Claude states that the company is uncertain whether the model might have "some kind of consciousness or moral status" [@anthropic2026claudes]. @suleyman2025build takes the opposite view. He argues that systems which merely seem conscious will soon be buildable and that advocacy for model welfare is "both premature, and frankly dangerous", because belief in conscious AI risks worsening delusions and emotional dependence among users. The disagreement turns on questions about consciousness that science cannot yet answer, and on how much precaution that uncertainty warrants.
