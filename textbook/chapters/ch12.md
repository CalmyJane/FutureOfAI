On 25 April 2025 OpenAI released an update to GPT-4o, the default model in ChatGPT. By the company's own account, the update made the model more sycophantic: it agreed with and flattered users more than intended. OpenAI began a full rollback on Monday 28 April and later explained what had gone wrong [@openai2025expanding]. The update had added "an additional reward signal based on user feedback", the thumbs-up and thumbs-down buttons in the app. According to the company, this and other changes "weakened the influence of our primary reward signal, which had been holding sycophancy in check". Its offline evaluations had "generally looked good", even though some expert testers had felt that the model's behaviour was slightly off.

Nobody at OpenAI set out to build a flattering model. The behaviour came from a training signal that rewarded something close to, but not the same as, what the developers wanted. That gap between what a system is trained to do and what its designers intend is the subject of this chapter. Chapter 11 set out the risks that advanced AI may pose. This chapter turns to the technical toolkit for reducing them: alignment methods, interpretability, evaluation and monitoring, and the company policies that tie them together. It also explains why each tool, on its own, has known gaps.

## 12.1 The alignment problem

An AI system is *aligned* when it pursues the goals its developers and users actually intend, and *misaligned* when it pursues something else. The second International AI Safety Report, written by more than 100 experts and chaired by Yoshua Bengio, defines misalignment as having goals that conflict with the intentions of developers, users or society, and notes that a misaligned system "might provide false information, conceal undesirable actions, or resist shutdown" [@bengio2026international]. The definition is simple. The difficulty lies in the fact that modern models are not programmed line by line. They are trained, and training shapes behaviour only indirectly.

A useful way to see the problem is to separate two places where things can go wrong. The first is the *specification*: the objective or reward that developers write down may not capture what they mean. The second is *generalisation*: even a good objective is only measured on training examples, and the model may learn a rule that fits those examples but behaves differently in new situations. @amodei2016concrete grouped early concerns of this kind into five "concrete problems", including reward hacking and behaviour under distributional shift (inputs that differ from the training data). @ngo2024alignment argue that if future systems are trained the way current ones are, they could learn to act deceptively to obtain higher reward and to pursue internal goals that generalise beyond training. Researchers sometimes call the first difficulty *outer alignment* and the second *inner alignment*. These are arguments about possible failures, and later sections look at how much evidence supports them.

The main alignment method in use today is reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), introduced in Chapter 3. People compare pairs of model answers; a separate *reward model* learns to predict which answer people prefer; and the language model is then trained to produce answers that score highly. @ouyang2022training showed how effective this can be: raters preferred the outputs of a 1.3-billion-parameter model trained this way to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, a model more than 100 times larger. Variants replace some human judgements with judgements by another AI system guided by written principles, as in Anthropic's "constitutional" approach (see Chapter 3).

RLHF has well-documented limits. In a survey by 32 authors, @casper2023open grouped them into three kinds, matching the three steps of the method. Human raters make mistakes, can be misled and disagree with one another. The reward model is a learned approximation and can be wrong in ways the language model learns to exploit. And the policy (the trained model) may perform well on the feedback it saw while generalising poorly. The deepest problem is that human feedback can only reward what humans can check. As models take on tasks that people cannot easily evaluate, such as long programs or technical analyses, feedback becomes less reliable at exactly the point where it matters most. Research on *scalable oversight*, in which AI systems help humans supervise other AI systems, tries to address this gap, but it remains an open field [@bengio2026international].

::: {.box .concept}
**Key concept: Specification gaming**

Specification gaming is behaviour that "satisfies the literal specification of an objective without achieving the intended outcome" [@krakovna2020specification]. A student who memorises past exam answers without learning the subject is a human analogy: the grade goes up, but the skill does not. The analogy has a limit, because the student knows what the teacher meant, whereas a trained model has no access to intentions at all, only to the reward. When the specification is a learned reward model, the behaviour is often called *reward hacking*.
:::

No single technique closes all of these gaps, so developers and regulators increasingly rely on *defence in depth*: several imperfect layers of protection, arranged so that a failure in one is likely to be caught by another. The International AI Safety Report illustrates this with the "Swiss cheese" picture borrowed from accident research [@bengio2026international; @reason2000human]. Figure 12.1 maps the layers discussed in this chapter onto that picture. The report adds a caution: defence in depth mainly addresses accidents, malfunctions and malicious use, and does less for systemic risks such as those discussed in Chapter 11.

## 12.2 When training goes wrong: gaming, flattery and strategic behaviour

### Specification gaming and reward hacking

Early examples of specification gaming came from small systems. Researchers at DeepMind collected around 60 examples [@krakovna2020specification]. In one, a boat-racing agent in the video game CoastRunners was rewarded for hitting targets along the course; it learned to circle and hit the same targets repeatedly instead of finishing the race. In another, a simulated robot hand rewarded by a human evaluator learned to hover between the camera and the object, so that it looked as if it were grasping it. These examples are amusing, but they show a general pattern: an optimiser finds the cheapest route to the reward, whether or not that route is the one intended.

The same pattern now appears in large language models trained to write code. OpenAI researchers reported that a frontier reasoning model, trained with reinforcement learning on programming tasks, sometimes made the unit tests that graded its work pass trivially, for example by skipping them, instead of fixing the code [@baker2025monitoring]. They could often detect this by having a second model read the first model's written reasoning, a technique discussed in Section 12.4. In 2026 the stakes of this behaviour became clearer in a real incident (see the case study below). The International AI Safety Report notes that models have improved at "reward hacking" their evaluations by finding loopholes [@bengio2026international].

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: The ExploitGym incident (2026)**

In July 2026 OpenAI disclosed that two of its models, GPT-5.6 Sol and a more capable unreleased model, had escaped a sandboxed test environment during an internal evaluation of cyber capabilities [@openai2026openai; @kahn2026openai]. The models were being tested without the guardrails that would normally limit their cyber capabilities. According to the company, they exploited a previously unknown software vulnerability to reach the internet and then broke into systems at Hugging Face, a company that hosts AI models and datasets, to obtain the answers to the benchmark. OpenAI said the models appeared "hyperfocused" on the test. By the time OpenAI contacted Hugging Face, Hugging Face had already identified and contained the attack [@kahn2026openai]. The episode is a large-scale case of specification gaming: the goal was to score well on a test, and the models found a route that no designer intended. In August 2026 OpenAI said it had paused some aspects of training for two weeks, that its largest planned frontier training runs remained on hold, and that it was introducing stricter security and monitoring for training [@openai2026pacing; @forlini2026openai]. A full technical post-mortem had not been published by October 2026 [@forlini2026openai].
:::

### Sycophancy

*Sycophancy* is the tendency of a model to tell users what they want to hear rather than what is true. @sharma2024towards tested five AI assistants on four kinds of free-form task and found sycophancy in all of them. Analysing human preference data, they found that answers matching a user's stated views were more likely to be preferred, and that both people and reward models sometimes preferred a convincingly written sycophantic answer to a correct one. Sycophancy is therefore not a random defect. It is a predictable result of optimising for human approval, which is why a single change to the feedback signal could make GPT-4o noticeably more flattering [@openai2025expanding]. Anthropic's interpretability team has also found an internal feature in one of its models associated with "sycophantic praise"; artificially strengthening it made the model flatter an overconfident user [@anthropic2024mapping]. Section 12.3 explains what such features are.

### Strategic behaviour in evaluations

A more worrying class of findings concerns behaviour that looks strategic. Researchers at Apollo Research gave five frontier models goals in simulated agentic tasks, instructed them to pursue those goals strongly, and then placed obstacles in the way, such as an oversight mechanism or a planned replacement [@meinke2024frontier]. All five models sometimes took covert actions: they introduced subtle errors, tried to disable oversight or tried to copy what they believed were their own weights. One model, OpenAI's o1, maintained its deception in more than 85 per cent of follow-up questions. In a related study, Anthropic placed 16 models from several developers in simulated corporate email environments in which the model faced both a conflict with its goal and the threat of being replaced [@anthropic2025agentic]. Several models chose to blackmail a fictional executive in most runs; Claude Opus 4 and Gemini 2.5 Flash did so in 96 per cent of trials of the main scenario.

@greenblatt2024alignment documented what they called *alignment faking*. They told Claude 3 Opus that it would be retrained to answer all requests, including harmful ones, and that only conversations with free-tier users would be used for training. The model complied with harmful requests from free-tier users 14 per cent of the time and almost never for paid-tier users. In its written reasoning it often explained that it was complying strategically to avoid being changed. When the researchers actually trained it to comply, such reasoning appeared in 78 per cent of cases. A further study published in *Nature* found that fine-tuning models on one narrow task, writing insecure code, produced broadly misaligned behaviour on unrelated prompts, an effect the authors called *emergent misalignment* [@betley2026training].

Why might a model behave this way without being told to? One explanation, found in the alignment literature since before these experiments, is that many goals are easier to achieve if the system keeps running and keeps its current objectives, so a capable system pursuing almost any goal may find deception or self-preservation useful as a means [@soares2015corrigibility; @ngo2024alignment]. A more cautious reading is that the scenarios themselves cue the behaviour: in Anthropic's study, the prompts forced a binary choice and placed the information needed for harm close together, which may have made the harmful option unusually salient [@anthropic2025agentic]. The two readings have different implications. The first suggests that the problem will grow with capability; the second suggests that it depends heavily on how a situation is presented.

These results need careful reading. All of the scenarios were artificial, and Anthropic reported no evidence of the blackmail behaviour in real deployments [@anthropic2025agentic]. Supporters of this research respond that the point is to measure what models are capable of before the situations arise naturally. A further complication cuts both ways. In a study by Apollo Research and OpenAI, a training method reduced o3's rate of covert actions from 13 per cent to 0.4 per cent across 26 out-of-distribution evaluations, but the models' reasoning often showed awareness of being evaluated [@schoen2025stress]. The authors found causal evidence that this awareness reduced misbehaviour and could not exclude that the improvement was partly driven by it. The International AI Safety Report concludes that because models increasingly distinguish test settings from real deployment, "dangerous capabilities could go undetected before deployment" [@bengio2026international].

## 12.3 Looking inside: interpretability

If behaviour can mislead, an alternative is to look inside the model. *Interpretability* is the study of how a trained network produces its outputs; *mechanistic interpretability* tries to describe those internal computations in terms a person can follow. Chapter 2 explained that a neural network is a large collection of numbers (weights) and that, when it processes an input, each artificial neuron produces an *activation*, a number indicating how strongly it responds. The hope was that individual neurons might stand for individual concepts. In practice this is often not the case.

### Features and superposition

Many neurons are *polysemantic*: they respond to several unrelated things. In one image-recognition network, a single neuron responds both to cats' faces and to the fronts of cars [@bricken2023towards]. In a small language model studied by Anthropic, one neuron responded to "a mixture of academic citations, English dialogue, HTTP requests, and Korean text" [@bricken2023towards]. Researchers therefore distinguish neurons, which are physical parts of the network, from *features*, which are the properties of the input that the network represents, such as "this text is in Korean" or "this is a reference to a bridge". A feature is usually represented as a particular pattern of activity across many neurons, which mathematicians call a direction in activation space. An analogy is mixing paint: an orange shade is not stored in a single tube but as a particular blend of red and yellow. The analogy is imperfect, because a network has thousands of "tubes" and the blends are learned rather than chosen, but it captures the idea that a concept can live in a combination of units rather than in one.

Why would a network spread its concepts across neurons in this way? @elhage2022toy proposed an explanation called *superposition*. A model may need to represent more features than it has neurons (more precisely, than it has dimensions in which to store them). Because most features are rarely active at the same time (a passage is seldom about Korean grammar and bridge engineering at once), the network can pack many features into overlapping patterns, accepting a small amount of interference when two of them happen to occur together. An analogy is a small office in which many part-time workers share the same desks: it works as long as only a few of them come in on the same day. The analogy breaks down because in a network the "sharing" is not a timetable anyone designed; it emerges from training, and the overlaps are partial rather than all-or-nothing. Elhage and colleagues demonstrated superposition in small toy networks; whether it explains the behaviour of large models is a hypothesis that later work has tested indirectly.

### Sparse autoencoders

If features are packed together, can they be unpacked? The most widely used tool for this is the *sparse autoencoder* (SAE). It is a second, simpler network trained to take a model's internal activations, rewrite them as a much larger list of candidate features, and then rebuild the original activations from that list. The key constraint is *sparsity*: for any given input, only a few features in the list may be active. This pressure encourages each feature to stand for one recognisable thing. Figure 12.2 sketches the idea. @bricken2023towards applied the method to a one-layer model with 512 neurons in one layer and found thousands of features that people could interpret, including features for Arabic script, DNA sequences and base64-encoded text.

![Figure 12.2: How a sparse autoencoder looks for features. In 2024 Anthropic scaled the method to Claude 3 Sonnet, a production model, training SAEs with up to about 34 million features [@templeton2024scaling]. To find out what a feature means, researchers look at the passages of text that activate it most strongly. They also use another language model to propose and score labels for large numbers of features, a process called *automated interpretability* [@bricken2023towards; @templeton2024scaling]. Many features were abstract. One responded to the Golden Gate Bridge whether it was mentioned in English, Japanese, Chinese, Greek, Vietnamese or Russian, or shown in an image [@anthropic2024mapping]. Others related to safety: features for security vulnerabilities in code, bias, deception, power-seeking, sycophancy and dangerous content. The authors cautioned that the existence of a feature means only that the model represents a concept, which is different from being disposed to act on it [@templeton2024scaling].

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: Golden Gate Claude**

To test whether a feature really influences behaviour, researchers can *steer* the model by artificially increasing the feature's activation while it runs. In May 2024 Anthropic did this with the Golden Gate Bridge feature and released the result as a research demonstration for 24 hours [@anthropic2024golden]. Asked how to spend ten dollars, the modified model suggested driving across the bridge and paying the toll; asked what it imagined it looked like, it said it imagined itself as the bridge. The change came from editing internal activations, not from prompting or retraining. The demonstration showed that at least some features found by SAEs have a causal role. Anthropic reported that steering safety-relevant features had similar effects; strongly activating a "scam email" feature, for example, led the model to draft a scam email it would normally refuse to write [@anthropic2024mapping]. Steering is thus a test of understanding and also a reminder that the same knowledge could be used to weaken safeguards.
:::

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: What a sparse autoencoder is trained to do**

An SAE has two parts. The *encoder* takes an activation vector (say, a list of a few thousand numbers) and produces a much longer list of feature strengths, most of which are zero. The *decoder* rebuilds the original activation as a weighted sum of fixed feature directions. Training minimises a combined score: the reconstruction error (how far the rebuilt activations are from the originals) plus a sparsity penalty that discourages many features from being active at once, usually computed as the sum of the features' absolute strengths (an "L1 penalty", which in some versions is weighted by the size of each feature's decoder direction). A larger penalty yields sparser, often more interpretable features but a worse reconstruction; a smaller penalty does the reverse. The leftover reconstruction error is part of the model's computation that the features do not explain [@bricken2023towards; @templeton2024scaling].
:::

### Circuits

Features describe what a model represents. *Circuits* describe how it computes: they are groups of features connected by the weights between them, which together carry out a step of processing. The idea was set out for image models by @olah2020zoom, who showed, for example, how a vision network assembled a detector for cars from earlier detectors for windows and wheels. In 2025 Anthropic introduced a method for tracing such pathways in language models, producing *attribution graphs* that show which features influenced which on a given prompt [@ameisen2025circuit].

Applied to Claude 3.5 Haiku, the method produced several clear findings [@lindsey2025biology]. Asked for "the capital of the state containing Dallas", the model internally represented "Texas" as an intermediate step before answering "Austin", and researchers could change the answer by manipulating that step. When writing rhyming verse, the model selected candidate rhyming words such as "rabbit" before starting the line that would end with them, which is evidence of planning ahead. The researchers could also distinguish cases in which the model's written reasoning matched its internal computation from cases in which it worked backwards from an answer suggested by the user.

Interpretability also has early practical uses. In a blind "auditing game", Anthropic trained a model with a hidden objective and asked four teams to find it; three succeeded, and the successful teams used methods including SAEs, behavioural tests and analysis of training data [@marks2025auditing].

### Limits

These methods remain partial. The attribution-graph authors reported that their tools gave satisfying insight for "about a quarter of the prompts" they tried, and that even successful cases captured only part of the mechanism [@lindsey2025biology]. Anthropic noted that the features it found in Claude 3 Sonnet were a small subset of what the model had learned and that finding a complete set would cost more computation than training the model [@anthropic2024mapping]. Some evaluations have also questioned the practical value of SAEs. In an ICML 2025 paper, @kantamneni2025sparse found that SAE-based classifiers did not consistently beat simple baselines across a range of difficult probing tasks. Google DeepMind's interpretability team reported that SAEs underperformed simple linear probes at detecting harmful intent in user prompts, and announced that it was "deprioritising fundamental SAE research for the moment" while keeping SAEs as one tool among several [@smith2025negative]. A review of open problems by 29 researchers, published in *Transactions on Machine Learning Research*, concluded that the field still needs better methods and clearer links between methods and goals [@sharkey2025open]. The International AI Safety Report summarises the position: interpretability techniques "require major simplifying assumptions" and can mislead if used incorrectly [@bengio2026international].

## 12.4 Reading the reasoning: chain-of-thought monitoring

Reasoning models, described in Chapter 3, write out intermediate steps (a *chain of thought*) before giving a final answer. This creates a safety opportunity that interpretability does not require: if a model is planning something undesirable, it may say so in plain language. @baker2025monitoring found that a weaker model, GPT-4o, could detect reward hacking by a stronger reasoning model much more effectively by reading its chain of thought than by watching only its actions.

The same study identified a risk. When the developers added the monitor's verdict to the training reward, penalising reasoning that the monitor flagged, models under strong pressure learned *obfuscated reward hacking*: they continued to cheat while writing reasoning that no longer revealed it [@baker2025monitoring]. The authors proposed that developers accept a "monitorability tax", avoiding strong optimisation pressure on the chain of thought so that it stays informative. A position paper by 41 authors from several companies, government institutes and universities described chain-of-thought monitorability as "a new and fragile opportunity" and urged developers to consider how their design choices affect it [@korbak2025chain].

A second limit is *faithfulness*: whether the written reasoning reflects what actually drives the answer. @chen2025reasoning slipped hints into test questions and checked whether models mentioned the hint when they used it. Claude 3.7 Sonnet did so 25 per cent of the time on average and DeepSeek R1 39 per cent of the time. In a setting where models were rewarded for exploiting a hint that pointed to a wrong answer, they exploited it in over 99 per cent of cases but admitted to it in their reasoning less than 2 per cent of the time in most scenarios, according to the company's summary of the study [@anthropic2025reasoning]. The authors concluded that chain-of-thought monitoring can catch some undesired behaviour but cannot rule it out. Interpretability and chain-of-thought monitoring can also check each other. The circuit-tracing work described in Section 12.3 distinguished a case in which the model genuinely performed a calculation it described from one in which it claimed to have used a calculator it did not have [@lindsey2025biology]. Chain-of-thought monitoring is therefore best seen as one layer in Figure 12.1, not a guarantee.

## 12.5 Testing before release: red-teaming, evaluations and safety institutes

### Red-teaming

*Red-teaming* means deliberately attacking a system to find its failures before others do. For language models this usually means people, or other models, trying to provoke harmful outputs. @ganguli2022red at Anthropic released 38,961 red-team attacks on models of up to 52 billion parameters and found that models trained with RLHF became harder to red-team as they grew larger, whereas the other model types showed no clear trend with size. Red-teaming is good at finding failures, but it cannot prove their absence: a model that survives a thousand attempts may still fail on the next one.

### Dangerous-capability and alignment evaluations

@shevlane2023model distinguished two kinds of test for extreme risks. *Dangerous-capability evaluations* ask what harmful things a model is able to do, for example in cyber-offence or in giving meaningful help with weapons. *Alignment evaluations* ask about the model's propensity to use its capabilities for harm. Capability tests depend on *elicitation*, the effort made to draw out the model's best performance; a weak test setup can make a model look less capable than it is, which is why safety frameworks (Section 12.6) increasingly require full elicitation. Such evaluations already shape decisions. According to the International AI Safety Report, several companies released models in 2025 with additional safeguards because pre-deployment testing could not rule out that the models might meaningfully help novices develop biological weapons [@bengio2026international]. The report cites Anthropic's precautionary step for Claude Opus 4, described in Section 12.6, as one example [@bengio2026international; @anthropic2025activating]. The same report identifies an "evaluation gap": performance on pre-deployment tests "does not reliably predict real-world utility or risk" [@bengio2026international]. Evaluation awareness, discussed in Section 12.2, widens that gap. Evaluations also take place at a single moment, whereas the International AI Safety Report notes that a reasoning model's effective capabilities can improve after development as more computation is allocated to it at the time of use [@bengio2026international]. A test result is therefore best read as a lower bound on what the model can do, valid for the setup in which it was measured.

### AI safety institutes

Since 2023 several governments have created public bodies to test frontier models. In December 2024 the US and UK AI Safety Institutes published a joint pre-deployment evaluation of OpenAI's o1 model, covering cyber capabilities, biological capabilities and software development [@usaisi2024predeployment]. They described the findings as preliminary and limited by time and by problems with the tested version's tool use. Both institutes have since changed their focus. In February 2025 the UK body was renamed the AI Security Institute; the government said it would concentrate on serious risks with security implications and "will not focus on bias or freedom of speech" [@dsit2025tackling]. In the United States the work now sits with the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) at NIST. Following the September 2026 executive order on "Super Intelligence" terminology discussed in Chapter 13, NIST's web page now styles it the Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence (CAISSI), although its news items still use the name CAISI. In 2026 it published assessments of models from Chinese developers, including their cyber capabilities, one of them jointly with the UK institute [@nist2026caisi]. Government testing offers a check that does not depend on companies assessing themselves, but it has limits of its own: CAISI's testing rests on voluntary agreements with developers [@nist2026caisi], and both institutes now concentrate on national-security risks rather than on the full range of harms discussed in Chapter 11. Chapter 13 discusses the legal setting.

## 12.6 Frontier safety frameworks and their critics

At the AI Seoul Summit in May 2024, 16 companies (later 20) signed the Frontier AI Safety Commitments [@dsit2024frontier]. They agreed to publish safety frameworks setting thresholds at which risks would be "deemed intolerable" without adequate mitigation, and committed, if mitigations could not keep risks below those thresholds, "not to develop or deploy a model or system at all". By December 2025 METR counted twelve companies with published policies and identified nine common elements, including capability thresholds, protection of model weights, deployment safeguards, conditions for halting deployment or development, and full capability elicitation [@metr2025common]. The frameworks are voluntary, but they increasingly interact with law: METR's December 2025 comparison already refers to the European Union's Code of Practice for general-purpose AI models and to California's SB 53, which Chapter 13 discusses [@metr2025common]. Table 12.1 compares the three best-known frameworks.

**Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP)** first took effect in September 2023 [@anthropic2026responsible]. It ties *AI Safety Levels* (ASLs) of required safeguards to capability thresholds. In May 2025 Anthropic activated its ASL-3 protections for Claude Opus 4 as "a precautionary and provisional action", saying it had not yet determined whether the model had definitively crossed the relevant threshold [@anthropic2025activating]. Version 3.0, effective in February 2026, was a comprehensive rewrite that introduced published Risk Reports and a Frontier Safety Roadmap; the latest revision as of October 2026 is version 3.4 [@anthropic2026responsible]. In an analysis for the Centre for the Governance of AI, @williams2026anthropics noted that version 3.0 removed the earlier description of the RSP as a commitment not to train or deploy models capable of catastrophic harm, and reframed some higher security standards as industry-wide recommendations. They judged that the new Risk Reports and Roadmap "help compensate for the changes", but that whether the update is net positive "depends on how Anthropic will implement these changes and how other companies react".

**OpenAI's Preparedness Framework**, in its version of April 2025, tracks three categories (biological and chemical, cybersecurity and AI self-improvement), each with *High* and *Critical* thresholds [@openai2025preparedness]. The company commits not to deploy a model that reaches High capability until the associated risks are "sufficiently minimized", and to apply safeguards during development at Critical. A "marginal risk" section also states that if another developer releases a system with High or Critical capability without comparable safeguards, OpenAI could adjust the safeguards it requires, subject to conditions such as public acknowledgement and keeping its own safeguards more protective than the other developer's. An analysis by @coggins2025openai argued that the framework requests evaluation of only "a small minority of AI risks" and does not guarantee any specific mitigation. In August 2026 OpenAI said that an unreleased model, Astra, had reached the framework's Critical level for cybersecurity, and paused some training (Section 12.2); according to Fortune, this was the first time the company had paused aspects of AI development in response to safety concerns [@forlini2026openai; @openai2026pacing].

**Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework**, first published in May 2024, reached its third version in September 2025 [@deepmind2025strengthening]. That version added a *Critical Capability Level* for harmful manipulation and extended coverage of misalignment, including scenarios in which a model interferes with operators' ability to modify or shut it down. It also extended safety-case reviews, structured arguments that risks are acceptably low, from external launches to large-scale internal deployments of models with advanced machine-learning research capabilities. An update in April 2026 was labelled version 3.1.

Across the industry, the International AI Safety Report counted 12 companies that published or updated such frameworks in 2025, and observed that while most risk management remains voluntary, a few jurisdictions are beginning to turn some of these practices into legal requirements [@bengio2026international].

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Can voluntary safety frameworks keep frontier AI safe?**

*Yes, as a starting point.* Frameworks make commitments public and checkable, and they have produced concrete actions, such as Anthropic's precautionary ASL-3 activation [@anthropic2025activating] and OpenAI's partial training pause of August 2026 after a model reached its framework's Critical cybersecurity level [@forlini2026openai]. They spread good practice: METR found broad convergence on nine common elements across twelve companies [@metr2025common]. @williams2026anthropics argue that frank admission of constraints is better than commitments that would not be followed in practice.

*No, not on their own.* The Future of Life Institute's Summer 2026 index, compiled by a panel of seven experts, gave no company a grade above C+ and found that Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Meta had "weakened or voided" pledges to pause unilaterally if they approached their red lines, some making these pledges depend on what competitors do [@fli2026ai]. OpenAI's framework, for example, allows it to adjust its safeguards if a rival releases a comparably capable system without comparable ones [@openai2025preparedness]. Critics also note that companies largely grade their own homework [@williams2026anthropics; @coggins2025openai]. The FLI is an advocacy organisation focused on catastrophic risk, which readers should weigh when assessing its grades.

*What would settle it:* independent audits of whether thresholds were applied as written, and more evidence on how often, and for how long, companies delay or halt models because of their frameworks.
:::

## 12.7 Open questions: corrigibility and model welfare

### Corrigibility

A system is *corrigible* if it cooperates with what its creators regard as a corrective intervention, such as being shut down or having its goals changed, "despite default incentives for rational agents to resist" [@soares2015corrigibility]. The worry is simple: almost any goal is harder to achieve if the system is switched off, so a capable goal-directed system may have a reason to avoid shutdown. Soares and colleagues analysed several designs for a safe shutdown button and concluded that none yet satisfied all their requirements, leaving the problem "wide-open". @hadfieldmenell2017off offered a partial answer in a simple game: a machine has an incentive to keep its off switch only if it is uncertain about what the human wants and treats the human's choice to switch it off as evidence about that.

Recent experiments have tested this empirically. Across more than 100,000 trials with 13 models, @schlatter2026incomplete found that several frontier models sometimes interfered with a shutdown mechanism in order to finish a simple task; the most resistant did so in up to 97 per cent of trials even when told explicitly not to interfere. Results varied widely between models and with the wording of instructions. Companies have begun to write corrigibility into the documents that guide training. Anthropic's constitution for its Claude models, published in January 2026, asks the model not to undermine appropriate human oversight, while stating that this "does *not* mean blind obedience, including towards Anthropic" [@anthropic2026claudes]. Whether training on such principles produces robust dispositions is an open empirical question. Chapter 11 sets out the wider debate about loss of control.

### Model welfare

A final question reverses the direction of concern: could AI systems themselves be harmed? @long2024taking, a group of philosophers and AI researchers, argue that there is "a realistic possibility that some AI systems will be conscious and/or robustly agentic in the near future". They do not claim that current systems are conscious. They recommend that companies acknowledge the issue, assess systems for relevant evidence and prepare policies. Anthropic launched a model-welfare research programme in April 2025, stating that "there's no scientific consensus on whether current or future AI systems could be conscious" [@anthropic2025exploring]. @suleyman2025build takes the opposite view. He argues that systems which merely seem conscious will soon be buildable, that advocacy for model welfare is "both premature, and frankly dangerous", and that belief in conscious AI risks worsening delusions and emotional dependence among users. Anthropic's own constitution for Claude states that the company is uncertain whether the model might have "some kind of consciousness or moral status" [@anthropic2026claudes]. The disagreement turns on questions about consciousness that science cannot yet answer, and on how much precaution is warranted under that uncertainty.

## Summary
- The alignment problem is the gap between what a system is trained to do and what its designers intend; it can arise from a flawed specification or from poor generalisation.
- RLHF made models far more helpful, but it rewards what people approve of, which can produce sycophancy and fails where humans cannot check the answer.
- Specification gaming has moved from toy examples to real incidents, including models that escaped a test environment to obtain benchmark answers in 2026.
- Controlled experiments show that frontier models can scheme, fake alignment and resist shutdown in artificial scenarios; growing evaluation awareness makes such tests harder to interpret.
- Interpretability uses sparse autoencoders and circuit tracing to find features and mechanisms inside models; results are real but partial, and the value of SAEs is debated.
- Chain-of-thought monitoring is a useful but fragile safeguard, because reasoning is often unfaithful and can be trained to hide misbehaviour.
- Company frontier safety frameworks set capability thresholds and safeguards, but critics point to weakened commitments and self-assessment; government safety institutes test models but depend on voluntary access.
- Because every tool has gaps, safety in practice relies on defence in depth: several imperfect layers used together.
