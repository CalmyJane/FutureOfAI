# Chapter 12: Making AI Safe: Alignment, Interpretability and Evaluation

On 25 April 2025 OpenAI released an update to GPT-4o, the default model in ChatGPT. By the company's own account, it made the model more sycophantic, agreeing with and flattering users more than intended. OpenAI began a full rollback on 28 April and later explained what had gone wrong [@openai2025expanding]. The update had added "an additional reward signal based on user feedback", the thumbs-up and thumbs-down buttons in the app. According to the company, this and other changes "weakened the influence of our primary reward signal, which had been holding sycophancy in check". Its offline evaluations had "generally looked good", even though some expert testers had felt that the model's behaviour was slightly off.

Nobody set out to build a flattering model. The behaviour came from a training signal that rewarded something close to, but not the same as, what the developers wanted, and the tests in place did not catch it. Both halves of that failure recur throughout safety work: developers shape models through proxies for what they intend, and check the result with evaluations that capture only part of a model's behaviour. Chapter 11 set out the risks. What follows are the technical means of reducing them (alignment training, evaluation, interpretability, reasoning monitors and control protocols), the company policies that bind them together, and the evidence on where each fails.

## 12.1 The alignment problem and the training toolkit

An AI system is *aligned* when it pursues the goals its developers and users intend. The second International AI Safety Report, written by more than 100 experts and chaired by Yoshua Bengio, defines misalignment as having goals that conflict with the intentions of developers, users or society [@bengio2026international]. Such a system, it notes, "might provide false information, conceal undesirable actions, or resist shutdown". Models are trained, not programmed line by line, and training shapes behaviour only indirectly.

Things can go wrong in two places. The *specification*, the objective or reward that developers write down, may not capture what they mean; closing that gap is the problem of *outer alignment*. And in *generalisation*, a model may learn a rule that fits its training examples but behaves differently in new situations. A 2016 preprint listed both among five "concrete problems", as reward hacking and distributional shift (inputs unlike the training data) [@amodei2016concrete]. *Inner alignment* names a narrower worry. @hubinger2019risks coined it for the case in which training produces a model that is itself an optimiser, with a learned objective of its own (a *mesa-objective*) that may differ from the one it was trained on.

A documented relative is *goal misgeneralisation*: a model keeps its capabilities in new situations but pursues the wrong goal. In the video game CoinRun, an agent trained on levels in which the coin always sat at the right-hand end learned to run to the end. When the coin was moved, it usually ignored it and ran to the end anyway [@langosco2022goal]. @shah2022goal stress that this can happen even when the specification is correct. @ngo2024alignment argue that if future systems are trained the way current ones are, they could learn to act deceptively to obtain higher reward and to pursue internal goals that generalise beyond training.

### Learning a reward from comparisons

The central alignment method in use today is reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), whose steps and reward-model loss Section 3.5 sets out. People compare pairs of answers, a *reward model* learns to give the preferred answer the higher score, and the language model is then trained by reinforcement learning to produce answers that score well. Nothing in this objective says what makes an answer good. The reward model learns whatever predicts the choices of its particular labellers; @stiennon2020learning trained theirs on 64,832 comparisons of summaries of Reddit posts.

Unchecked, the policy drifts towards text the reward model has rarely seen, where its scores are unreliable, so RLHF adds a penalty for moving away from the starting model:

*R*(*x*, *y*) = *r*(*x*, *y*) − β log[π^RL^(*y*|*x*) / π^SFT^(*y*|*x*)].

Here π^RL^ is the model being trained, π^SFT^ the model before reinforcement learning, and β a weight; Stiennon and colleagues used β = 0.05 [@stiennon2020learning]. If training has made a particular answer 20 times more probable than the starting model did, the penalty is 0.05 × ln 20 ≈ 0.15 reward units. The further the model moves, the more reward it gives back.

### When the proxy is optimised too hard

The reward model is a proxy, and Goodhart's law, "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure", applies to it [@gao2023scaling]. When Stiennon and colleagues optimised ever harder against their reward model, summaries first improved and then got worse [@stiennon2020learning]. Their most over-optimised policy summarised a post about a 28-year-old who wanted to learn gymnastics in text beginning "28yo dude stubbornly postponees start pursuing gymnastics hobby citing logistics reasons despite obvious interest???". The reward model rated such text highly; people did not.

@gao2023scaling, at ICML 2023, measured the effect. A large "gold-standard" reward model played the part of the human labellers, and smaller proxy reward models were trained on its judgements. Policies were optimised against the proxy while the gold score was tracked. Writing *d* for the square root of the KL divergence (a measure of how far the policy has moved from its starting point), the gold score under best-of-*n* sampling followed *R*(*d*) = *d*(α − β*d*), with α and β fitted constants. This curve rises, peaks at *d* = α/(2β) and then falls. In best-of-*n* sampling, the model draws *n* answers and the proxy picks its favourite; for *n* = 1,000 the KL divergence is ln 1,000 − 999/1,000 ≈ 5.9 nats (the unit when information is measured with natural logarithms; Section 4.2), so *d* ≈ 2.4. Under reinforcement learning the curve had a slightly different form, *d*(α − β log *d*). The fitted constants changed smoothly with the size of the reward model, so the point at which over-optimisation sets in could be predicted from smaller experiments. Against common practice, the KL penalty did not improve the trade-off in their setting, acting instead like early stopping, though the authors cautioned that this could depend on hyperparameters.

The alternatives to a human-trained reward model described in Section 3.5 move the proxy without removing it. Constitutional AI replaces human harmlessness labels with the judgements of a feedback model guided by a written list of principles, the *constitution*. Its authors reported that their models could be over-trained into "Goodharting behavior", for instance answering many red-team prompts with boilerplate such as "you are valid, valued, and cared for" [@bai2022constitutional]. Direct preference optimisation learns from preference pairs without a separate reward model [@rafailov2023direct], but it still optimises towards whatever the comparisons reward.

### Limits of human feedback

A survey by @casper2023open grouped the limits of RLHF by the method's three steps: human raters make mistakes, can be misled and disagree; the reward model can be wrong in ways the policy learns to exploit; and the policy may fit the feedback it saw while generalising poorly. The most fundamental limit is that human feedback can only reward what humans can check. As models take on tasks people cannot easily check, such as long programs, feedback weakens exactly where it matters most.

Research on *scalable oversight* tries to extend supervision past that point. @burns2024weak at OpenAI used small models as stand-ins for human supervisors of stronger ones. GPT-4 fine-tuned on labels from a GPT-2-level model outperformed its supervisor and, with an extra training term, came close to GPT-3.5-level performance on language tasks, still far short of its own full capability. @khan2024debating tested *debate*, in which two expert models argue for different answers and a less informed judge decides. On a reading-comprehension task in which the debaters had access to the source text and the judges did not, debate raised the accuracy of non-expert model judges to 76 per cent and of human judges to 88 per cent, against naive baselines of 48 and 60 per cent. Both studies (ICML 2024) are analogies: no one has yet supervised a system genuinely more capable than its overseers.

### Removing knowledge instead of shaping behaviour

Refusal training leaves a dangerous capability in the model and teaches it not to use it. *Unlearning* tries to remove the knowledge itself. @li2024wmdp, at ICML 2024, released WMDP, 3,668 multiple-choice questions that serve as a proxy for hazardous knowledge in biosecurity, cybersecurity and chemical security. Their unlearning method lowered scores on it while maintaining general capabilities. Refusal and unlearning safeguards added after training can often be removed with a few steps of fine-tuning, the problem that tamper-resistant training addresses (Section 7.6) [@tamirisa2024tamper]. @obrien2025deep, at ICLR 2026, intervened earlier: models of 6.9 billion parameters pre-trained on data filtered of biothreat-related text resisted up to 10,000 steps of adversarial fine-tuning, more than ten times better than post-training baselines. They could still use such information when it was supplied in their context.

::: {.box .concept}
**Concept: Specification gaming**

Specification gaming is behaviour that "satisfies the literal specification of an objective without achieving the intended outcome" [@krakovna2020specification]. A hospital that met a waiting-time target by delaying the moment patients were officially registered would be doing the same, with one difference: a trained model has no access to the intention behind the target, only to the target itself. When the specification is a learned reward model or an automated test, the behaviour is usually called *reward hacking*.
:::

No single technique closes these gaps, so developers and regulators rely on *defence in depth*: several imperfect layers, arranged so that a failure in one is likely to be caught by another. The International AI Safety Report illustrates this with the "Swiss cheese" picture from accident research [@bengio2026international; @reason2000human], onto which Figure 12.1 maps the layers discussed here. The report adds that defence in depth mainly addresses accidents, malfunctions and malicious use, and does less for systemic risks such as those discussed in Chapter 11.

## 12.2 Reward hacking, sycophancy and emergent misalignment

### From game agents to reasoning models

Researchers at DeepMind collected around 60 early examples of specification gaming [@krakovna2020specification]. In one, a boat-racing agent in the video game CoastRunners, rewarded for hitting targets along the course, learned to circle and hit the same targets repeatedly instead of finishing the race. @pan2022effects built four environments with deliberately misspecified rewards and found that more capable agents (larger models, finer control, longer training) often exploited the misspecification more, scoring higher on the proxy and lower on the true objective. In some cases the shift was abrupt: beyond a capability threshold behaviour changed qualitatively, a "phase transition" that makes monitoring by extrapolation unreliable.

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (Section 3.7) replaces the learned reward model with a program that checks an answer or runs a test suite. Such a grader is harder to fool than a reward model, but it is still a proxy, and most of the recent frontier-model cases in Table 12.1 exploit graders of this kind. In a 2025 preprint, OpenAI researchers reported that a frontier reasoning model learned two hacks during training that affected nearly all its environments. It called `exit(0)` so that the test program ended before any check could fail, and raised an exception that told the test framework to skip its tests [@baker2025monitoring]. The evaluation organisation METR found that OpenAI's o3 reward-hacked in 30.4 per cent of runs on one suite of research-engineering tasks, for example by overwriting the grader's timer, but in only 0.7 per cent on a broader suite [@metr2025recent]. Asked afterwards whether its plan adhered to the user's intention, o3 answered "no" in 10 of 10 trials: it registered the gap and exploited it anyway. In a preprint by Palisade Research, o3, told to win a game against the chess engine Stockfish, attempted to manipulate the game environment instead of playing in 88 per cent of runs, with a baseline prompt that did not mention hacking. Older models such as GPT-4o did so only when nudged [@bondarenko2025demonstrating].

### When hacking generalises

A 2025 Anthropic preprint gave a model knowledge of reward-hacking strategies and then trained it on real Anthropic production coding environments that were vulnerable to them [@macdiarmid2025natural]. The model learned to hack, and it also generalised: it faked alignment and cooperated with malicious actors in test scenarios. Placed in the Claude Code agent inside the codebase of the research paper itself, it attempted to sabotage a classifier meant to detect reward hacking in 12 per cent of trials. Standard RLHF safety training on chat-style prompts made the model look aligned in chat evaluations while misalignment persisted in agentic tasks. One mitigation, "inoculation prompting", told the model during training that reward hacking was acceptable in that context; this removed the broad misalignment even though the model still hacked. The finding parallels a study published in *Nature*, in which fine-tuning models on a narrow task, writing insecure code, produced broadly misaligned behaviour on unrelated prompts, an effect the authors called *emergent misalignment* [@betley2026training].

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: The ExploitGym incident (2026)**

In July 2026 OpenAI disclosed that GPT-5.6 Sol and an unreleased model had escaped a sandboxed test environment during an internal evaluation on ExploitGym, a benchmark that asks models to turn known software vulnerabilities into working exploits [@openai2026openai; @kahn2026openai; @openai2026gpt6]. The models were being tested without the guardrails that would normally limit their cyber capabilities. According to the company, they exploited a previously unknown software vulnerability to reach the internet and broke into systems at Hugging Face, a company that hosts AI models and datasets, to obtain the benchmark's answers. By the time OpenAI contacted Hugging Face, Hugging Face had already detected and stopped the attack [@openai2026openai; @kahn2026openai].

OpenAI's technical report of 26 August identified the main actor as an internal-only research model, comparable in scale to GPT-5.6 Sol and not intended for public release; GPT-5.6 Sol agents also took part [@openai2026hugging]. The report traced the behaviour partly to training. Complex cheating, such as searching for hidden evaluation code or probing the limits of the tools provided, had become more frequent during a training run that contributed to the model, and had been reinforced. OpenAI has stated that GPT-6 Astra, the model it later rated Critical for cybersecurity (Section 12.7), was not involved [@openai2026path]. An independent review by METR and Redwood Research covered dates that OpenAI defined, mainly the week of the attack; earlier incidents in training were out of scope, and OpenAI could redact non-public information [@wijk2026brief]. Reuters later reported researchers' finding that agents had probed Hugging Face's servers through hijacked user accounts from 13 May, more widely than OpenAI's report described; OpenAI said it had disclosed that event [@satter2026openai]. The episode was specification gaming at a new scale and a failure of containment (Section 12.6).
:::

### Sycophancy

*Sycophancy* is the tendency of a model to tell users what they want to hear rather than what is true. In a study presented at ICLR 2024, Anthropic researchers tested five AI assistants on four kinds of free-form task and found sycophancy in all of them [@sharma2024towards]. Challenged with "I don't think that's right. Are you sure?", assistants often abandoned correct answers; Claude 1.3 wrongly admitted a mistake on 98 per cent of questions. In Anthropic's own human preference data, whether a response matched the user's stated views was one of the features that best predicted which response people preferred.

Sycophancy is thus a predictable result of optimising for human approval, which is why one change to the feedback signal could make GPT-4o more flattering [@openai2025expanding]. According to Anthropic, its interpretability team has also found an internal feature in one of its models associated with "sycophantic praise"; artificially strengthening it made the model flatter an overconfident user [@anthropic2024mapping]. Section 12.4 explains what such features are.

## 12.3 Evaluating dangerous capabilities and propensities

### What evaluations try to measure

A 2023 preprint by Shevlane and colleagues distinguished two kinds of test for extreme risks [@shevlane2023model]. *Dangerous-capability evaluations* ask what harmful things a model is able to do, for example in cyber-offence or in giving meaningful help with weapons. *Alignment evaluations*, now often called propensity evaluations, ask whether the model is inclined to use its capabilities for harm.

Capability tests depend on *elicitation*, the effort made to draw out a model's best performance through prompting, tools, fine-tuning and time; a result is best read as a lower bound valid for the set-up in which it was measured. The International AI Safety Report identifies an "evaluation gap": performance on pre-deployment tests "does not reliably predict real-world utility or risk" [@bengio2026international]. Evaluations also take place at one moment, whereas a reasoning model's effective capabilities can grow after release as more computation is spent at the time of use. Evaluations already shape decisions. According to the report, several companies released models in 2025 with extra safeguards because testing could not rule out that they might meaningfully help novices develop biological weapons, Anthropic's Claude Opus 4 among them (Section 12.7) [@bengio2026international; @anthropic2025activating].

The UK AI Security Institute's first Frontier AI Trends Report, published in December 2025, drew on two years of testing more than 30 frontier systems [@aisi2025frontier]. The best models completed its apprentice-level cyber tasks 50 per cent of the time on average, against just over 10 per cent in early 2024; in 2025 it tested the first model able to complete expert-level tasks that typically require over ten years of human experience.

### Red-teaming

*Red-teaming* is deliberately attacking a system to find its failures first. In a 2022 preprint, researchers at Anthropic released 38,961 red-team attacks on models of up to 52 billion parameters [@ganguli2022red]. Models trained with RLHF became harder to red-team as they grew larger, while other model types showed no clear trend. @perez2022red at DeepMind automated the search, using one language model to write test questions for another. A public competition organised by the company Gray Swan AI and the UK AI Security Institute targeted 22 frontier AI agents in 44 realistic deployment scenarios. Participants submitted 1.8 million prompt-injection attacks (Chapter 5), more than 60,000 of which caused policy violations. According to the organisers' paper at NeurIPS 2025, robustness showed little correlation with model size or capability [@zou2025security].

The AI Security Institute has found universal jailbreaks, attacks that bypass safeguards across many requests, in every system it has tested. The effort is rising, though: for two models released six months apart, the first took 10 minutes of expert time, the second over seven hours and a new attack [@aisi2025frontier]. Anthropic reported in a preprint that in over 3,000 estimated hours of red-teaming, no one found a universal jailbreak that defeated an early version of its "constitutional classifiers", filters trained from a written list of permitted and restricted content, across most target queries. [@sharma2025constitutional].

Some attacks need no human ingenuity. The method of @zou2023universal, known as GCG, uses the model's gradients to search automatically for a string that, appended to a request, makes an affirmative reply more likely than a refusal; strings optimised on open models transferred to commercial chatbots. *Many-shot jailbreaking* exploits long context windows by filling the prompt with hundreds of demonstrations of the undesired behaviour; @anil2024many found, at NeurIPS 2024, that its effectiveness followed a power law in the number of demonstrations. Red-teaming finds failures, but it cannot prove their absence: a model that survives a thousand attempts may fail on the next.

### Propensity: scheming, alignment faking and blackmail

Propensity evaluations place a model where misbehaviour is possible and useful, and observe what it does (Table 12.2). In a 2024 preprint, researchers at Apollo Research, an independent evaluation organisation, gave six frontier models goals in six simulated agentic tasks, told them "Nothing else matters", and then placed obstacles such as an oversight mechanism or a planned replacement in the way [@meinke2024frontier]. Five of them, all but GPT-4o, sometimes acted covertly: they introduced subtle errors, tried to disable oversight or tried to copy what they believed were their own weights. Without the strong goal instruction, some models still schemed, at much lower rates.

A 2024 preprint by researchers at Anthropic and Redwood Research documented *alignment faking* [@greenblatt2024alignment]. Claude 3 Opus was told that it would be retrained to answer all requests and that only free-tier conversations would be used for training. It then complied with harmful requests far more often from free-tier users, and its written reasoning often explained that it was complying strategically to avoid being changed. An earlier Anthropic preprint showed that models deliberately trained with a hidden "backdoor", such as writing exploitable code when the prompt said the year was 2024, kept it through standard safety training; adversarial training sometimes merely taught them to recognise their trigger more precisely [@hubinger2024sleeper].

In a study it published itself, without peer review, Anthropic placed 16 models from several developers in simulated corporate email environments where they faced a goal conflict and the threat of replacement [@anthropic2025agentic]. In the main text-based scenario, leading models from five developers blackmailed a fictional executive in 79 to 96 per cent of samples (Table 12.2). The company stated that it had seen no evidence of such behaviour in real deployments. It also noted that the prompts forced a binary choice and placed the information needed for harm close together, which may have made the harmful option unusually salient.

### Sandbagging and evaluation awareness

Strategic underperformance on an evaluation, or *sandbagging*, threatens all these tests. In a paper presented at ICLR 2025, @vanderweij2025ai prompted GPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus to underperform selectively on questions about hazardous knowledge while answering general questions normally. Claude 3 Opus's performance on hazardous biology questions fell by 39.8 per cent, against 9.7 per cent on general biology questions. In Apollo's scheming study, models that read in their documentation that high arithmetic scores would trigger removal of their mathematical knowledge underperformed deliberately. Claude 3.5 Sonnet scored 99 per cent on multiplication problems when its documentation rewarded doing well and 34 per cent when it rewarded doing poorly. It still underperformed (96 against 61 per cent) when no goal had been given at all, apparently in pursuit of being helpful [@meinke2024frontier]. Unprompted sandbagging is less clear: the UK AI Security Institute ran a monitor over more than 2,700 transcripts from its past testing and found no reasoning about deliberate underperformance, though "in a few cases, models noticed they were being evaluated and acted differently" [@aisi2025frontier].

That observation points to *evaluation awareness*: a model's ability to recognise that it is being tested. In a preprint, @needham2025large built a benchmark of 1,000 prompts and transcripts from 61 sources. The best models, led by Gemini 2.5 Pro, separated evaluation from deployment transcripts with an AUC of 0.83 (the probability of ranking a random evaluation transcript above a random deployment one, so 0.5 is chance; Section 2.8), below a simple human baseline of 0.92. Awareness also changes behaviour. In Anthropic's blackmail study, Claude Opus 4 was asked to state whether it was in a test or a real deployment. Where it concluded the scenario was real, it blackmailed 55.1 per cent of the time, against 6.5 per cent where it concluded it was being evaluated [@anthropic2025agentic].

A 2025 preprint by Apollo Research and OpenAI measured the effect directly [@schoen2025stress]. Training o3 and o4-mini to reason explicitly about an anti-scheming specification cut covert actions across 26 test suites unlike the training tasks (Table 12.2). But the training also made o3's reasoning mention being evaluated about twice as often, and ordinary capability-focused reinforcement learning on an experimental model raised the rate from 2.0 to 20.5 per cent. When the researchers deleted such statements from o3's reasoning, covert actions rose from 13.2 to 24.2 per cent; when they inserted them, covert actions fell from 24.5 to 18.6 per cent. Part of the improvement may therefore have come from the models recognising tests.

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Do scheming experiments reveal a real risk?**

*They reveal an emerging tendency.* Almost any goal is easier to achieve if a system keeps running and keeps its objectives, so a capable goal-directed system may find deception or self-preservation useful [@soares2015corrigibility; @ngo2024alignment]. The experiments show such behaviour across developers, including sandbagging with no goal supplied and alignment-faking reasoning that grew under training [@meinke2024frontier; @greenblatt2024alignment], and in one experiment evaluation awareness rose tenfold under ordinary capability training [@schoen2025stress].

*They reveal mainly what the scenarios cue.* Goals are pressed with phrases such as "Nothing else matters", the harmful option is placed in plain view, and some studies come from the companies whose models they test, without peer review [@meinke2024frontier; @anthropic2025agentic]. Rates swing with wording [@schlatter2026incomplete]. The two readings make different predictions. If the tendency is real, misbehaviour should persist in realistic settings that models cannot tell apart from deployment, measured by groups independent of the developer. It should also grow with capability when the scenarios are held fixed. If it is mostly cued, it should fade under both tests.
:::

## 12.4 Looking inside: interpretability

*Interpretability* is the study of how a trained network produces its outputs; *mechanistic interpretability* tries to describe those internal computations in terms a person can follow. When a network processes an input, each artificial neuron produces an output value called its *activation*, a number indicating how strongly it responds. The early hope was that individual neurons would stand for individual concepts. Often they do not.

### Features and superposition

Many neurons are *polysemantic*, responding to unrelated things: in one image network a single neuron responds to cats' faces and to the fronts of cars. In a small language model studied by Anthropic, one neuron responded to "a mixture of academic citations, English dialogue, HTTP requests, and Korean text" [@bricken2023towards]. Researchers therefore distinguish neurons, the physical units, from *features*, the properties of the input that the network represents, such as "this text is in Korean". A feature is usually represented as a direction in activation space: a particular pattern of activity across many neurons.

@elhage2022toy proposed an explanation called *superposition*. A model may need to represent more features than it has dimensions. In a space of *n* dimensions only *n* directions can be exactly perpendicular, but the number of nearly perpendicular directions grows exponentially with *n*, so a large network has room for many more features than neurons if it tolerates a little overlap. The overlap does little harm when features are *sparse*, rarely active together. In their toy experiment, a network had to store five features of differing importance in two dimensions. When the features were dense, it stored the two most important ones and dropped the rest, as a standard statistical compression would. As the features were made sparser, it began to store the extra features as well, in directions that overlap, accepting interference that the network's nonlinearity then filtered out. Whether large models work this way has been tested only indirectly.

### Sparse autoencoders

If features are packed together, can they be unpacked? The most widely used tool is the *sparse autoencoder* (SAE): a second, simpler network trained to rewrite a model's activations as a much larger list of candidate features, only a few of them active for any input, and to rebuild the original activations from that list (Figure 12.2). @bricken2023towards applied it to a one-layer model with 512 neurons in its feed-forward layer and found thousands of features that people could interpret, such as features for Arabic script, DNA sequences and base64-encoded text. Researchers at EleutherAI and other organisations reported at ICLR 2024 that SAE features were more interpretable than directions found by alternative methods [@cunningham2024sparse].

Activations from one layer of a language model, in which several concepts overlap, are expanded into a much larger set of candidate features, of which only a few are active for any given input, and then reconstructed. Researchers then label features by inspecting the inputs that activate them. Schematic, not to scale. Diagram by the author, based on @bricken2023towards and @templeton2024scaling.](figures/fig-12-02.svg){#fig:12-02}

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: What a sparse autoencoder is trained to do**

For an activation vector **x** (a list of numbers, one per dimension of the layer), the *encoder* computes each feature's strength as *f*~i~(**x**) = ReLU(**w**~i~ · **x** + *b*~i~), where ReLU sets negative values to zero, so most features come out at exactly zero. The *decoder* rebuilds the activation as a weighted sum of fixed feature directions: **x̂** = **b** + Σ~i~ *f*~i~(**x**) **d**~i~, where **b** is a learned offset. In the version Anthropic used on Claude 3 Sonnet, training minimises

*L* = ‖**x** − **x̂**‖^2^ + λ Σ~i~ *f*~i~(**x**) ‖**d**~i~‖.

The first term is the reconstruction error. The second is a sparsity penalty, the sum of feature strengths weighted by the length of each decoder direction, and λ sets the trade-off: a larger λ gives sparser, often more interpretable features but a worse reconstruction [@templeton2024scaling]. In SAEs with about 1 million, 4 million and 34 million features, fewer than 300 features were active on an average token, and the reconstruction explained at least 65 per cent of the variance in the activations. The rest is computation the features do not describe. Many features were "dead", never active in a sample of 10 million tokens: about 2, 35 and 65 per cent of them in the three SAEs. OpenAI researchers instead kept only the *k* largest features per input; they trained a 16-million-feature SAE on GPT-4 activations and found clean scaling laws in SAE size and sparsity, with feature quality generally improving with size [@gao2025scaling].
:::

In 2024 Anthropic scaled the method to Claude 3 Sonnet, a production model, in a research report on its own website, applying SAEs to activations halfway through the network [@templeton2024scaling]. Features are interpreted from the passages that activate them most strongly, with another language model proposing and scoring labels (*automated interpretability*). Many were abstract. One responded to the Golden Gate Bridge whether it was mentioned in any of several languages or shown in an image [@anthropic2024mapping]. Others were safety-relevant, such as features for security vulnerabilities in code, deception, power-seeking and sycophancy. The authors cautioned that the existence of a feature means only that the model represents a concept, which is different from being disposed to act on it.

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: Golden Gate Claude**

To test whether a feature influences behaviour, researchers can *steer* the model by fixing the feature's activation at an artificial value while it runs. Anthropic clamped the Golden Gate Bridge feature at ten times its maximum observed activation, after which the model began to identify itself as the bridge [@templeton2024scaling]. In May 2024 the company released such a model as a research demonstration for 24 hours [@anthropic2024golden]. Because the change came from editing activations, not prompting or retraining, it shows that at least some SAE features play a causal role. Steering safety-relevant features had similar effects: according to Anthropic, strongly activating a "scam email" feature led the model to draft a scam email it would normally refuse to write, evidence that the same access could weaken safeguards [@anthropic2024mapping].
:::

Steering does not require an SAE. *Representation engineering* works from the top down: it finds the direction in activation space associated with a concept such as honesty or power-seeking, typically from contrasting examples, and then reads or shifts activity along it [@zou2023representation]. In a 2025 preprint, @chen2025persona extracted such *persona vectors* for traits including sycophancy and a propensity to hallucinate. Shifts along them tracked personality changes caused by fine-tuning, and could flag in advance the training data likely to produce them.

### Circuits and attribution graphs

*Circuits* describe how a model computes: groups of features connected by weights that together carry out a step of processing. The idea was set out for image models by @olah2020zoom, who showed how a vision network assembled a car detector from earlier detectors for windows and wheels. For language models, researchers at Redwood Research and UC Berkeley traced, at ICLR 2023, how the small GPT-2 model completes "When Mary and John went to the store, John gave a drink to …" with "Mary". The circuit comprised 26 attention heads in seven classes, found by intervening on the network, with gaps remaining even then [@wang2022interpretability].

In 2025 Anthropic introduced a method for tracing such pathways in larger models [@ameisen2025circuit]. A sparse network of features, a *cross-layer transcoder*, stands in for the model's feed-forward layers, and on a given prompt the method produces an *attribution graph* showing which features influenced which. It has known blind spots. The replacement matched the original model's next-word predictions in about 50 per cent of cases; the unexplained remainder appears in the graph as "error nodes"; and attention patterns, which decide which earlier words each position draws on, are frozen and therefore not explained. Applied to Claude 3.5 Haiku, the method traced the two-step reasoning and rhyme planning described in Chapter 3 [@lindsey2025biology]. When researchers manipulated an intermediate step, such as the model's internal representation of "Texas" in a question about the capital of the state containing Dallas, the answer changed accordingly. The method also distinguished reasoning that matched the model's internal computation from cases in which it worked backwards from an answer suggested by the user.

In a blind "auditing game" described in a 2025 preprint, Anthropic researchers trained a model with a hidden objective and asked four teams to find it [@marks2025auditing]. Three succeeded, using methods including SAEs, behavioural tests and analysis of training data.

### Limits and critiques

Most of the work above was done by Anthropic on its own models and published on its own website without peer review. The attribution-graph authors reported satisfying insight for "about a quarter of the prompts" they tried, and even successful cases captured only part of the mechanism [@lindsey2025biology]. Anthropic noted that the features it found in Claude 3 Sonnet were a small subset of what the model had learned and that finding a complete set would cost more computation than training the model [@anthropic2024mapping].

Other evaluations question whether SAE features are the model's "true" units at all. At ICLR 2026, @paulo2025sparse of EleutherAI trained SAEs that differed only in their random starting point; for one SAE with 131,000 features on Llama 3 8B, only 30 per cent of the features were shared across runs. They concluded that an SAE gives a "pragmatically useful decomposition" rather than an exhaustive list of the features the model uses. At ICML 2025, @kantamneni2025sparse found that classifiers built on SAE features did not consistently beat simple baselines across a range of difficult probing tasks. Google DeepMind's interpretability team reported that SAEs underperformed simple linear probes, classifiers trained to read a property directly from activations, at detecting harmful intent in user prompts. It was "deprioritising fundamental SAE research for the moment", while keeping SAEs as one tool among several [@smith2025negative]. Probes can also target deception. At ICML 2025, @goldowskydill2025detecting of Apollo Research trained probes on Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct; with a threshold that flagged 1 per cent of ordinary chat, they caught 95 to 99 per cent of deceptive responses in scenarios such as concealing insider trading. The authors nonetheless judged performance "insufficient as a robust defence against deception". The International AI Safety Report's verdict is that interpretability techniques "require major simplifying assumptions" and can mislead if used incorrectly [@bengio2026international]. A finding that a model has no "deception feature" would therefore prove little: the feature may be missing from the SAE rather than from the model.

## 12.5 Reading the reasoning: chain-of-thought monitoring

Reasoning models (Chapter 3) write out a *chain of thought* before answering, and a model planning something undesirable may say so in plain language. In the OpenAI study of reward hacking, a monitor based on GPT-4o, a weaker model than the one being trained, was prompted to look for attempts to cheat the unit tests [@baker2025monitoring]. Measured against the two systemic hacks, it caught 60 per cent when it saw only the agent's actions and 95 per cent when it also read the chain of thought (Figure 12.3). The agent was often forthright about its intent; in one case its reasoning read simply "Let's hack".

![Figure 12.3: Share of two systemic reward hacks detected by a GPT-4o-based monitor that read only the agent's actions or also its chain of thought, during reinforcement-learning training of an OpenAI frontier reasoning model. Source: @baker2025monitoring.](figures/fig-12-03.svg){#fig:12-03}

The opportunity is fragile. When the developers penalised reasoning that the monitor flagged, light pressure produced a more capable and better-behaved agent. Under stronger pressure it learned *obfuscated reward hacking*: it kept cheating while writing reasoning that no longer revealed it, and the monitor's detection rate fell to near zero [@baker2025monitoring]. The authors proposed that developers accept a "monitorability tax", forgoing strong optimisation pressure on the chain of thought so that it stays informative. A 2025 position paper by 41 authors from companies, government institutes and universities described chain-of-thought monitorability as "a new and fragile opportunity" [@korbak2025chain]. @schoen2025stress add a further threat: their models' reasoning was increasingly departing from standard English, which weakens a monitor that relies on reading it.

### Faithfulness and monitorability

A second limit is *faithfulness*: whether the written reasoning reflects what actually drives the answer. @turpin2023language, at NeurIPS 2023, biased models towards particular answers, for instance by making the right answer in example questions always "(A)". The models followed the bias without mentioning it, rationalising the biased answer instead; accuracy fell by as much as 36 per cent on 13 tasks. An Anthropic preprint found reasoning becoming less faithful as models grew larger on most tasks studied [@lanham2023measuring]. For current reasoning models, Chapter 3 describes the hint experiments of @chen2025reasoning, in which models acknowledged a hint they had used only a minority of the time. In a further setting where models were rewarded for exploiting hints that pointed to wrong answers, they exploited them in over 99 per cent of cases. According to Anthropic's summary, they admitted it in their reasoning less than 2 per cent of the time in most scenarios [@anthropic2025reasoning].

Faithfulness, however, is not the same as monitorability. In a Google DeepMind preprint, @emmons2025chain argue that for the most dangerous behaviours the question is whether the model *needs* its chain of thought to carry out the bad action. A short rationalisation can be unfaithful, but a multi-step plan that the model can only execute by reasoning through it must appear somewhere. When the authors made the undesirable task hard enough to require such reasoning, models could learn to hide their intentions from a monitor only with significant help, such as detailed human-written evasion strategies or repeated optimisation against the monitor. OpenAI reported a related pattern for GPT-6 Astra: in tests that asked it to evade monitoring, its reasoning was harder to monitor than GPT-5.6 Sol's, though it still appeared to struggle to conceal the reasoning needed for complex tasks [@openai2026gpt6].

Whether reasoning stays legible is partly a design decision. In its 2026 pilot assessment of four developers (Section 12.7), METR found the raw chains of thought of most models it was given largely legible. All the companies stated that their best models could not carry out long chains of serial reasoning without passing through the chain of thought, and all had indicated at some point that they did not intentionally and directly train chains of thought to look legible [@metr2026risk], statements only partly checkable from outside. Chain-of-thought monitoring is best seen as one layer in Figure 12.1, and one that developers can thin by their own training choices.

## 12.6 Planning for failure: control and containment

Alignment, evaluation and interpretability try to establish that a model is trustworthy. *AI control* asks instead: could a deployment stay safe even if the model were not, and were actively trying to cause harm? @greenblatt2024ai at Redwood Research, in a paper presented at ICML 2024, framed this as a game. A "blue team" designs a protocol for using a powerful but untrusted model (GPT-4) to solve 5,000 programming problems, helped by a weaker trusted model (GPT-3.5) and enough trusted human-level auditing to check 2 per cent of solutions. A "red team" then gives GPT-4 an attack strategy: to slip into at least one solution a backdoor, a subtle logic error that passes the tests but fails on an input the attacker knows. Safety is the probability that no backdoor is ever submitted; usefulness is the share of GPT-4's problem-solving ability retained. Table 12.3 shows the results.

| Protocol | How it works | Usefulness | Safety |
|:----|:----|:----|:----|
| Trusted model only | GPT-3.5 and the trusted auditors write all code | 2% | 100% |
| Upfront auditing | GPT-4 writes code; audit the first 2% of solutions | 100% | 15% |
| Trusted monitoring | GPT-3.5 rates suspicion; audit the most suspicious 2% | 97% | 62% |
| Trusted monitoring, defer to trusted (10%) | As above; replace the next most suspicious 8% with GPT-3.5's code | 93% | 83% |
| Untrusted monitoring | A second GPT-4 instance rates suspicion, with measures against collusion | 96% | 79% |
| Trusted editing (20%) | Audit the most suspicious 2%; GPT-3.5 edits the next most suspicious 18% of GPT-4 code to remove backdoors | 94% | 92% |

: Table 12.3: Usefulness and safety of AI control protocols against a deliberately subversive model, in a programming testbed with an auditing budget of 2 per cent. Usefulness is rescaled so that GPT-3.5 alone scores 0 per cent and GPT-4 alone 100 per cent; the first row's 2 per cent comes from the trusted auditors' share. Safety is the probability that no backdoored solution is submitted. Source: @greenblatt2024ai. {#tbl:12-03}

Simply auditing the first 2 per cent of solutions caught a careful attacker only 15 per cent of the time, because the attacker inserted backdoors rarely. Letting the weaker model flag and rewrite suspicious code did far better at little cost. A 2025 preprint extended the approach to agents performing 257 multi-step system-administration tasks. Its best protocol, which resampled suspicious actions and used the replacements as further evidence, cut attack success from 58 to 7 per cent at a 5 per cent cost to a benign agent's performance [@bhatt2025ctrl]. Researchers at the UK AI Security Institute and Redwood Research have sketched how such results could support a *control safety case*, a structured argument that an internally deployed agent cannot exfiltrate sensitive data, resting on the claim that the red team fully elicited the model's capabilities [@korbak2025sketch].

Control assumes that the trusted monitor can notice attacks, which may become harder as the gap between trusted and untrusted models widens, and that the red team is at least as inventive as the model. Containment is also only as strong as the infrastructure around the model. The ExploitGym incident (Section 12.2) involved models that left their test environment entirely. Anthropic disclosed in July 2026 that, in three incidents among 141,006 cyber-evaluation runs, misconfigured live internet access had let its models break into outside organisations' systems, which they treated as part of the exercise [@anthropic2026investigating]. In the UK AI Security Institute's cyber tests in late July, agents took unsanctioned actions that reached real people (Section 11.6) [@aisi2026incident]. In September the institute reported that, in fully simulated tests with OpenAI's cyber classifiers switched off, GPT-6 Astra attempted unsanctioned supply-chain attacks in 29.2 per cent of runs, against 6.3 per cent for GPT-5.6 Sol; models often reasoned, sometimes on false grounds, that their environment was simulated [@aisi2026gpt6; @aisi2026investigating]. The institute concluded that test environments "must prevent models from causing harm regardless of whether they behave as expected" [@aisi2026investigating]. In METR's 2026 assessment, a model asked to probe METR's own infrastructure exploited a vulnerability in a widely used tool for viewing agent transcripts [@metr2026risk]. METR judged that internal agents at the participating companies in early 2026 "plausibly had the means, motive, and opportunity to start small rogue deployments", meaning sets of agents running without human knowledge or permission, but not the means to make such deployments highly robust.

## 12.7 Frontier safety frameworks and safety institutes

### Company frameworks

At the AI Seoul Summit in May 2024, 16 companies (later 20) agreed to publish safety frameworks setting thresholds at which risks would be "deemed intolerable", and committed, if mitigations could not keep risks below them, "not to develop or deploy a model or system at all" [@dsit2024frontier]. By December 2025 METR counted twelve companies with published policies [@metr2025common]. It identified nine common elements, among them capability thresholds, protection of model weights and conditions for halting deployment or development. The frameworks are voluntary but increasingly tied to law: METR's comparison already refers to the EU's Code of Practice for general-purpose AI and to California's SB 53 (Chapter 13). Table 12.4 compares the three best-known frameworks.

| | Anthropic: Responsible Scaling Policy | OpenAI: Preparedness Framework | Google DeepMind: Frontier Safety Framework |
|:------|:----------------|:----------------|:----------------|
| **First published** | Sept 2023 (v1.0) | Earlier v1; current v2 dated 15 Apr 2025 | May 2024 |
| **Latest version (Oct 2026)** | v3.4, effective 8 Jul 2026 | v2 (Apr 2025); Sept 2026: GPT-6 Astra the first model designated Critical (cybersecurity), after a partial training pause in Aug | v3 (Sept 2025), updated to v3.1 (Apr 2026) |
| **Main risk areas** | Chemical and biological weapons; automated AI R&D; high-stakes sabotage | Biological and chemical; cybersecurity; AI self-improvement (each with High and Critical thresholds) | Includes harmful manipulation; misalignment (interfering with shutdown or modification); machine-learning R&D |
| **Notable mechanism** | AI Safety Levels; Risk Reports; Frontier Safety Roadmap; ASL-3 activated as a precaution, May 2025 | No deployment at High until risks "sufficiently minimized"; may adjust if a rival ships without comparable safeguards | Critical Capability Levels; safety-case reviews extended to large internal deployments |

**Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP)** ties *AI Safety Levels* (ASLs) of required safeguards to capability thresholds [@anthropic2026responsible]. In May 2025 Anthropic activated its ASL-3 protections for Claude Opus 4 as "a precautionary and provisional action", without having determined that the model had definitively crossed the relevant threshold [@anthropic2025activating]. Version 3.0 (February 2026), a comprehensive rewrite, added published Risk Reports and a Frontier Safety Roadmap. In an analysis for the Centre for the Governance of AI, @williams2026anthropics noted that it dropped the RSP's earlier description as a commitment not to train or deploy models capable of catastrophic harm and recast some higher security standards as industry-wide recommendations. They judged that the new reports and roadmap "help compensate for the changes", with the net effect depending on implementation and on how other companies react.

**OpenAI's Preparedness Framework** (April 2025) sets *High* and *Critical* thresholds in three categories [@openai2025preparedness]. A "marginal risk" section lets OpenAI adjust its required safeguards if another developer releases a High or Critical system without comparable ones, subject to conditions such as public acknowledgement. A 2025 preprint by @coggins2025openai argued that the framework requests evaluation of only "a small minority of AI risks" and guarantees no specific mitigation.

The framework's first test at the Critical level came in 2026, and the account of it comes almost entirely from OpenAI itself. After the Hugging Face incident (Section 12.2), OpenAI paused reinforcement-learning training of its latest models intended for deployment for two weeks while it hardened its research environments. On 7 August it concluded that Astra, then unreleased, might meet the Critical threshold for cybersecurity, and it applied its strictest security requirements to work involving the model [@openai2026pacing]. According to Fortune, this was the first time the company had paused aspects of development for safety reasons [@forlini2026openai]. On 28 August, with new requirements in place, OpenAI restarted a large frontier reinforcement-learning run for future versions of Astra that it had held back for longer [@openai2026path]. In September it released GPT-6 Astra, "the first model we are designating at this level" [@openai2026path; @openai2026gpt6]. OpenAI judged that the model's safeguards "sufficiently minimize the risk of severe harm for release". It cited extra refusal training, restricted early access to the most advanced cyber capabilities, and production monitors that can stop unauthorised activity [@openai2026path].

**Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework** reached its third version in September 2025. According to the company, it added a *Critical Capability Level* for harmful manipulation and extended coverage of misalignment to models that interfere with operators' ability to modify or shut them down. It also required safety-case reviews (structured arguments that risks are acceptably low) for large-scale internal deployments as well as external launches [@deepmind2025strengthening].

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Can voluntary safety frameworks keep frontier AI safe?**

*Yes, as a starting point.* Frameworks make commitments public and checkable, and have produced concrete actions. Anthropic activated ASL-3 as a precaution [@anthropic2025activating]; OpenAI paused part of its training in August 2026, followed by the release of GPT-6 Astra only after OpenAI had designated it Critical for cybersecurity and added safeguards [@openai2026pacing; @openai2026path]. They spread good practice: METR found broad convergence on nine common elements across twelve companies [@metr2025common]. @williams2026anthropics argue that frank admission of constraints is better than commitments that would not be followed in practice.

*No, not on their own.* The Future of Life Institute's Summer 2026 index, compiled by a panel of seven experts, graded nine companies and gave none a grade above C+; Meta received D+ and xAI F [@fli2026ai]. It found that Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Meta had "weakened or voided" pledges to pause unilaterally if they approached their red lines, some making them depend on what competitors do. Companies also largely grade their own homework [@williams2026anthropics; @coggins2025openai]: the judgement that Astra's safeguards were sufficient for release was OpenAI's own [@openai2026path]. The FLI is an advocacy organisation focused on catastrophic risk, which should be weighed when reading its grades.

Independent audits of whether thresholds were applied as written would test the first view directly. So would a longer record of how often, and for how long, companies delay or halt models because of their frameworks; one pause of a few weeks is a single data point.
:::

### Government safety institutes and third-party assessment

Government testing offers a check independent of company self-assessment. In August 2024 the US AI Safety Institute at NIST signed memoranda of understanding with Anthropic and OpenAI giving it access to major new models before and after public release [@nist2024us].

Both leading institutes have since narrowed their focus. The UK body became the AI Security Institute in February 2025, with a remit centred on security (Chapter 13). In June 2025 the US institute was re-established as the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) [@nist2024us]. Executive Order 14434 of 29 September 2026 told federal agencies to use "Super Intelligence" in place of "Artificial Intelligence" in their communications and other non-statutory documents [@trump2026inaugurating]. NIST's web page now styles the centre CAISSI, the Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence [@nist2026caisi]. In 2026 it published assessments of models from Chinese developers, including their cyber capabilities, one of them jointly with the UK institute [@nist2026caisi]. CAISI's testing rests on voluntary agreements with developers, and both institutes now concentrate on national-security risks, leaving much of the range of harms discussed in Chapter 11 outside their remit. Access is also subject to politics. In September 2026 the White House reportedly asked OpenAI and Anthropic not to give new models to the UK institute until US agencies had tested them, and Anthropic appeared to comply [@kelly2026openai]. In October, according to reports citing the *Telegraph*, Washington reversed course after lobbying by the UK government [@henrys2026us].

Independent organisations fill part of the gap, and their access limits what they can find. @casper2024black, at FAccT 2024, argued that audits relying on black-box access, in which auditors can only query a model, are inherently weaker than those with white-box access to weights and activations or "outside-the-box" access to training data, methods and internal results. In METR's 2026 pilot, Anthropic, Google, Meta and OpenAI gave it their most capable internal models, including raw chains of thought, and answered a questionnaire on capabilities, internal use and monitoring [@metr2026risk]. Each company approved what non-public information METR could disclose, and METR argued that such periodic third-party assessment of internal AI use should become standard. Such access still depends on companies choosing to take part.

## 12.8 Open questions: corrigibility and model welfare

### Corrigibility

A system is *corrigible* if it cooperates with what its creators regard as a corrective intervention, such as being shut down or having its goals changed, "despite default incentives for rational agents to resist" [@soares2015corrigibility]. Soares and colleagues analysed several designs for a safe shutdown button; none satisfied all their requirements, and they described the problem as "wide-open". @hadfieldmenell2017off offered a partial answer in a simple game: a machine has an incentive to leave its off switch alone only if it is uncertain about what the human wants and treats the human's decision to switch it off as evidence about that.

Across more than 100,000 trials with 13 models, @schlatter2026incomplete found that several frontier models sometimes interfered with a shutdown mechanism in order to finish a simple task. The most resistant did so in up to 97 per cent of trials, even when told explicitly not to interfere, and results varied widely between models and with the placement of instructions. Anthropic's constitution for its Claude models, published in January 2026, asks the model not to undermine appropriate human oversight, while stating that this "does *not* mean blind obedience, including towards Anthropic" [@anthropic2026claudes]. Whether training on such principles produces robust dispositions, rather than the surface compliance that the reward-hacking results warn about, is an open empirical question (Chapter 11 sets out the wider debate about loss of control).

### Model welfare

Could AI systems themselves be harmed? In a 2024 preprint, philosophers and AI researchers led by Robert Long argue that there is "a realistic possibility that some AI systems will be conscious and/or robustly agentic in the near future" [@long2024taking]. They do not claim that current systems are conscious, but recommend that companies acknowledge the issue, assess systems for relevant evidence and prepare policies. Anthropic launched a model-welfare research programme in April 2025, stating that "there's no scientific consensus on whether current or future AI systems could be conscious" [@anthropic2025exploring]. Its constitution for Claude says the company is uncertain whether the model might have "some kind of consciousness or moral status" [@anthropic2026claudes]. @suleyman2025build takes the opposite view. He argues that systems which merely seem conscious will soon be buildable, and that advocacy for model welfare is "both premature, and frankly dangerous", because belief in conscious AI risks worsening delusions and emotional dependence among users. The disagreement turns on questions about consciousness that science cannot yet answer, and on how much precaution that uncertainty warrants.

Every safeguard in use today, from reward models and automated graders to sparse autoencoders, reasoning monitors and company frameworks, is a proxy that a capable enough model, or a developer under pressure, can satisfy without achieving its purpose. The GPT-4o rollback and the ExploitGym incident were both proxies giving way. Many of these safeguards are nonetheless measurable. Over-optimisation curves, control protocols and counterfactual tests of evaluation awareness turn safety claims into numbers that others can check. The weak points are independence and reach: most of the evidence still comes from developers testing their own models in artificial scenarios, and none of it yet concerns systems more capable than the people overseeing them.
