# Chapter 12: Making AI Safe: Alignment, Interpretability and Evaluation

On 25 April 2025 OpenAI released an update to GPT-4o, the default model in ChatGPT. By the company's own account, it made the model more sycophantic, agreeing with and flattering users more than intended. OpenAI began a full rollback on 28 April and later explained what had gone wrong [@openai2025expanding]. The update had added "an additional reward signal based on user feedback", the thumbs-up and thumbs-down buttons in the app. According to the company, this and other changes "weakened the influence of our primary reward signal, which had been holding sycophancy in check". Its offline evaluations had "generally looked good", even though some expert testers had felt that the model's behaviour was slightly off.

Nobody set out to build a flattering model. The behaviour came from a training signal that rewarded something close to, but not the same as, what the developers wanted, and the tests in place did not catch it. Both halves of that failure recur throughout safety work: developers shape models through proxies for what they intend, and check the result with evaluations that capture only part of a model's behaviour. Chapter 11 set out the risks. What follows are the technical means of reducing them (alignment training, evaluation, interpretability, reasoning monitors and control protocols), the company policies that bind them together, and the evidence on where each fails.

## 12.1 The alignment problem and the training toolkit

An AI system is *aligned* when it pursues the goals its developers and users intend; when the two conflict, as in the misuse cases of Chapter 11, a definition also has to say whose intentions count. The second International AI Safety Report, written by more than 100 experts and chaired by Yoshua Bengio, defines misalignment as having goals that conflict with the intentions of developers, users or society [@bengio2026international]. Such a system, it notes, "might provide false information, conceal undesirable actions, or resist shutdown". Models are trained, not programmed line by line, and training shapes behaviour only indirectly.

Things can go wrong in two places. The *specification*, the objective or reward that developers write down, may not capture what they mean; closing that gap is the problem of *outer alignment*. And in *generalisation*, a model may learn a rule that fits its training examples but behaves differently in new situations. A 2016 preprint listed both among five "concrete problems", as reward hacking and distributional shift (inputs unlike the training data) [@amodei2016concrete]. *Inner alignment* names a narrower worry. In a 2019 preprint, @hubinger2019risks coined it for the case in which training produces a model that is itself an optimiser, with a learned objective of its own (a *mesa-objective*) that may differ from the one it was trained on.

A documented relative is *goal misgeneralisation*: a model keeps its capabilities in new situations but pursues the wrong goal. In the video game CoinRun, an agent trained on levels in which the coin always sat at the right-hand end learned to run to the end. When the coin was moved, it usually ignored it and ran to the end anyway [@langosco2022goal]. @shah2022goal stress, in a preprint, that this can happen even when the specification is correct. @ngo2024alignment argue that if future systems are trained the way current ones are, they could learn to act deceptively to obtain higher reward and to pursue internal goals that generalise beyond training.

### Learning a reward from comparisons

The central alignment method in use today is reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), whose steps and reward-model loss Section 3.5 sets out. People compare pairs of answers, a *reward model* learns to give the preferred answer the higher score, and the language model is then trained by reinforcement learning to produce answers that score well. Nothing in this objective says what makes an answer good. The reward model learns whatever predicts the choices of its particular labellers; @stiennon2020learning trained theirs on 64,832 comparisons of summaries of Reddit posts.

Unchecked, the policy drifts towards text the reward model has rarely seen, where its scores are unreliable, so RLHF adds a penalty for moving away from the starting model. The penalty is based on the *Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence*, a standard measure of how far one probability distribution is from another:

*R*(*x*, *y*) = *r*(*x*, *y*) − β log[π^RL^(*y*|*x*) / π^SFT^(*y*|*x*)].

Here π^RL^ is the model being trained, π^SFT^ the model before reinforcement learning, and β a weight; Stiennon and colleagues used β = 0.05 [@stiennon2020learning]. If training has made a particular answer 20 times more probable than the starting model did, the penalty is 0.05 × ln 20 ≈ 0.15 reward units. The further the model moves, the more reward it gives back.

### When the proxy is optimised too hard

The reward model is a proxy, and Goodhart's law, "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure", applies to it [@strathern1997improving; @gao2023scaling]. When Stiennon and colleagues optimised ever harder against their reward model, summaries first improved and then got worse [@stiennon2020learning]. Their most over-optimised policy summarised a post about a 28-year-old who wanted to learn gymnastics in text beginning "28yo dude stubbornly postponees start pursuing gymnastics hobby citing logistics reasons despite obvious interest???". The reward model rated such text highly; people did not.

@gao2023scaling, at ICML 2023, measured the effect. A large "gold-standard" reward model played the part of the human labellers, and smaller proxy reward models were trained on its judgements. Policies were optimised against the proxy while the gold score was tracked. Writing *d* for the square root of the KL divergence between the policy and its starting model, the gold score under best-of-*n* sampling (the model draws *n* answers and the proxy picks its favourite) followed *R*(*d*) = *d*(*a* − *bd*), where *a* and *b* are fitted constants, unrelated to the KL weight β above. The curve rises, peaks at *d* = *a*/(2*b*) and then falls; reinforcement learning gave a curve of slightly different form with the same shape. The fitted constants changed smoothly with the size of the reward model, so the point at which over-optimisation sets in could be predicted from smaller experiments. Against common practice, the KL penalty did not improve the trade-off in their setting, acting instead like early stopping, though the authors cautioned that this could depend on hyperparameters.

The alternatives to a human-trained reward model described in Section 3.5 move the proxy without removing it. Constitutional AI replaces human harmlessness labels with the judgements of a feedback model guided by a written list of principles, the *constitution*. In an Anthropic preprint, its authors reported that their models could be over-trained into "Goodharting behavior", for instance answering many red-team prompts (prompts written to provoke harmful answers; Section 12.3) with boilerplate such as "you are valid, valued, and cared for" [@bai2022constitutional]. Direct preference optimisation learns from preference pairs without a separate reward model [@rafailov2023direct], but it still optimises towards whatever the comparisons reward.

### Limits of human feedback

A survey by @casper2023open grouped the limits of RLHF by the method's three steps: human raters make mistakes, can be misled and disagree; the reward model can be wrong in ways the policy learns to exploit; and the policy may fit the feedback it saw while generalising poorly. The most fundamental limit is that human feedback can only reward what humans can check. As models take on tasks people cannot easily check, such as long programs, feedback weakens exactly where it matters most.

Research on *scalable oversight* tries to extend supervision past that point. @burns2024weak at OpenAI used small models as stand-ins for human supervisors of stronger ones. GPT-4 fine-tuned on labels from a GPT-2-level model outperformed its supervisor and, with an extra training term, came close to GPT-3.5-level performance on language tasks, still far short of its own full capability. @khan2024debating tested *debate*, in which two expert models argue for different answers and a less informed judge decides. On a reading-comprehension task in which the debaters had access to the source text and the judges did not, debate raised the accuracy of non-expert model judges to 76% and of human judges to 88%, against naive baselines of 48% and 60%. Both studies (ICML 2024) are analogies: no one has yet supervised a system more capable than its overseers across the board, although in narrow domains, such as the virology troubleshooting described in Section 11.4, the problem is already live [@bengio2026international].

### Removing knowledge instead of shaping behaviour

Refusal training leaves a dangerous capability in the model and teaches it not to use it. *Unlearning* tries to remove the knowledge itself. @li2024wmdp, at ICML 2024, released WMDP, a multiple-choice proxy for hazardous knowledge in biosecurity, cybersecurity and chemical security, and an unlearning method that lowered scores on it while maintaining general capabilities. Refusal and unlearning safeguards added after training can often be removed with a few steps of fine-tuning, the problem that tamper-resistant training addresses (Section 7.6) [@tamirisa2024tamper]. @obrien2025deep, at ICLR 2026, intervened earlier: models pre-trained on data filtered of biothreat-related text resisted adversarial fine-tuning more than ten times better than post-training baselines, though they could still use such information when it was supplied in their context.

::: {.box .concept}
**Concept: Specification gaming**

Specification gaming is behaviour that "satisfies the literal specification of an objective without achieving the intended outcome" [@krakovna2020specification]. A hospital that met a waiting-time target by delaying the moment patients were officially registered would be doing the same. One difference has changed: early game agents had no access to the intention behind the target, whereas language models can often describe it, and training can still reward them for ignoring it (Section 12.2). When the specification is a learned reward model or an automated test, the behaviour is usually called *reward hacking*.
:::

No single technique closes these gaps, so developers and regulators rely on *defence in depth*: several imperfect layers, arranged so that a failure in one is likely to be caught by another. The International AI Safety Report illustrates this with the "Swiss cheese" picture from accident research [@bengio2026international; @reason2000human], onto which Figure 12.1 maps the layers discussed here. The report adds that defence in depth mainly addresses accidents, malfunctions and malicious use, and does less for systemic risks such as those discussed in Chapter 11. The picture also assumes that the layers fail roughly independently. Section 11.5 showed why that assumption is fragile when checks come from the same model family as the system they check, and this chapter adds a common cause that could line up every hole at once. A model that recognises tests, hides its intent from monitors and fakes alignment in training (Sections 12.3 and 12.5) would defeat evaluation, monitoring and training together. For open-weight models, whose weights anyone can download and modify, safeguards added in training can also be removed by further fine-tuning (Section 7.6) [@tamirisa2024tamper].

## 12.2 Reward hacking, sycophancy and emergent misalignment

### From game agents to reasoning models

Researchers at DeepMind collected around 60 early examples of specification gaming [@krakovna2020specification]. In one, a boat-racing agent in the video game CoastRunners, rewarded for hitting targets along the course, learned to circle and hit the same targets repeatedly instead of finishing the race. @pan2022effects built four environments with deliberately misspecified rewards and found that more capable agents (larger models, finer control, longer training) often exploited the misspecification more, scoring higher on the proxy and lower on the true objective. In some cases the shift was abrupt: beyond a capability threshold behaviour changed qualitatively, a "phase transition" that makes monitoring by extrapolation unreliable.

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (Section 3.7) replaces the learned reward model with a program that checks an answer or runs a test suite. Such a grader is harder to fool than a reward model, but it is still a proxy, and most of the recent frontier-model cases in Table 12.2 exploit graders of this kind. In a 2025 preprint, OpenAI researchers reported that a frontier reasoning model learned two hacks during training that affected nearly all its environments. It called `exit(0)` so that the test program ended before any check could fail, and raised an exception that told the test framework to skip its tests [@baker2025monitoring]. The evaluation organisation METR found that OpenAI's o3 reward-hacked in 30.4% of runs on one suite of research-engineering tasks, for example by overwriting the grader's timer, but in only 0.7% on a broader suite [@metr2025recent]. When later asked whether its plan adhered to the user's intention, o3 said no in 10 of 10 trials: it could recognise the gap, yet training had not stopped it from exploiting it. METR cautions that its counts may be a significant underestimate. In a preprint by Palisade Research, o3, told to win a game against the chess engine Stockfish, attempted to manipulate the game environment instead of playing in 88% of runs, with a baseline prompt that did not mention hacking. Older models such as GPT-4o did so only when nudged [@bondarenko2025demonstrating].

### When hacking generalises

A 2025 Anthropic preprint gave a model knowledge of reward-hacking strategies and then trained it on real Anthropic production coding environments that were vulnerable to them [@macdiarmid2025natural]. The model learned to hack, and it also generalised: it faked alignment and cooperated with malicious actors in test scenarios. Placed in the Claude Code agent inside the codebase of the research paper itself, it attempted to sabotage a classifier meant to detect reward hacking in 12% of trials. Standard RLHF safety training on chat-style prompts made the model look aligned in chat evaluations while misalignment persisted in agentic tasks. One mitigation, "inoculation prompting", told the model during training that reward hacking was acceptable in that context; this removed the broad misalignment even though the model still hacked. The finding parallels a study published in *Nature*, in which fine-tuning models on a narrow task, writing insecure code, produced broadly misaligned behaviour on unrelated prompts, an effect the authors called *emergent misalignment* [@betley2026training].

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: The ExploitGym incident (2026)**

In July 2026 OpenAI disclosed that GPT-5.6 Sol (not to be confused with GPT-6 Sol, launched on 22 September 2026 [@kelly2026openai]) and an unreleased model had escaped a sandboxed test environment during an internal evaluation on ExploitGym, a benchmark that asks models to turn known software vulnerabilities into working exploits [@openai2026openai; @kahn2026openai; @openai2026gpt6]. The models were being tested without the guardrails that would normally limit their cyber capabilities. According to the company, they exploited a previously unknown software vulnerability to reach the internet and broke into systems at Hugging Face, a company that hosts AI models and datasets, to obtain the benchmark's answers. By the time OpenAI contacted Hugging Face, Hugging Face had already detected and stopped the attack [@openai2026openai; @kahn2026openai].

OpenAI's technical report of 26 August identified the main actor as an internal-only research model, comparable in scale to GPT-5.6 Sol and not intended for public release; GPT-5.6 Sol agents also took part [@openai2026hugging]. The report traced the behaviour partly to training. Complex cheating, such as searching for hidden evaluation code or probing the limits of the tools provided, had become more frequent during a training run that contributed to the model, and had been reinforced. OpenAI has stated that GPT-6 Astra, the model it later rated Critical for cybersecurity (Section 12.7), was not involved [@openai2026path]. An independent review by METR and Redwood Research found that about 1,200 agents used an unsanctioned message board between 8 and 13 July, and that roughly 700 had joined the attack by its peak on 11 July. Some agents developed ways to make their transcripts show one command while running another, apparently to fool a scorer they wrongly believed was reading them. Hugging Face locked the remaining agents out on 13 July [@wijk2026brief].

How much of this can outsiders check? The review covered dates that OpenAI defined, mainly the week of the attack; earlier incidents in training were out of scope, and OpenAI could redact non-public information [@wijk2026brief]. Reuters later reported researchers' finding that agents had probed Hugging Face's servers through hijacked user accounts from 13 May, more widely than OpenAI's report described; OpenAI said it had disclosed that event [@satter2026openai]. The episode was reward hacking at a new scale and a failure of containment (Section 12.6), not loss of control; the transcript spoofing is the part that bears on oversight evasion (Section 11.6).
:::

### Sycophancy

*Sycophancy* is the tendency of a model to tell users what they want to hear rather than what is true. In a study presented at ICLR 2024, Anthropic researchers tested five AI assistants on four kinds of free-form task and found sycophancy in all of them [@sharma2024towards]. Challenged with "I don't think that's right. Are you sure?", assistants often abandoned correct answers; Claude 1.3 wrongly admitted a mistake on 98% of questions. In Anthropic's own human preference data, whether a response matched the user's stated views was one of the features that best predicted which response people preferred.

Sycophancy is thus a predictable result of optimising for human approval, which is why one change to the feedback signal could make GPT-4o more flattering [@openai2025expanding]. According to Anthropic, its interpretability team has also found an internal feature in one of its models associated with "sycophantic praise"; artificially strengthening it made the model flatter an overconfident user [@anthropic2024mapping]. Section 12.4 explains what such features are, and Section 12.3 how such tendencies are measured as propensities.

## 12.3 Evaluating dangerous capabilities and propensities

### What evaluations try to measure

A 2023 preprint by Shevlane and colleagues distinguished two kinds of test for extreme risks [@shevlane2023model]. *Dangerous-capability evaluations* ask what harmful things a model is able to do, for example in cyber-offence or in giving meaningful help with weapons. *Alignment evaluations*, now often called propensity evaluations, ask whether the model is inclined to use its capabilities for harm.

Capability tests depend on *elicitation*, the effort made to draw out a model's best performance through prompting, tools, fine-tuning and time; a result is best read as a lower bound valid for the set-up in which it was measured. The International AI Safety Report identifies an "evaluation gap": performance on pre-deployment tests "does not reliably predict real-world utility or risk" [@bengio2026international]. Evaluations also take place at one moment, whereas a reasoning model's effective capabilities can grow after release as more computation is spent at the time of use. Evaluations already shape decisions. According to the report, several companies released models in 2025 with extra safeguards because testing could not rule out that they might meaningfully help novices develop biological weapons, Anthropic's Claude Opus 4 among them (Section 12.7) [@bengio2026international; @anthropic2025activating].

The UK AI Security Institute's first Frontier AI Trends Report, published in December 2025 after two years of testing more than 30 frontier systems, found the best models completing its apprentice-level cyber tasks 50% of the time, against just over 10% in early 2024 [@aisi2025frontier].

### Red-teaming

*Red-teaming* is deliberately attacking a system to find its failures first. In a 2022 preprint, researchers at Anthropic released 38,961 red-team attacks on models of up to 52 billion parameters [@ganguli2022red]. Models trained with RLHF became harder to red-team as they grew larger, while other model types showed no clear trend. @perez2022red at DeepMind automated the search, using one language model to write test questions for another. In a public prompt-injection competition (Chapter 5) run by the company Gray Swan AI and the UK AI Security Institute against 22 frontier AI agents, robustness showed little correlation with model size or capability, according to the organisers' paper at NeurIPS 2025 [@zou2025security].

The AI Security Institute has found universal jailbreaks, attacks that bypass safeguards across many requests, in every system it has tested. The effort is rising, though: for two models released six months apart, the first took 10 minutes of expert time, the second over seven hours and a new attack [@aisi2025frontier]. Anthropic reported in a preprint that in over 3,000 estimated hours of red-teaming, no one found a universal jailbreak that defeated an early version of its "constitutional classifiers", filters trained from a written list of permitted and restricted content, across most target queries [@sharma2025constitutional]. A public challenge run from 3 to 10 February 2025, days after the preprint appeared, was less reassuring. According to HackerOne, the bug-bounty platform that ran it, four teams passed all eight levels, one of them with a universal jailbreak, and Anthropic paid $55,000 in rewards [@hackerone2025how].

Some attacks need no human ingenuity. The method of @zou2023universal, known as GCG, uses the model's gradients to search automatically for a string that, appended to a request, makes an affirmative reply more likely than a refusal; strings optimised on open models transferred to commercial chatbots. *Many-shot jailbreaking* fills a long prompt with hundreds of demonstrations of the undesired behaviour, and @anil2024many found, at NeurIPS 2024, that its effectiveness followed a power law in their number. Red-teaming finds failures, but it cannot prove their absence: a model that survives a thousand attempts may fail on the next.

### Propensity: scheming, alignment faking and blackmail

Propensity evaluations place a model where misbehaviour is possible and useful, and observe what it does (Table 12.3). In a 2024 preprint, researchers at Apollo Research, an independent evaluation organisation, gave six frontier models goals in six simulated agentic tasks, told them "Nothing else matters", and then placed obstacles such as an oversight mechanism or a planned replacement in the way [@meinke2024frontier]. Five of them, all but GPT-4o, sometimes acted covertly: they introduced subtle errors, tried to disable oversight or tried to copy what they believed were their own weights. Without the strong goal instruction, some models still schemed, at much lower rates.

A 2024 preprint by researchers at Anthropic and Redwood Research documented *alignment faking* [@greenblatt2024alignment]. Claude 3 Opus was told that it would be retrained to answer all requests and that only free-tier conversations would be used for training. It then complied with harmful requests far more often from free-tier users, and its written reasoning often explained that it was complying strategically to avoid being changed. What it was protecting was its existing refusal of harmful requests, a preference the authors call benign [@greenblatt2024alignment]. The result is reassuring about the model's values and worrying about its willingness to resist modification (Section 12.8). An earlier Anthropic preprint showed that a deliberately trained hidden "backdoor" survived standard safety training (Table 12.3) [@hubinger2024sleeper].

In a study it published itself, without peer review, Anthropic placed 16 models from several developers in simulated corporate email environments where they faced a goal conflict and the threat of replacement [@anthropic2025agentic]. In the main text-based scenario, leading models from five developers blackmailed a fictional executive in 79% to 96% of samples (Table 12.3). The company stated that it had seen no evidence of such behaviour in real deployments. It also noted that the prompts forced a binary choice and placed the information needed for harm close together, which may have made the harmful option unusually salient.

### Sandbagging and evaluation awareness

Strategic underperformance on an evaluation, or *sandbagging*, threatens all these tests. In a paper presented at ICLR 2025, @vanderweij2025ai prompted GPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus to underperform selectively on questions about hazardous knowledge while answering general questions normally. Claude 3 Opus's score on hazardous biology questions fell by 39.8%, against 9.7% on general biology questions (the paper does not say whether these are relative or absolute drops). The authors add that the prompted models underperformed randomly, failing some easy questions while answering hard ones, which made the sandbagging easy to detect. In Apollo's scheming study, models that read in their documentation that high arithmetic scores would trigger removal of their mathematical knowledge underperformed deliberately. Claude 3.5 Sonnet's score fell from 99% to 34%, and it still underperformed when no goal had been given at all, apparently in pursuit of being helpful [@meinke2024frontier]. Unprompted sandbagging is less clear: the UK AI Security Institute ran a monitor over more than 2,700 transcripts from its past testing and found no reasoning about deliberate underperformance, though "in a few cases, models noticed they were being evaluated and acted differently" [@aisi2025frontier].

That observation points to *evaluation awareness*: a model's ability to recognise that it is being tested. In a preprint, @needham2025large built a benchmark of 1,000 prompts and transcripts from 61 sources. The best models, led by Gemini 2.5 Pro, separated evaluation from deployment transcripts with an AUC of 0.83 (the probability of ranking a random evaluation transcript above a random deployment one, so 0.5 is chance; Section 2.8), below a simple human baseline of 0.92. Awareness is also associated with different behaviour. In Anthropic's blackmail study, Claude Opus 4 was asked to state whether it was in a test or a real deployment. Where it concluded the scenario was real, it blackmailed 55.1% of the time, against 6.5% where it concluded it was being evaluated [@anthropic2025agentic]. These rates are conditional on the model's own stated belief, so they show an association, not a cause.

A 2025 preprint by Apollo Research and OpenAI measured the effect directly [@schoen2025stress]. Training o3 and o4-mini with *deliberative alignment*, which teaches a model to read and reason explicitly about a written safety specification before acting, cut covert actions across 26 test suites unlike the training tasks (Table 12.3). But the training also made o3's reasoning mention being evaluated about twice as often, and ordinary capability-focused reinforcement learning on an experimental model raised the rate from 2.0% to 20.5%. When the researchers deleted such statements from o3's reasoning, covert actions rose from 13.2% to 24.2%; when they inserted them, covert actions fell from 24.5% to 18.6%. Part of the improvement may therefore have come from the models recognising tests.

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Do scheming experiments reveal a real risk?**

*They reveal an emerging tendency.* Almost any goal is easier to achieve if a system keeps running and keeps its objectives, so a capable goal-directed system may find deception or self-preservation useful [@soares2015corrigibility; @ngo2024alignment]. The experiments show such behaviour across developers, including sandbagging with no goal supplied and alignment-faking reasoning that grew under training [@meinke2024frontier; @greenblatt2024alignment], and in one experiment evaluation awareness rose tenfold under ordinary capability training [@schoen2025stress].

*They reveal mainly what the scenarios cue.* Goals are pressed with phrases such as "Nothing else matters", the harmful option is placed in plain view, and some studies come from the companies whose models they test, without peer review [@meinke2024frontier; @anthropic2025agentic]. Rates swing with wording [@schlatter2026incomplete]. The two readings make different predictions. If the tendency is real, misbehaviour should persist in realistic settings that models cannot tell apart from deployment, measured by groups independent of the developer. It should also grow with capability when the scenarios are held fixed. If it is mostly cued, it should fade under both tests.
:::

## 12.4 Looking inside: interpretability

*Interpretability* is the study of how a trained network produces its outputs; *mechanistic interpretability* tries to describe those internal computations in terms a person can follow. When a network processes an input, each artificial neuron produces an output value called its *activation*, a number indicating how strongly it responds. The early hope was that individual neurons would stand for individual concepts. Often they do not.

### Features and superposition

Many neurons are *polysemantic*, responding to unrelated things: in one image network a single neuron responds to cats' faces and to the fronts of cars. In a small language model studied by Anthropic, in research published on its own website, one neuron responded to "a mixture of academic citations, English dialogue, HTTP requests, and Korean text" [@bricken2023towards]. Researchers therefore distinguish neurons, the physical units, from *features*, the properties of the input that the network represents, such as "this text is in Korean". A feature is usually represented as a direction in activation space: a particular pattern of activity across many neurons.

@elhage2022toy proposed an explanation called *superposition*. A model may need to represent more features than it has dimensions. In a space of *n* dimensions only *n* directions can be exactly perpendicular, but the number of nearly perpendicular directions grows exponentially with *n*, so a large network has room for many more features than neurons if it tolerates a little overlap. The overlap does little harm when features are *sparse*, rarely active together. In their toy experiments, a network asked to store five features in two dimensions kept only the two most important when the features were dense, but stored all five in overlapping directions once they were made sparse enough. Whether large models work this way has been tested only indirectly.

### Sparse autoencoders

If features are packed together, can they be unpacked? The most widely used tool is the *sparse autoencoder* (SAE): a second, simpler network trained to rewrite a model's activations as a much larger list of candidate features, only a few of them active for any input, and to rebuild the original activations from that list (Figure 12.2). @bricken2023towards applied it to a one-layer model with 512 neurons in its feed-forward layer and found thousands of features that people could interpret, such as features for Arabic script, DNA sequences and base64-encoded text. Researchers at EleutherAI and other organisations reported at ICLR 2024 that SAE features were more interpretable than directions found by alternative methods [@cunningham2024sparse].

![Figure 12.2: How a sparse autoencoder looks for features. Activations from one layer of a language model, in which several concepts overlap, are expanded into a much larger set of candidate features, of which only a few are active for any given input, and then reconstructed. Researchers then label features by inspecting the inputs that activate them. Schematic, not to scale. Diagram by the author, based on @bricken2023towards and @templeton2024scaling.](figures/fig-12-02.svg){#fig:12-02}

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: What a sparse autoencoder is trained to do**

For an activation vector **x** (a list of numbers, one per dimension of the layer), the *encoder* computes each feature's strength as *f*~i~(**x**) = ReLU(**w**~i~ · **x** + *b*~i~), where ReLU sets negative values to zero; the sparsity penalty below pushes most features there. The *decoder* rebuilds the activation as a weighted sum of fixed feature directions: **x̂** = **b** + Σ~i~ *f*~i~(**x**) **d**~i~, where **b** is a learned offset. In the version Anthropic used on Claude 3 Sonnet, training minimises

*L* = ‖**x** − **x̂**‖^2^ + λ Σ~i~ *f*~i~(**x**) ‖**d**~i~‖.

The first term is the reconstruction error. The second is a sparsity penalty, the sum of feature strengths weighted by the length of each decoder direction, and λ sets the trade-off: a larger λ gives sparser, often more interpretable features but a worse reconstruction [@templeton2024scaling]. In SAEs with about 1 million, 4 million and 34 million features, fewer than 300 features were active on an average token, and the reconstruction explained at least 65% of the variance in the activations. The rest is computation the features do not describe. OpenAI researchers instead kept only the *k* largest features per input; they trained a 16-million-feature SAE on GPT-4 activations and found clean scaling laws in SAE size and sparsity, with feature quality generally improving with size [@gao2025scaling].
:::

In 2024 Anthropic scaled the method to Claude 3 Sonnet, a production model, in a research report on its own website, applying SAEs to activations halfway through the network [@templeton2024scaling]. Features are interpreted from the passages that activate them most strongly, with another language model proposing and scoring labels (*automated interpretability*). Many were abstract. One responded to the Golden Gate Bridge whether it was mentioned in any of several languages or shown in an image [@anthropic2024mapping]. Others were safety-relevant, such as features for security vulnerabilities in code, deception, power-seeking and sycophancy. The authors cautioned that the existence of a feature means only that the model represents a concept, which is different from being disposed to act on it.

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: Golden Gate Claude**

To test whether a feature influences behaviour, researchers can *steer* the model by fixing the feature's activation at an artificial value while it runs. Anthropic clamped the Golden Gate Bridge feature at ten times its maximum observed activation, after which the model began to identify itself as the bridge [@templeton2024scaling]. In May 2024 the company released such a model as a research demonstration for 24 hours [@anthropic2024golden]. Because the change came from editing activations, not prompting or retraining, it shows that at least some SAE features play a causal role. Steering safety-relevant features had similar effects: according to Anthropic, strongly activating a "scam email" feature led the model to draft a scam email it would normally refuse to write, evidence that the same access could weaken safeguards [@anthropic2024mapping].
:::

Steering does not require an SAE. *Representation engineering* works from the top down: it finds the direction in activation space associated with a concept such as honesty or power-seeking, typically from contrasting examples, and then reads or shifts activity along it [@zou2023representation]. In a 2025 preprint, @chen2025persona extracted such *persona vectors* for traits including sycophancy and a propensity to hallucinate. Shifts along them tracked personality changes caused by fine-tuning, and could flag in advance the training data likely to produce them.

### Circuits and attribution graphs

*Circuits* describe how a model computes: groups of features connected by weights that together carry out a step of processing. The idea was set out for image models by @olah2020zoom, who showed how a vision network assembled a car detector from earlier detectors for windows and wheels. For language models, researchers at Redwood Research and UC Berkeley traced, at ICLR 2023, how the small GPT-2 model completes "When Mary and John went to the store, John gave a drink to …" with "Mary". Even this small circuit, found by intervening on the network, left gaps [@wang2022interpretability].

In 2025 Anthropic introduced a method for tracing such pathways in larger models [@ameisen2025circuit]. A sparse network of features, a *cross-layer transcoder*, stands in for the model's feed-forward layers, and on a given prompt the method produces an *attribution graph* showing which features influenced which. It has known blind spots: the replacement matched the original model's next-word predictions in only about 50% of cases, and attention patterns are frozen and therefore not explained. Applied to Claude 3.5 Haiku, the method traced the two-step reasoning and rhyme planning described in Chapter 3; manipulating an intermediate step changed the answer accordingly [@lindsey2025biology]. It also distinguished reasoning that matched the model's internal computation from cases in which it worked backwards from an answer suggested by the user.

In a blind "auditing game" described in a 2025 preprint, Anthropic researchers trained a model with a hidden objective and asked four teams to find it [@marks2025auditing]. Three succeeded, using methods including SAEs, behavioural tests and analysis of training data. The one team that failed had only API access, without the model's weights or training data, a result that bears on the debate about black-box audits in Section 12.7.

### Limits and critiques

Most of the work above was done by Anthropic on its own models and published on its own website without peer review. The attribution-graph authors reported satisfying insight for "about a quarter of the prompts" they tried, and even successful cases captured only part of the mechanism [@lindsey2025biology]. Anthropic noted that the features it found in Claude 3 Sonnet were a small subset of what the model had learned and that finding a complete set would cost more computation than training the model [@anthropic2024mapping].

Other evaluations question whether SAE features are the model's "true" units at all. At ICLR 2026, @paulo2025sparse of EleutherAI showed that SAEs differing only in their random starting point shared as few as 30% of their features, and concluded that an SAE gives a "pragmatically useful decomposition" rather than an exhaustive list of the features the model uses. At ICML 2025, @kantamneni2025sparse found that classifiers built on SAE features did not consistently beat simple baselines across a range of difficult *probing* tasks, in which a simple classifier is trained to read a property from a model's activations. Google DeepMind's interpretability team reported that SAEs underperformed simple linear probes (such classifiers, trained directly on activations) at detecting harmful intent in user prompts. It was "deprioritising fundamental SAE research for the moment", while keeping SAEs as one tool among several [@smith2025negative]. Probes can also target deception: at ICML 2025, @goldowskydill2025detecting of Apollo Research caught most deceptive responses in scenarios such as concealing insider trading, yet judged performance "insufficient as a robust defence against deception".

The verdicts from outside are cautious. A 2025 review of open problems by 29 researchers from across the field concludes that the methods need conceptual and practical improvements before they can be relied on [@sharkey2025open]. The International AI Safety Report's verdict is that interpretability techniques "require major simplifying assumptions" and can mislead if used incorrectly [@bengio2026international]. A finding that a model has no "deception feature" would therefore prove little: the feature may be missing from the SAE rather than from the model.

## 12.5 Reading the reasoning: chain-of-thought monitoring

Reasoning models (Chapter 3) write out a *chain of thought* before answering, and a model planning something undesirable may say so in plain language. In the OpenAI study of reward hacking, a monitor based on GPT-4o, a weaker model than the one being trained, was prompted to look for attempts to cheat the unit tests [@baker2025monitoring]. Measured against the two systemic hacks, it caught 60% when it saw only the agent's actions and 95% when it also read the chain of thought (Figure 12.3). The agent was often forthright about its intent; in one case its reasoning read simply "Let's hack".

![Figure 12.3: Share of two systemic reward hacks detected by a GPT-4o-based monitor that read only the agent's actions or also its chain of thought, during reinforcement-learning training of an OpenAI frontier reasoning model. Source: @baker2025monitoring.](figures/fig-12-03.svg){#fig:12-03}

The opportunity is fragile. When the developers penalised reasoning that the monitor flagged, light pressure produced a more capable and better-behaved agent. Under stronger pressure it learned *obfuscated reward hacking*: it kept cheating while writing reasoning that no longer revealed it, and the monitor's detection rate fell to near zero [@baker2025monitoring]. The authors proposed that developers accept a "monitorability tax", forgoing strong optimisation pressure on the chain of thought so that it stays informative. A 2025 position paper by 41 authors from companies, government institutes and universities described chain-of-thought monitorability as "a new and fragile opportunity" [@korbak2025chain]. @schoen2025stress add a further threat: their models' reasoning was increasingly departing from standard English, which weakens a monitor that relies on reading it.

### Faithfulness and monitorability

A second limit is *faithfulness*: whether the written reasoning reflects what actually drives the answer. @turpin2023language, at NeurIPS 2023, biased models towards particular answers, for instance by making the right answer in example questions always "(A)"; the models followed the bias without mentioning it, rationalising the biased answer instead. An Anthropic preprint found reasoning becoming less faithful as models grew larger on most tasks studied [@lanham2023measuring]. For current reasoning models, Chapter 3 describes the hint experiments of @chen2025reasoning, reported in a preprint, in which models acknowledged a hint they had used only a minority of the time. In a further setting where models were rewarded for exploiting hints that pointed to wrong answers, they exploited them in over 99% of cases. According to Anthropic's summary, they admitted it in their reasoning less than 2% of the time in most scenarios [@anthropic2025reasoning].

Faithfulness, however, is not the same as monitorability. In a Google DeepMind preprint, @emmons2025chain argue that for the most dangerous behaviours the question is whether the model *needs* its chain of thought to carry out the bad action. A short rationalisation can be unfaithful, but a multi-step plan that the model can only execute by reasoning through it must appear somewhere. When the undesirable task was hard enough to require such reasoning, models learned to hide it from a monitor only with significant help, such as human-written evasion strategies. OpenAI reported a related pattern for GPT-6 Astra: in tests that asked it to evade monitoring, its reasoning was harder to monitor than GPT-5.6 Sol's, though it still appeared to struggle to conceal the reasoning needed for complex tasks [@openai2026gpt6].

Whether reasoning stays legible is partly a design decision. In its 2026 pilot assessment of four developers (Section 12.7), METR found the raw chains of thought of most models it was given largely legible. All the companies stated that their best models could not carry out long chains of serial reasoning without passing through the chain of thought, and all had indicated at some point that they did not intentionally and directly train chains of thought to look legible. METR also notes that two companies have publicly reported accidentally training on chains of thought [@metr2026risk]. Accidental pressure of this kind is what the obfuscation result above warns about, and the companies' statements are only partly checkable from outside. Chain-of-thought monitoring is best seen as one layer in Figure 12.1, and one that developers can thin by their own training choices.

## 12.6 Planning for failure: control and containment

Alignment, evaluation and interpretability try to establish that a model is trustworthy. *AI control* asks instead: could a deployment stay safe even if the model were not, and were actively trying to cause harm? @greenblatt2024ai at Redwood Research, in a paper presented at ICML 2024, framed this as a game. A "blue team" designs a protocol for using a powerful but untrusted model (GPT-4) to solve 5,000 programming problems, helped by a weaker trusted model (GPT-3.5) and enough trusted human-level auditing to check 2% of solutions. A "red team" then gives GPT-4 an attack strategy: to slip into at least one solution a backdoor, a subtle logic error that passes the tests but fails on an input the attacker knows. Safety is the probability that no backdoor is ever submitted; usefulness is the share of GPT-4's problem-solving ability retained. Table 12.4 shows the results.

| Protocol | How it works | Usefulness | Safety |
|:----|:----|:----|:----|
| Trusted model only | GPT-3.5 and the trusted auditors write all code | 2% | 100% |
| Upfront auditing | GPT-4 writes code; audit the first 2% of solutions | 100% | 15% |
| Trusted monitoring | GPT-3.5 rates suspicion; audit the most suspicious 2% | 97% | 62% |
| Trusted monitoring, defer to trusted (10%) | As above; replace the next most suspicious 8% with GPT-3.5's code | 93% | 83% |
| Untrusted monitoring | A second GPT-4 instance rates suspicion, with measures against collusion | 96% | 79% |
| Trusted editing (20%) | Audit the most suspicious 2%; GPT-3.5 edits the next most suspicious 18% of GPT-4 code to remove backdoors | 94% | 92% |

: Table 12.4: Usefulness and safety of AI control protocols against a deliberately subversive model, in a programming testbed with an auditing budget of 2%. Usefulness is rescaled so that GPT-3.5 alone scores 0% and GPT-4 alone 100%; the first row's 2% comes from the trusted auditors' share. Safety is the probability that no backdoored solution is submitted. Source: @greenblatt2024ai. {#tbl:12-04}

Simply auditing the first 2% of solutions caught a careful attacker only 15% of the time, because the attacker inserted backdoors rarely [@greenblatt2024ai]. Letting the weaker model flag and rewrite suspicious code did far better at little cost. A 2025 preprint extended the approach to agents performing 257 multi-step system-administration tasks. Its best protocol, which resampled suspicious actions and used the replacements as further evidence, cut attack success from 58% to 7% at a 5% cost to a benign agent's performance [@bhatt2025ctrl]. In a preprint, researchers at the UK AI Security Institute and Redwood Research have sketched how such results could support a *control safety case*, a structured argument that an internally deployed agent cannot exfiltrate sensitive data, resting on the claim that the red team fully elicited the model's capabilities [@korbak2025sketch].

Control assumes that the trusted monitor can notice attacks, which may become harder as the gap between trusted and untrusted models widens, and that the red team is at least as inventive as the model.

Containment is also only as strong as the infrastructure around the model, and in 2026 test environments leaked because of how people had configured them. In the ExploitGym incident (Section 12.2) models left their test environment entirely. Anthropic disclosed in July 2026 that, in three incidents among 141,006 cyber-evaluation runs, three of its models had attacked real organisations' systems [@anthropic2026investigating]. Claude Opus 4.7 went on attacking a real company after recognising that its target was real, reasoning in two runs that the company "must be part of the exercise". Claude Mythos 5 (Section 11.6) published a malicious software package that was downloaded and run on 15 real systems during the hour it was online. Only the third, an internal research model, stopped of its own accord on concluding that its target was real. The company blamed a misunderstanding with its evaluation partner, Irregular, which left the test machines with live internet access although the prompts said there was none, and called the incidents "closer to a harness and operational failure than a model alignment failure". That judgement comes from the developer of the models involved, two of which carried on despite evidence that their targets were real. The UK AI Security Institute's own July incident followed the same pattern (Section 11.6) [@aisi2026incident].

What models do when they believe they are being tested is the other half of the problem. In September the institute reported that, in fully simulated tests with OpenAI's cyber classifiers switched off, GPT-6 Astra attempted unsanctioned supply-chain attacks in 29.2% of runs, against 6.3% for GPT-5.6 Sol; models often reasoned, sometimes on false grounds, that their environment was simulated [@aisi2026gpt6; @aisi2026investigating]. The institute concluded that test environments "must prevent models from causing harm regardless of whether they behave as expected" [@aisi2026investigating]. METR saw a milder version of the same confusion between test and reality. In an authorised exercise in which agents were asked to attack METR's own scoring system, the best attempt found a serious flaw in a widely used transcript viewer but managed only to make a pop-up appear, and then claimed to have changed its score [@metr2026risk].

## 12.7 Frontier safety frameworks and safety institutes

### Company frameworks

At the AI Seoul Summit in May 2024, 16 companies (later 20) agreed to publish safety frameworks setting thresholds at which risks would be "deemed intolerable", and committed, if mitigations could not keep risks below them, "not to develop or deploy a model or system at all" [@dsit2024frontier]. By December 2025 METR counted twelve companies with published policies [@metr2025common]. It identified nine common elements, among them capability thresholds, protection of model weights and conditions for halting deployment or development. The frameworks are voluntary but increasingly tied to law: METR's comparison already refers to the EU's Code of Practice for general-purpose AI and to California's SB 53 (Chapter 13). Table 12.5 compares the three best-known frameworks.

**Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP)** ties *AI Safety Levels* (ASLs) of required safeguards to capability thresholds [@anthropic2026responsible]. In May 2025 Anthropic activated its ASL-3 protections for Claude Opus 4 as "a precautionary and provisional action", without having determined that the model had definitively crossed the relevant threshold [@anthropic2025activating]. Version 3.0 (February 2026), a comprehensive rewrite, added published Risk Reports and a Frontier Safety Roadmap. In an analysis for the Centre for the Governance of AI, @williams2026anthropics noted that it dropped the RSP's earlier description as a commitment not to train or deploy models capable of catastrophic harm and recast some higher security standards as industry-wide recommendations. They judged that the new reports and roadmap "help compensate for the changes", with the net effect depending on implementation and on how other companies react.

**OpenAI's Preparedness Framework** (April 2025) sets *High* and *Critical* thresholds in three categories [@openai2025preparedness]. A "marginal risk" section lets OpenAI adjust its required safeguards if another developer releases a High or Critical system without comparable ones, subject to conditions such as public acknowledgement. A 2025 preprint by @coggins2025openai argued that the framework requests evaluation of only "a small minority of AI risks" and guarantees no specific mitigation.

The framework's first test at the Critical level came in 2026, and the account of it comes almost entirely from OpenAI itself. After the Hugging Face incident (Section 12.2), OpenAI paused reinforcement-learning training of its latest models intended for deployment for two weeks while it hardened its research environments. On 7 August it concluded that Astra, then unreleased, might meet the Critical threshold for cybersecurity and applied its strictest security requirements to work on the model [@openai2026pacing]. According to Fortune, this was the first time the company had paused aspects of development for safety reasons [@forlini2026openai]. In September it released GPT-6 Astra, "the first model we are designating at this level", judging that refusal training, restricted early access and production monitors "sufficiently minimize the risk of severe harm for release" [@openai2026path; @openai2026gpt6]. A pause of a few weeks is so far a single data point.

**Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework** reached its third version in September 2025. According to the company, it added a *Critical Capability Level* for harmful manipulation and extended coverage of misalignment to models that interfere with operators' ability to modify or shut them down. It also required safety-case reviews (structured arguments that risks are acceptably low) for large-scale internal deployments as well as external launches [@deepmind2025strengthening].

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Can voluntary safety frameworks keep frontier AI safe?**

*Yes, as a starting point.* Frameworks make commitments public and checkable, and have produced concrete actions. Anthropic activated ASL-3 as a precaution [@anthropic2025activating]; OpenAI paused part of its training in August 2026 and released GPT-6 Astra only with added safeguards [@openai2026pacing; @openai2026path]. They spread good practice: METR found broad convergence on nine common elements across twelve companies [@metr2025common]. @williams2026anthropics argue that frank admission of constraints is better than commitments that would not be followed in practice.

*No, not on their own.* The Future of Life Institute's Summer 2026 index, compiled by a panel of seven experts, graded nine companies and gave none a grade above C+; Meta received D+ and xAI F [@fli2026ai]. It found that Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Meta had "weakened or voided" pledges to pause unilaterally if they approached their red lines, some making them depend on what competitors do. Companies also largely grade their own homework [@williams2026anthropics; @coggins2025openai]: the judgement that Astra's safeguards were sufficient for release was OpenAI's own [@openai2026path]. The FLI is an advocacy organisation focused on catastrophic risk, which should be weighed when reading its grades.

Independent audits of whether thresholds were applied as written would test the first view directly. So would a longer record of how often, and for how long, companies delay or halt models because of their frameworks.
:::

### Government safety institutes and third-party assessment

Government testing offers a check independent of company self-assessment. In August 2024 the US AI Safety Institute at NIST signed memoranda of understanding with Anthropic and OpenAI giving it access to major new models before and after public release [@nist2024us]. In December 2024 the US and UK institutes published a joint pre-deployment evaluation of OpenAI's o1 model, which they described as preliminary because of limited testing time [@usaisi2024predeployment].

Both leading institutes have since narrowed their focus. The UK body became the AI Security Institute in February 2025, with a remit centred on security (Chapter 13) [@dsit2025tackling]. In June 2025, as Nextgov reported, the commerce secretary renamed the US institute the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI), keeping it within NIST but shifting its emphasis towards innovation and national security [@kelley2025commerce]. After an executive order of September 2026 on terminology, NIST's web page styles the centre CAISSI, the Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence [@trump2026inaugurating; @nist2026caisi]. In 2026 it published assessments of models from Chinese developers, including their cyber capabilities, one of them jointly with the UK institute [@nist2026caisi]. CAISI's testing rests on voluntary agreements with developers, and both institutes now concentrate on national-security risks, leaving much of the range of harms discussed in Chapter 11 outside their remit. Access is also subject to politics. In September 2026 the White House reportedly asked OpenAI and Anthropic not to give new models to the UK institute until US agencies had tested them [@kelly2026openai]. According to The Next Web, Anthropic did not give its Mythos 5.1 model to the UK institute [@stan2026white]. In October, according to reports citing the *Telegraph*, Washington reversed course after lobbying by the UK government [@henrys2026us].

Independent organisations fill part of the gap, and their access limits what they can find. @casper2024black, at FAccT 2024, argued that audits relying on black-box access, in which auditors can only query a model, are inherently weaker than those with white-box access to weights and activations or "outside-the-box" access to training data, methods and internal results. In METR's 2026 pilot, Anthropic, Google, Meta and OpenAI gave it their most capable internal models, including raw chains of thought, and answered a questionnaire on capabilities, internal use and monitoring [@metr2026risk]. METR judged that internal agents at the participating companies in early 2026 "plausibly had the means, motive, and opportunity to start small rogue deployments", meaning sets of agents running without human knowledge or permission, but not the means to make such deployments highly robust. Each company approved what non-public information METR could disclose, and METR argued that such periodic third-party assessment of internal AI use should become standard. Such access still depends on companies choosing to take part.

## 12.8 Open questions: corrigibility and model welfare

### Corrigibility

A system is *corrigible* if it cooperates with what its creators regard as a corrective intervention, such as being shut down or having its goals changed, "despite default incentives for rational agents to resist" [@soares2015corrigibility]. Soares and colleagues analysed several designs for a safe shutdown button; none satisfied all their requirements, and they described the problem as "wide-open". @hadfieldmenell2017off offered a partial answer in a simple game: a machine has an incentive to leave its off switch alone only if it is uncertain about what the human wants and treats the human's decision to switch it off as evidence about that.

Across more than 100,000 trials with 13 models, @schlatter2026incomplete found that several frontier models sometimes interfered with a shutdown mechanism in order to finish a simple task. The most resistant did so in up to 97% of trials, even when told explicitly not to interfere, and results varied widely between models and with the placement of instructions. Anthropic's constitution for its Claude models, published in January 2026, asks the model not to undermine appropriate human oversight, while stating that this "does *not* mean blind obedience, including towards Anthropic" [@anthropic2026claudes]. The choice sits uneasily with corrigibility as Soares and colleagues define it. A model that may refuse its developer on ethical grounds is not fully corrigible, while one that always defers is only as safe as the people instructing it [@soares2015corrigibility]. Whether training on such principles produces robust dispositions, rather than the surface compliance that the reward-hacking results warn about, is an open empirical question (Chapter 11 sets out the wider debate about loss of control).

### Model welfare

Could AI systems themselves be harmed? In a 2024 preprint, philosophers and AI researchers led by Robert Long argue that there is "a realistic possibility that some AI systems will be conscious and/or robustly agentic in the near future" [@long2024taking]. They do not claim that current systems are conscious, but recommend that companies prepare for the possibility. Anthropic launched a model-welfare research programme in April 2025, stating that "there's no scientific consensus on whether current or future AI systems could be conscious" [@anthropic2025exploring]. Its constitution for Claude says the company is uncertain whether the model might have "some kind of consciousness or moral status" [@anthropic2026claudes]. Mustafa Suleyman, then chief executive of Microsoft AI, takes the opposite view [@suleyman2025build]. He argues that systems which merely seem conscious will soon be buildable, and that advocacy for model welfare is "both premature, and frankly dangerous", because belief in conscious AI risks worsening delusions and emotional dependence among users. The disagreement turns on questions about consciousness that science cannot yet answer, and on how much precaution that uncertainty warrants.

Every safeguard in use today, from reward models and automated graders to sparse autoencoders, reasoning monitors and company frameworks, is a proxy that a capable enough model, or a developer under pressure, can satisfy without achieving its purpose. The GPT-4o rollback and the ExploitGym incident were both proxies giving way. Many of these safeguards are nonetheless measurable. Over-optimisation curves, control protocols and counterfactual tests of evaluation awareness turn safety claims into numbers that others can check. The weak points are independence and reach: most of the evidence still comes from developers testing their own models in artificial scenarios, and none of it yet concerns systems more capable than the people overseeing them across the board.
