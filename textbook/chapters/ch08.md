# Chapter 8: Work, Productivity and the Economy

In the first half of 2025 the research organisation METR ran a randomised trial with 16 experienced open-source software developers. Each of 246 real issues from their own projects was randomly assigned to be done with or without AI tools. Before starting, the developers expected AI to cut their time per issue by 24%, and economists and machine-learning experts surveyed by METR expected savings of 38–39%. When the developers' recorded times were compared, the issues on which AI was allowed had taken 19% *longer*. After the study the developers still believed that AI had made them about 20% faster [@becker2025measuring].

Two years earlier a widely cited experiment had found the opposite. College-educated professionals given ChatGPT for short writing tasks finished them 40% faster, and blind graders rated their work 18% better [@noy2023experimental]. Both studies were randomised and both timed work carefully. They studied different people, doing different tasks, with different generations of tools, and the gap between their answers is the central fact about the evidence on AI and work: effects depend heavily on setting, and the settings that are easiest to study are not typical of the economy.

The evidence can be arranged along a chain that runs from technical potential to economic outcomes (Figure 8.1): what AI could do (exposure), whether it is used (adoption), what it does to tasks when used (experiments), how firms respond (jobs and wages) and what this adds up to (macroeconomics). Some effects lie outside the chain: the hidden labour behind AI, the erosion or building of skills, and the distribution of gains and losses.

## 8.1 Tasks and exposure: how the measures are built

Economists who study technology and work treat a job as a bundle of **tasks**, the specific activities a worker performs, such as drafting a letter, checking an invoice or calming an angry customer. In the framework of @acemoglu2019automation, automation lets machines take over tasks that people used to do. This has a *displacement effect*, which lowers the demand for labour in those tasks. New technologies also create new tasks in which people have the advantage, a *reinstatement effect* that raises labour demand. Whether a technology helps or hurts workers overall depends on the balance between the two and on how fast new tasks appear.

Both effects are visible in the historical record. Using eight decades of US census data, @autor2024new find that the majority of current employment is in job specialties introduced since 1940; the same study finds that the demand-reducing effect of automation innovations has grown stronger since 1980, while the demand-raising effect of augmentation innovations has not. @acemoglu2022tasks estimate that between 50% and 70% of the change in the US wage structure between 1980 and 2016 is accounted for by relative wage declines of worker groups specialised in routine tasks in industries undergoing rapid automation. Their model also explains how large changes in inequality can coexist with modest productivity gains, a combination that recurs in the AI forecasts discussed in Section 8.5.

### Three ways to score an occupation

An **exposure measure** scores each occupation by how much of its work a technology could, in principle, perform or speed up. All widely used measures start from O\*NET, the US Department of Labor's database that describes about a thousand occupations by their tasks, abilities and work activities. They differ in what they score and who does the scoring.

The *AI Occupational Exposure* (AIOE) index of @felten2021occupational was built before the current generation of large language models. It links ten AI applications tracked by the Electronic Frontier Foundation (among them image recognition, reading comprehension, language modelling, translation and speech recognition) to the 52 human abilities that O\*NET uses to describe more than 800 occupations, such as oral comprehension, inductive reasoning or arm–hand steadiness. A crowd-sourced survey rated how related each application is to each ability. An occupation's score is an average of the abilities' AI relatedness, each ability weighted by its prevalence and importance in that occupation. In a 2023 preprint the authors set the weight of every application except language modelling to zero; the re-weighted scores correlate at 0.979 with the original index. Telemarketers come top, and post-secondary teachers of English, foreign languages and history rank near the top [@felten2023language]. The method measures *relatedness* of abilities, not whether a task can actually be done.

@eloundou2023gpts scored tasks directly. They used O\*NET version 27.2, covering 1,016 occupations, 19,265 task statements and 2,087 "detailed work activities". Each item was labelled with a rubric. It counts as *directly exposed* (E1) if access to a large language model (LLM) through an interface such as ChatGPT could cut the time needed by at least half at equivalent quality, meaning that "a third party, typically the recipient of the output, would not notice or care about LLM assistance". It counts as *exposed with complementary software* (E2) if the 50% saving would require additional tools built on the model, including image generation. Otherwise it is *not exposed* (E0). The authors and experienced annotators who had reviewed outputs of GPT-3, GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 labelled the detailed work activities and a subset of tasks, and an early version of GPT-4 labelled every task, with a slightly modified rubric used as the prompt. The human and GPT-4 labels agreed 80.8% of the time on the narrowest measure and 82.1% on the broadest. The labels are summarised in three measures: *α* counts only E1; *β* adds half of E2, to reflect that software takes time to build; *ζ* counts all of E2.

The preprint's headline numbers use the middle measure, *β*: about 80% of US workers are in occupations with at least 10% of their tasks exposed, and about 19% in occupations where more than half are exposed. The choice of measure moves the results far more than the choice of labeller (Table 8.1). With an LLM alone about 15% of tasks are exposed; once LLM-powered software is counted the share is 47% on human labels and 56% on GPT-4's, and, on human labels, the share of workers with more than half their tasks exposed rises from 3% to as much as 49% [@eloundou2023gpts]. Unlike earlier measures of exposure to machine learning, exposure on this rubric rises with wages, and occupations that depend heavily on science and critical-thinking skills are less exposed. A shorter version appeared in *Science* in 2024 [@eloundou2024gpts].

The third approach is the refined index from the International Labour Organization (ILO) and Poland's NASK research institute, published in May 2025 [@gmyrek2025generative]. Its authors first asked three LLMs (GPT-4, GPT-4o and Gemini Flash 1.5) to score every one of the 29,753 tasks in Poland's occupational classification from 0 (cannot be automated) to 1 (could be done without a human operator). They then surveyed 1,640 people working in Poland, collecting 52,558 ratings of 2,861 tasks, and put a sample to national and international experts in several rounds of structured discussion. The human scores were fed into an AI model that re-scored the nearly 30,000 Polish tasks, and the same model then re-scored the tasks of the international ISCO-08 classification used in the ILO's 2023 index. Occupations are placed in four exposure "gradients" using both the mean task score *μ* and its spread *σ*: Gradient 4 requires *μ* ≥ 0.6 and *μ* − *σ* ≥ 0.5, meaning that almost every task is highly exposed. The design matters because an occupation with a moderate average but a few highly exposed tasks is more likely to be reshaped than replaced.

The refinement lowered some earlier scores. Average occupational scores fell slightly from 0.30 in the 2023 index to 0.29, and clerical work, though still the most exposed group, lost ground because tasks such as taking meeting notes or scheduling, once scored as high as 0.9, were judged after two years of practical use to need substantial human effort [@gmyrek2025generative]. On the new index, one in four workers worldwide is in an occupation with some exposure. Only 3.3% of global employment falls in Gradient 4. Overall exposure is 34% of employment in high-income countries and 11% in low-income ones [@gmyrek2025generative]. Announcing the index, the ILO concluded that "transformation, not replacement, is the most likely outcome" [@ilo2025one].

An International Monetary Fund staff note from January 2024 reached higher numbers: almost 40% of global employment exposed, about 60% in advanced economies, 40% in emerging markets and 26% in low-income countries. In advanced economies it judged that about half of exposed jobs might benefit from AI and half might be negatively affected [@cazzaniga2024genai]. The IMF figure rests on the ability-based AIOE index of Felten and colleagues and counts an occupation as highly exposed whenever its score lies above the median, a cut-off the note itself describes as relative, so the share depends on where the line is drawn. The ILO counts an occupation as exposed only if a substantial part of its tasks scores high on an absolute 0–1 scale of automation potential (*μ* + *σ* ≥ 0.5 even for its lowest gradient), which places fewer workers above the line [@gmyrek2025generative].

::: {.box .concept}
**Concept: Exposure**

An occupation's exposure to AI is an estimate of the share of its work that AI could technically perform or speed up. It is not a forecast: it contains no information about cost, timing, regulation or whether firms will adopt the technology, and a high score is compatible with a job that disappears, shrinks, grows or simply changes. The three families of measures embed different assumptions. The AIOE scores abilities rather than tasks; the Eloundou rubric sets a threshold (a 50% time saving at unchanged quality) and lets the model judge its own usefulness; the ILO–NASK index asks workers and experts and then generalises their judgements with a model. Each also treats tasks as separable, although in real jobs one task often depends on another.
:::

### What exposure cannot tell

Every exposure measure relies on judgements about what AI can do, by people or by models, and those judgements date quickly, as the ILO's downward revision of clerical tasks shows. The measures treat tasks as independent, whereas a job whose tasks are 60% exposed may still need a person for the remaining 40%. And none of them can tell whether AI will substitute for a worker or complement one. A tool that drafts legal documents might reduce demand for junior lawyers, or make legal services cheap enough that demand for them grows. Figure 8.1 places exposure at the start of the chain for this reason: it is the step that is easiest to estimate for every occupation and the one furthest from outcomes.

## 8.2 Adoption: who uses AI, and for what

A tool affects productivity only if people use it. Adoption is surprisingly hard to measure: the answer depends on whether workers or firms are asked, on how the question is worded, and on whether occasional or intensive use counts. Table 8.2 sets out the main measures.

### Worker surveys

@bick2024rapid run a nationally representative survey of US adults aged 18 to 64. By August 2025, 54.6% of them used generative AI [@bick2025state]. That figure reflects the authors' reweighting of all rounds after a test of question order showed that their earlier results had underestimated use, a reminder of how fragile the measurement is. Measured from the launch of the first mass-market product, workplace adoption had matched the pace of the personal computer, and overall adoption had outpaced both the PC and the internet. Regular use at work was less widespread: in late 2024, 23% of employed respondents had used generative AI for work in the previous week, and 9% every working day [@bick2024rapid].

Intensity is far lower than adoption. Generative AI was used in 5.7% of all work hours in August 2025, up from 4.1% in November 2024, and workers reported time savings equal to 1.6% of all work hours, counting non-users (1.4% in the February 2025 version of the working paper). The authors infer that generative AI "may have increased labor productivity by up to 1.3%" since ChatGPT's release, with the caveat that saved time may go to less productive activities [@bick2025state].

In Denmark, Humlum and Vestergaard ran large surveys in late 2023 and 2024, with Statistics Denmark, of workers in 11 occupations thought to be highly exposed to AI chatbots: accountants, customer-support specialists, financial advisers, HR professionals, IT-support specialists, journalists, legal professionals, marketing professionals, office clerks, software developers and teachers. The latest round covered about 25,000 workers at 7,000 workplaces and was linked to official employment records. The revised version of their working paper (March 2026) shows how much employers matter. In workplaces that neither encouraged use nor provided tools or training, about 40% of workers had used chatbots at work and about 7% used them daily. In workplaces that encouraged use, supplied enterprise chatbots and trained staff, 93% had used them, 28% used them daily and 19% reported saving more than an hour a day. About 8% of users without employer initiatives, and about 17% with them, had taken on entirely new tasks created by chatbots, such as reviewing AI output or writing usage policies [@humlum2025still]. (In the first version, of May 2025, users reported average time savings of only 2.8% of work hours [@humlum2025large].)

### Firm surveys

The US Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS) regularly asks firms whether they use AI. Until November 2025 the question asked about AI used in *producing goods or services*; by late 2025 about 10% of firms said yes, double the rate of mid-2024. From 17 November 2025 the question covered AI in *any business function*, and in early 2026 about 20% of firms reported use. Use was highest in information and professional services, at nearly four in ten firms; in agriculture, transport, accommodation and food services and construction it was below 10% [@garcialuna2026ai].

A Census Bureau working paper on a special AI supplement to the BTOS, covering November 2025 to January 2026, shows how large firms drive the totals and how shallow most use is [@bonney2026microstructure]. In that period 18% of firms used AI in a business function, or 32% when firms are weighted by employment, and among very large firms in information, professional services and finance the rate reached 50–60%. Of the adopters, 57% used AI in three or fewer functions, most often sales and marketing (52%). Two-thirds used AI only to augment tasks, and just 2% reported that AI had reduced their employment.

### Usage data from AI companies

AI providers can observe use directly. Anthropic, the developer of the Claude models, publishes an "Economic Index" based on privacy-preserving analysis of conversations. Its first report, a preprint by company researchers analysing more than four million conversations, found that software development and writing together accounted for nearly half of usage, and that slightly more usage suggested augmentation (57%) than automation (43%) [@handa2025which]. According to the company's January 2026 report, based on one million consumer conversations and one million business transcripts from November 2025, consumer conversations were split between augmentation (52%) and automation (45%), whereas about three-quarters of business interactions through the programming interface, in which firms connect the model to their own software, were automation [@anthropic2026economic].

These data cover real use at scale, but for one company's products and customers only. The "automation" and "augmentation" labels are assigned by a language model from conversation patterns, not measured from effects on jobs. A June 2026 report linked usage to a survey of about 9,700 users, but computer and mathematical occupations made up about 30% of respondents against 4% of US employment [@massenkoff2026anthropic].

## 8.3 What experiments show about task productivity

The strongest evidence about cause and effect comes from **randomised controlled trials (RCTs)**, in which chance decides which people or tasks get access to AI, so that the groups differ on average only in that access. Many other studies are **observational**: they compare people or firms who chose to adopt with those who did not, so differences may reflect who chose rather than what they chose. Some observational studies are **quasi-experiments**, exploiting a natural source of variation, such as a staggered roll-out, to approximate a trial. Even an RCT measures the effect only for its sample, task and tool. Table 8.2 compares the main studies, and Figure 8.2 plots their estimates.

### Writing and consulting

@noy2023experimental recruited marketers, grant writers, consultants, data analysts, HR professionals and managers. Each wrote two occupation-specific pieces, such as a press release, a short report, an analysis plan or a delicate email, with large bonuses for quality. Between the tasks, a random half were walked through ChatGPT and allowed to use it; the control group was instead asked to sign up for an unrelated text editor. Experienced professionals in the same occupations graded the work blind. Time fell by 40% and quality rose by 18%. The gains were largest for participants who had done worst on the first task, so inequality between workers narrowed. Two weeks later, treated participants were twice as likely as controls to be using ChatGPT in their real jobs.

@dellacqua2026navigating ran a preregistered experiment with 758 consultants at Boston Consulting Group, first circulated as a working paper in 2023. After a baseline task, participants were randomly assigned to work without AI, with GPT-4, or with GPT-4 plus a short overview of prompting. On 18 realistic consulting tasks chosen to lie within the model's capabilities, those with AI completed 12.2% more tasks, worked 25.1% faster and produced work of significantly higher quality. On a complex managerial task selected to lie outside those capabilities, consultants using AI were 19% less likely than the control group to reach the correct answer. The authors call this pattern the **jagged technological frontier**: AI's competence does not decline smoothly as tasks become harder for humans, and a worker who cannot see where the frontier runs may rely on the tool exactly where it is weakest.

### Customer support

The most detailed field study looked at a real workplace over many months. @brynjolfsson2025generative studied an AI assistant introduced at a Fortune 500 firm selling business software, whose agents answered technical questions by online chat. Access was rolled out to different agents at different times, mainly between late 2020 and early 2021. The authors used **difference-in-differences**: they compared the change in performance of agents who gained access with the change, over the same period, among agents who had not yet gained it. The design rests on the assumption that the timing of access was unrelated to other changes in performance, which the authors probe with several alternative estimators.

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: An AI assistant in a customer-support centre**

The firm's agents, 89% of them outside the United States and mainly in the Philippines, served small and medium-sized US businesses. The assistant, built on GPT-3 and fine-tuned by a third-party company, suggested replies and links to documentation in real time; agents could ignore the suggestions [@brynjolfsson2025generative]. Across 5,172 agents, access raised issues resolved per hour by 15% on average. The least skilled fifth of agents gained about 36%. For the most experienced and highest-skilled agents, gains in speed were small and the quality of their conversations declined slightly. Agents with two months' tenure who had the assistant performed about as well as agents with more than six months' tenure who did not.

Three further findings bear on the mechanism. Gains were largest for moderately rare problems, where agents had little experience but the system had enough training data. English fluency improved, particularly among international agents. And customers were more polite and less likely to ask for a manager. Section 8.6 considers what the agents' location means for who gains.
:::

### Software development

Software is the most-studied occupation, because output can be logged automatically. In an early experiment, @peng2023impact recruited 95 freelance programmers through Upwork in 2022 and randomly gave 45 of them GitHub Copilot, a code-completion tool. The task was to write a simple web server in JavaScript. Among those who finished, the treated group was 55.8% faster, with a **confidence interval** (the range of effects consistent with the data at the stated level of confidence) from 21% to 89%.

@cui2026effects analysed three field experiments in which Microsoft, Accenture and an anonymous Fortune 100 electronics manufacturer gave a random subset of developers access to Copilot as part of normal business, for two to eight months, after which everyone got access. Take-up was far from complete. At Microsoft only 8.5% of the treated group signed up in the first two weeks, rising to 42.5% after two reminder emails. The authors therefore use random assignment as an *instrument* for actual use, which estimates the effect on those who adopted. Pooling 4,867 developers, they estimate that using the tool raised completed tasks (pull requests, the unit in which code changes are submitted for review) by 26.08%, with a standard error of 10.3%; code builds rose by 38.38%, and commits by 13.55%, an estimate too imprecise to be statistically significant. At Microsoft, recent hires and junior staff gained significantly, while longer-tenured and senior developers did not.

The METR trial described at the start of the chapter is the main counterweight [@becker2025measuring]. Its 16 developers had typically more than a decade of experience and an average of five years and 1,500 commits on their repositories, which averaged ten years old and more than 1.1 million lines of code. They were paid $150 an hour. The authors examined 20 possible explanations for the slowdown and judged five likely contributors: over-optimism about AI, high familiarity with the code, large and complex repositories, low reliability of the AI (developers accepted fewer than 44% of generations and spent about 9% of their time reviewing and cleaning AI output), and tacit context that the tools could not use. The authors stress that the result does not show that AI slows down most developers, or that it would do so with later tools.

METR's follow-up, beginning in August 2025, enrolled 57 developers and more than 800 tasks. For the ten developers returning from the first study the estimated effect was an 18% speed-up; for the 47 new recruits it was 4%. Neither estimate was statistically distinguishable from zero. Between 30% and 50% of developers said they had not submitted some tasks because they did not want to do them without AI, and some developers declined to take part at all for the same reason. METR concluded that developers were probably sped up more in early 2026 than in early 2025 but that its data were "only very weak evidence" of how much [@metr2026changing]. The selection problem is itself a finding: once a tool becomes indispensable to some workers, randomising it away becomes hard.

### When AI widens gaps

Most studies find gains concentrated among weaker or less experienced workers. A field experiment with small-business owners in Kenya found the reverse [@otis2026uneven]. Entrepreneurs were randomly given access to a GPT-4-based business assistant. There was no statistically significant average effect on revenues or profits, but the effect for those who had been performing poorly at baseline was more than 0.2 standard deviations lower than for high performers. In subsample analyses, high performers may have gained more than 15% while low performers did nearly 10% worse. The questions asked and the advice received were similar across the two groups; the difference lay in which advice entrepreneurs chose to act on. Unlike a support agent choosing among suggested replies to a familiar query, an entrepreneur has to judge open-ended advice without quick feedback.

### Reading the evidence together

The estimates in Table 8.2 range from a 56% reduction in task time to a 19% increase. Three patterns hold across them. Gains are largest on short, well-defined tasks with clear success criteria, and smallest, or negative, for experts on complex work in contexts they know well. Gains usually shrink inequality *within* a task, because AI transfers the practices of strong performers to weak ones, but not when users must judge the quality of advice themselves. And the tools change faster than studies are published: Copilot in 2022, GPT-4 in 2023 and the coding agents of 2026 (Chapter 5) are different technologies.

The evidence base also carries a warning about its own reliability. In May 2025 MIT announced that it had "no confidence in the provenance, reliability or validity of the data" in a November 2024 preprint by a former doctoral student, *Artificial Intelligence, Scientific Discovery, and Product Innovation*, which had studied AI in materials research. MIT asked arXiv to mark the paper as withdrawn and contacted the *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, to which it had been submitted [@mit2025assuring].

## 8.4 Jobs and wages: the evidence so far

A productivity gain in a task leaves firms several options: employ fewer people, keep the same staff and produce more, or move workers into new tasks. Nearly four years after the launch of ChatGPT, the evidence on which they are choosing is partial and in places conflicting.

### Little sign of economy-wide disruption

The Danish study provides the most precise test. Linking survey answers to administrative records of monthly earnings, hours and occupations up to December 2024, Humlum and Vestergaard compare adopters and non-adopters before and after chatbots became available. The first version of the paper (May 2025) found "precise zeros", ruling out average effects on earnings or recorded hours larger than 1% [@humlum2025large]. The revised version (March 2026) again finds null effects: the pooled estimates rule out effects larger than 2% two years after ChatGPT's launch, no occupation shows a significant effect, and the nulls hold even among daily users who report substantial productivity gains [@humlum2025still]. What does move is the structure of work. Adopters were more likely to have switched occupations, especially into IT-support and clerical roles, and switchers' earnings grew 12 percentage points faster than those of other employed workers, though they were too few to move the average.

In the United States, the Yale Budget Lab measured how fast the mix of occupations changed in the 33 months after ChatGPT's release and compared it with the spread of computers and the internet [@gimbel2025evaluating]. The mix was changing somewhat faster than in earlier periods, but the difference was small and had begun before generative AI. Measures of exposure, automation and augmentation showed "no sign of being related to changes in employment or unemployment". The Economic Innovation Group, a Washington think tank, reached a similar conclusion with Current Population Survey data and five exposure measures [@eckhardt2025ai]. Between 2022 and early 2025, unemployment in the most exposed fifth of workers rose by 0.30 percentage points, against 0.94 points for the least exposed fifth; unemployment was rising for young workers and recent graduates in both exposed and less exposed occupations.

### Where substitution shows up

Clearer effects appear in online freelancing, where tasks are bought one at a time and buyers can switch to AI at once. Studying a large platform, @hui2024short find that after the release of ChatGPT and the image generators DALL-E 2 and Midjourney, freelancers in the most affected occupations saw falls in both employment and earnings, and that past performance did not protect them; there is suggestive evidence that top freelancers were hit harder. On another leading platform, @demirci2025who estimate a 21% fall in job posts for automation-prone writing and coding work relative to manual-intensive work within eight months of ChatGPT's release, and a 17% fall in posts for image creation after image generators appeared. The remaining automation-prone jobs were more complex and better paid. Freelance markets are small and unusually exposed, so these results show what substitution looks like where it is easy, not how fast it will happen in employment relationships.

### Early-career workers

Payroll data point to a group that may be affected first. In a working paper using monthly records from ADP, a payroll processor serving firms with more than 26 million US workers, @brynjolfsson2026canaries document "six facts" from a balanced panel of firms employing 3.5 to 5 million people a month. In the August 2026 version, with data to June 2026, employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the two most AI-exposed fifths of occupations had fallen about 11% since November 2022, while young workers in the three least exposed fifths grew about 10%. Their employment stood 19% below where it would have been had it kept pace with less-exposed peers, up from 15% at the July 2025 data vintage. Experienced workers showed no comparable gap. The adjustment came through reduced hiring rather than layoffs, appeared in employment rather than base pay, and was concentrated in occupations where AI is mostly used to automate; where it mostly complements workers, employment was flat or rising. Earlier versions headlined a regression-adjusted 13% relative decline (July 2025 data); the authors now prefer the simpler descriptive gap.

A separate working paper using résumé data on 65 million workers at more than 280,000 US firms found a related pattern [@hosseinimaasoum2025generative]. After firms posted job advertisements for staff to integrate generative AI, a signal of active adoption, junior employment at those firms fell sharply relative to non-adopting firms, while senior employment changed little, again mainly through slower hiring. Job advertisements suggest that entry-level roles are also being redesigned. According to PwC's 2026 *Global AI Jobs Barometer*, an analysis of more than one billion advertisements, US entry-level roles most exposed to AI were seven times more likely than others to require skills traditionally expected of senior staff, such as leadership or face-to-face interaction [@pwc2026global]. These are correlations, and the consultancy's analysis does not identify their cause.

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Is AI already reducing entry-level hiring?**

*The case that it is.* The ADP data show a 19% employment gap for 22- to 25-year-olds in highly exposed occupations by June 2026, widening steadily since the authors first documented it in August 2025 and concentrated where AI substitutes for tasks. It persists when technology firms and computer occupations are excluded and when the authors control for exposure to interest-rate rises and for remote work [@brynjolfsson2026canaries]. Firm-level résumé data show junior employment falling specifically at firms that adopt generative AI [@hosseinimaasoum2025generative], and freelance platforms show direct substitution in writing and coding [@demirci2025who].

*The case that it is not yet shown.* The ADP authors present their findings as "early, descriptive indicators" rather than causal estimates: the gap shrinks when education is controlled for, some divergence predates generative AI, and it is larger in the ADP sample than in national surveys. In Denmark, Humlum and Vestergaard replicate the decline in early-career employment in exposed occupations but find that it is *not* driven by firms that adopted chatbots; workplaces that encourage chatbot use show no differential change in employment, job creation or destruction, or the composition of hires and separations, including among early-career workers [@humlum2025still]. National US data show unemployment rising for young workers in exposed and unexposed occupations alike [@eckhardt2025ai].

*What would decide it.* The Danish test, linking each firm's actual adoption to its hiring, needs to be repeated with US data and with observations after 2024, when tools became more capable. The ADP gap is measured to mid-2026, the Danish records only to December 2024, so the two may describe different stages rather than contradict each other.
:::

## 8.5 The macroeconomic picture: why forecasts differ

If AI raises productivity in many tasks, the economy as a whole should grow faster. Published estimates of how much faster differ by more than an order of magnitude, and the reason can be traced to a handful of assumptions.

### Hulten's theorem and the task-based sum

@acemoglu2025simple anchors his estimate in a result from growth accounting, **Hulten's theorem** [@hulten1978growth]. In a competitive economy, the first-order effect of a small improvement in the technology for a particular task on aggregate productivity equals the task's share of GDP multiplied by the percentage cost saving in that task. Reallocations of labour and capital across tasks, and knock-on changes in prices, matter only at the second order. Summing over tasks, the gain in total factor productivity (TFP), the part of output growth not explained by more capital or labour, is approximately

*TFP gain ≈ (GDP share of tasks affected) × (average cost saving in those tasks).*

The cost saving in a task is the saving in labour cost multiplied by labour's share of that task's costs, because AI saves labour time but not the machines, materials or premises the task also uses.

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: Acemoglu's arithmetic**

The April 2024 working-paper version of @acemoglu2024simple fills in each term with published estimates. Weighting the tasks that @eloundou2023gpts classify as exposed by their occupations' share of the US wage bill gives 19.9% of tasks exposed. He then takes an estimate by Svanberg and colleagues that 23% of tasks exposed to computer vision could be automated profitably within ten years and applies the same share to all AI, so 0.199 × 0.23 ≈ 4.6% of tasks are affected. For the labour-cost saving he averages the 40% time saving in @noy2023experimental and the 14% in @brynjolfsson2025generative (as measured in that study's 2023 version), giving 27%; he leaves out the 55.8% in @peng2023impact as too narrow a task. With an AI-exposure-adjusted labour share of 0.57, the average total cost saving is 0.27 × 0.57 ≈ 15.4%. The TFP gain over ten years is then 0.046 × 0.154 ≈ 0.71%, or about 0.07 percentage points a year.

He then argues that early evidence comes from *easy* tasks with clear success criteria, and assumes a labour-cost saving of only 7% for *hard* tasks, half the customer-support figure. That lowers the ten-year gain to about 0.55%. Allowing for the extra investment AI would induce, GDP rises by 0.9–1.1% over ten years, or 1.6–1.8% if there is an investment boom like those of earlier automation technologies [@acemoglu2024simple]. The published version in *Economic Policy* reports slightly lower final figures, no more than 0.66% and, with hard tasks, less than 0.53% [@acemoglu2025simple].

The formula has known limits. It is a first-order approximation for small changes; it counts no new products or new tasks, which could make the true effect larger; and it ignores adjustment costs, which could make it smaller.
:::

### Same formula, different numbers

Philippe Aghion and Simon Bunel applied the same formula in a June 2024 note but with their own reading of the evidence [@aghion2024ai]. They take the share of exposed tasks from an IMF working-paper estimate for the United States (about 60%), assume half of these become profitable to automate because computing costs fall by 22% a year, and average all three early experiments to get a 40% labour-cost saving. Keeping the labour share at 0.57, their baseline is 0.68 percentage points of additional TFP growth *a year*, ten times Acemoglu's figure; the range across plausible inputs runs from 0.07 to 1.24 points. A second method, which assumes that AI will follow the path of electricity or of earlier digital technologies, gives 0.8 to 1.3 points a year. Table 8.3 sets the two task-based calculations side by side.

| Input | Acemoglu (2024 working paper) | Aghion & Bunel (2024 baseline) |
|---|---|---|
| Share of tasks exposed to AI | 19.9% | 60% |
| Share of exposed tasks profitable to automate within ten years | 23% | 50% |
| Labour-cost saving in affected tasks | 27% | 40% |
| Labour share of costs in affected tasks | 0.57 | 0.57 |
| Implied TFP gain over ten years | ≈0.71% | ≈6.8% |
| Implied addition to annual TFP growth | ≈0.07 percentage points | ≈0.68 percentage points |

Every input differs except the labour share, and the product of the differences is a tenfold gap. Acemoglu's figures and Aghion and Bunel's annual figure are as reported; their ten-year figure is the product of their inputs. Acemoglu's published version gives a slightly lower 0.66%. Source: @acemoglu2024simple; @aghion2024ai. {#tbl:08-03}

The Penn Wharton Budget Model at the University of Pennsylvania uses the same framework with an explicit diffusion path [@arnon2025projected]. It treats 40% of current US labour income as potentially exposed, assumes 23% of exposed tasks are automated, and lets labour-cost savings rise from about 25% to 40% as tools improve and spread at the pace of PCs, the internet, smartphones and cloud computing. Its September 2025 projection has productivity and GDP 1.5% higher by 2035, nearly 3% higher by 2055 and 3.7% higher by 2075 than without AI, with the largest contribution to TFP growth, about 0.2 percentage points, in 2032.

Estimates from financial firms and AI companies sit at the upper end. In research published by the investment bank Goldman Sachs in April 2023, its economists estimated that generative AI could raise global GDP by 7%, almost $7 trillion, and lift productivity growth by 1.5 percentage points over a ten-year period [@goldmansachs2023generative]. Anthropic's January 2026 report, using its own usage data, estimated that AI could add about 1.8 percentage points to annual US labour-productivity growth over the next decade, or about 1.0 point once task success rates are allowed for [@anthropic2026economic]. Both are estimates of labour productivity, which also rises when firms invest in more capital, and neither has been peer-reviewed.

### Why productivity statistics may lag

Even large eventual gains may not appear in official statistics for some time. @brynjolfsson2021productivity describe a **productivity J-curve**. Firms adopting a general-purpose technology must make large complementary investments, in reorganising work, training staff and building data systems. These intangible investments are poorly measured, so measured productivity growth is at first *understated* and later, when the benefits are harvested, *overstated*. For computer hardware and software, they estimate that US TFP was 15.9% higher by the end of 2017 than official measures suggested. The Danish evidence that employers absorb chatbots by reorganising tasks, with 85% of users reallocating saved time to other work, is consistent with the early, investment-heavy phase of such a curve [@humlum2025still]; so is the gap between time savings of 1–3% of work hours in surveys and gains of 15% or more in many experiments.

## 8.6 Hidden labour and collective bargaining

### The work behind the machine

AI systems depend on workers whom users rarely see. Models are trained and evaluated on data that people label, and the feedback training described in Chapter 3 relies on raters who compare and score model outputs. Mary Gray and Siddharth Suri call such task-by-task, platform-mediated labour **ghost work**. Their five-year study of on-demand workers in the United States and India found that many apparently automated services rely on people who are hired, managed and paid through software, often with few of the protections of standard employment [@gray2019ghost].

The best-documented case involves ChatGPT itself. In January 2023 an investigation by *TIME* reported that OpenAI had contracted Sama, an outsourcing firm, to have workers in Nairobi label text describing violence, hate speech and sexual abuse [@perrigo2023exclusive]. The labels trained a detector for such content that was later built into ChatGPT. According to *TIME*, the roughly three dozen workers took home between about $1.32 and $2 an hour, while OpenAI's contracts paid Sama $12.50 an hour; Sama said that this rate covered all its costs. All four workers interviewed said the work had left them mentally scarred and described counselling as rare and unhelpful, a claim Sama partly disputed; OpenAI said it took contractors' mental health "very seriously". Sama ended the work in February 2022, eight months early.

Systematic evidence points the same way. The Fairwork project at the Oxford Internet Institute scores online labour platforms out of ten against five principles: fair pay, conditions, contracts, management and representation. Its 2023 ratings covered 15 platforms and drew partly on a survey of 752 workers in 94 countries. Three platforms scored 5, none of the other twelve more than 3, and four scored zero. Microwork platforms supplying data annotation and model evaluation to AI companies "were among the lowest scores" [@fairwork2023work, p. 2]. Most demand for such work comes from clients in the Global North and most of the workforce is in the Global South. None of this labour appears in exposure indices or productivity experiments, which count the people who use AI, not those whose work goes into building it.

### Offshore work and who gains

The best-known field study is itself a study of offshore labour. In the customer-support case in Section 8.3, 89% of the agents worked outside the United States, mainly in the Philippines [@brynjolfsson2025generative]. Its results describe outsourced service work in a lower-wage economy and may not carry over to workers with different pay, training or language backgrounds. The finding that new agents with the assistant matched experienced agents without it also has two readings. For individual agents it means faster learning. For an outsourcing industry that competes partly on the experience of its workforce, it may mean that this advantage counts for less. Whether the gain reaches agents as higher pay or customers and the firm as lower costs depends on wage-setting, which the study was not designed to measure.

### Collective bargaining

The clearest union response came from Hollywood. In 2023 the Writers Guild of America (WGA) struck for 148 days [@wga2023what]. The resulting agreement with the studios, covering 25 September 2023 to 1 May 2026, set rules for AI [@wga2023summary]. AI cannot write or rewrite literary material, and AI-generated material does not count as source material, so it cannot be used to undermine a writer's credit or to pay a lower rewrite fee [@wgaw2026artificial]. A writer may use AI with the company's consent, but the company cannot require it, and companies must disclose when material handed to a writer was generated by AI. The Guild reserved the right to argue that training AI on writers' work is prohibited. The agreement does not ban the technology; it allocates control over it, a negotiated version of the line between augmentation and automation.

The successor agreement, covering 2 May 2026 to 1 May 2030, "preserves all of the protections negotiated in 2023" and adds a duty to give the Guild written notice when companies license writers' work to train a commercial generative AI system; the Guild may then ask to discuss the licence, including payment to writers [@wga2026summary]. Training data, left open in 2023, has thus become a subject of bargaining.

Other collective responses are smaller or less tested. In May 2023 more than 150 content moderators and data workers for Facebook, TikTok and ChatGPT met in Nairobi and voted to form a union [@perrigo2023african]. In December 2023 the US union federation AFL-CIO and Microsoft announced a partnership that, according to Microsoft, includes worker feedback to AI developers and a neutrality framework for future union organising [@microsoft2023aflcio].

## 8.7 Deskilling and the skills pipeline

A separate concern is that relying on AI may erode the skills of the people who use it. In a classic paper on industrial automation, @bainbridge1983ironies set out the **ironies of automation**. Automation leaves human operators the tasks designers could not automate, including monitoring and taking over when the system fails. Yet "physical skills deteriorate when they are not used" [@bainbridge1983ironies, sec. 1.1.1], so an experienced operator who has spent years watching an automated process may have become an inexperienced one by the time intervention is needed.

Evidence from medicine suggests a similar risk with AI. @budzyn2025endoscopist studied colonoscopies at four endoscopy centres in Poland that had introduced an AI system to help detect precancerous growths (adenomas). In a retrospective, observational analysis of 1,443 colonoscopies performed *without* AI assistance between September 2021 and March 2022, the adenoma detection rate fell from 28.4% in the months before AI was introduced to 22.4% afterwards. The authors concluded that continuous exposure to AI "might" reduce performance in unassisted procedures. The before-and-after design cannot exclude other changes over the same period, and the paper drew several published letters. A later prospective trial with 13 endoscopists, using a related measure, found no deskilling in colonoscopies done after AI use ended [@pedersen2026learning]. The question is open.

For knowledge work, the cleanest test so far is a small randomised experiment reported in a preprint by Anthropic researchers [@shen2026how]. Fifty-two programmers with at least a year of Python experience learned an asynchronous programming library they had not used before, half with an AI assistant. The AI group was not significantly faster, and on a quiz afterwards, taken without AI, it scored 17% lower, about two grade points, with weaker conceptual understanding, code reading and debugging. Participants who delegated the coding entirely gained some speed at the cost of learning the library; three of the six patterns of use the authors identified, all involving active engagement such as asking conceptual questions, preserved learning. Chapter 10 discusses comparable findings on learning in education.

Deskilling matters for the economy as well as for safety, because expertise is built by doing routine work. If junior workers no longer perform the tasks through which they once learned, and the payroll evidence in Section 8.4 suggests that fewer of them are being hired into exposed occupations, the supply of future experts able to supervise AI may narrow. The experiments point in two directions. AI raises novices' output most (Section 8.3), but in the one study that tested learning directly, the same help reduced what they learned. Neither the payroll data nor the PwC advertisement analysis can show that this pipeline is being damaged [@pwc2026global]; they show only that the conditions for it exist.

## 8.8 Inequality, distribution and policy

### Who gains?

Within tasks, AI usually narrows gaps. In customer support the least skilled agents gained most [@brynjolfsson2025generative]; in professional writing inequality between participants fell [@noy2023experimental]; in software development junior developers gained more [@cui2026effects]. The Kenyan experiment shows the exception: where users must judge open-ended advice, AI helped strong entrepreneurs and harmed weak ones [@otis2026uneven].

Compression within a task does not imply compression across the economy, because the value of the task itself may fall. @acemoglu2025simple shows that AI can increase inequality even when it raises the productivity of low-skill workers in some tasks. Empirically he finds that AI's exposure is more evenly spread across demographic groups than earlier automation, so it is unlikely to raise inequality as much, but also no evidence that it will reduce labour-income inequality, and he predicts a widening gap between income from capital and from labour. The IMF note similarly warns that higher returns to capital could increase wealth inequality [@cazzaniga2024genai]. In an NBER working paper, David Autor has argued the opposite possibility: because AI can support expert judgement, it could let a larger set of workers with complementary knowledge take on some of the higher-stakes decisions now reserved for elite experts in medicine, law or software and, "if used well", help restore middle-skill work [@autor2024applying]. He presents this as a possibility that depends on how the technology is designed and deployed, not as a forecast.

Exposure is unevenly distributed across groups and countries. On the Eloundou rubric, higher-wage occupations are generally more exposed, the reverse of earlier machine-learning measures [@eloundou2023gpts]. In high-income countries, according to the ILO–NASK index, jobs in the highest exposure gradient account for 9.6% of female employment but only 3.5% of male employment [@ilo2025one; @gmyrek2025generative]. The IMF finds that women and college-educated workers are more exposed but also better placed to benefit, and that older workers may find it harder to adapt; advanced economies are more exposed but also better prepared, which could widen income gaps between countries [@cazzaniga2024genai].

### The policy response

Governments have responded mainly through existing institutions, with a few targeted rules. The most concrete is the European Union's AI Act, whose wider structure Chapter 13 describes. It classifies as "high-risk" AI systems used to recruit or select workers, to make decisions on promotion or termination, to allocate tasks on the basis of individual behaviour or traits, and to monitor and evaluate workers' performance [@europeanparliament2024regulation, Annex III, point 4]. Employers deploying such systems must inform workers' representatives and the affected workers before using them [@europeanparliament2024regulation, art. 26(7)]. The 2026 Digital Omnibus deferred the application of these high-risk obligations from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027 [@europeanparliament2026regulation].

Beyond specific rules, four positions can be distinguished, and they answer different questions. The first concerns *which technologies are built*. In a 2023 policy memo, Acemoglu, Autor and Johnson argue that the private sector is pursuing a path that emphasises automation and workplace surveillance, and propose equalising tax rates on employing workers and on owning equipment and algorithms, limiting workplace surveillance, and funding research on technologies that complement workers [@acemoglu2023can]. Others put more weight on measuring effects before acting; the Yale Budget Lab, for example, stresses that "better data is needed" [@gimbel2025evaluating].

The second concerns *how fast AI spreads*. The US government's AI Action Plan of July 2025 commits to removing "red tape and onerous regulation" [@whitehouse2025winning]. Its labour response is a "worker-first" agenda built on existing institutions: AI skills in training and apprenticeship programmes, retraining for displaced workers, and an AI Workforce Research Hub in the Department of Labor. On this view the state's task is to help workers move, not to shape the technology.

The third concerns *who controls AI's use at work*. Sectoral bargaining, as in the writers' agreements of 2023 and 2026, can fit rules to particular tasks, and the AI Act's duty to inform workers' representatives gives it a legal foothold in the EU. It reaches only organised workers, which excludes most of the platform workers described in Section 8.6.

The fourth concerns *who bears the losses*. @korinek2019artificial show in simple economic models that, under plausible conditions, governments can tax the gains from AI without distorting incentives and use the revenue to compensate those who would otherwise lose. In a 2021 essay, Sam Altman, the chief executive of OpenAI, proposed an "American Equity Fund" paying out to every adult citizen, financed by an annual tax of 2.5% on the value of large companies and of land [@altman2021moores]. The most direct evidence on unconditional payments comes from a trial not designed with AI in mind. In a large US experiment, 1,000 low-income people received $1,000 a month for three years and 2,000 received $50 a month [@vivalt2026employment]. The larger payments reduced labour-market participation by 4.2 percentage points and working hours by one to two hours a week. Leisure rose most, and the quality of employment did not improve.
