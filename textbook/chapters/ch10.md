# Chapter 10: Education, Health and Everyday Life

In June and July 2024, first-year senior secondary students from nine public schools in Benin City, Nigeria, took part in an after-school programme to practise English. They worked in pairs with Microsoft Copilot, a chatbot built on GPT-4. Teachers set the topic and the opening prompt. The students then questioned the chatbot freely for 90 minutes, up to twice a week, for six weeks. Places were allocated by lottery among the students who volunteered, which turned the programme into a randomised controlled trial. In a working paper, the World Bank team that evaluated it reported a gain of 0.31 standard deviations on an end-of-programme assessment, and 0.23 standard deviations on the English component alone [@desimone2025chalkboards].

A few months earlier, in autumn 2023, a field experiment in a large high school in Turkey gave nearly 1,000 mathematics students access to GPT-4 during practice sessions. Students with an unrestricted chatbot solved more practice problems, then scored 17% lower than students without access when they sat an exam on their own [@bastani2025generative]. The two studies used similar technology and reached opposite headline results. The difference lies in how the tools were set up, what was measured and when, and the same questions decide how to read the evidence on AI in clinics and in private life. Effects on jobs and productivity are treated in Chapter 8, the general taxonomy of AI risks in Chapter 11, and the enforcement of data-protection law against AI developers in Chapter 13.

## 10.1 AI tutors: the benchmark and the trials

The hope behind AI tutoring has a long pedigree. In 1984 the educational psychologist Benjamin Bloom reported that students taught one-to-one by a tutor performed about two standard deviations better than students taught in a conventional class. He called the search for group methods that could match this the "2 sigma problem" [@bloom1984sigma]. Later research confirmed that tutoring works, but with much smaller effects. Kurt VanLehn's 2011 review of experiments comparing human tutoring, computer tutoring and no tutoring found an effect of 0.79 standard deviations for human tutors, far below Bloom's figure. Intelligent tutoring systems, the rule-based programs that give feedback on each step of a solution, reached 0.76, nearly as much [@vanlehn2011relative]. A meta-analysis of 50 controlled evaluations of such systems found a median effect of 0.66 standard deviations, but the effect depended heavily on whether the test had been written by the researchers or was a standardised one [@kulik2016effectiveness].

The best current estimate for human tutoring in schools comes from a meta-analysis of randomised field experiments in pre-school to year 12. It found a pooled effect of 0.288 standard deviations. Effects were largest for programmes that used teachers or paraprofessionals as tutors, took place in earlier grades, met at least three days a week and ran during the school day [@nickow2020impressive]. The obstacle has always been cost: a trained tutor for every pupil several times a week is unaffordable for most school systems. Large language models (LLMs, the text-generating systems described in Chapter 3) revived the question of whether software could supply part of what a tutor provides at a fraction of the price.

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: How to read an effect size and an education trial**

Education studies report results in standard deviations (SD) of the test score so that different tests can be compared. If scores are roughly normally distributed, an effect of 0.3 SD moves the average treated student from the 50th to about the 62nd percentile of the comparison group; 0.1 SD moves them to the 54th, and 0.63 SD to about the 74th. The psychologist Jacob Cohen's conventions, which call 0.2 "small" and 0.8 "large", come from laboratory psychology and mislead in schools. Matthew Kraft assembled 1,942 effect sizes from 747 randomised trials of education interventions with standardised achievement outcomes. Their median was 0.10 SD, and he proposed treating less than 0.05 as small, 0.05 to 0.20 as medium and 0.20 or more as large [@kraft2020interpreting]. By that yardstick, all the AI tutoring results below are large, which is a reason for both interest and suspicion.

Suspicion, because study design inflates effect sizes. In 645 studies drawn from 12 reviews, Cheung and Slavin found effects roughly twice as large in published articles as in unpublished reports, in small trials as in large ones, and on measures made by the experimenters as on independent tests. Quasi-experiments, which compare groups not formed by chance, produced larger effects than randomised experiments [@cheung2016how]. Three further details change how much weight a trial deserves. The first is the unit of randomisation: randomising whole classes, as in the Turkish trial, prevents students from sharing tools across arms, but students in the same class resemble one another, so the effective sample is smaller than the head count. The second is the estimand, the precise quantity a study sets out to estimate. An "intention-to-treat" estimate compares everyone offered a programme with everyone who was not, whether or not they attended, and answers the policy question "what happens if we offer this?" The third is attrition: if weaker students in one arm skip the final test, the remaining groups are no longer comparable.
:::

Of 1,328 volunteers, 657 were assigned to the programme and 671 to a control group that continued normal lessons; 422 and 337 respectively sat the final assessment [@desimone2025chalkboards]. The authors estimate that the six-week programme produced gains equivalent to 1.5 to 2 years of ordinary schooling, at about US\$48 per pupil (US\$9 at the margin), which would place it among the more cost-effective education interventions evaluated in developing countries. Effects were positive across the range of prior attainment but largest for girls and for students who were already doing well; the authors caution that the result for girls may partly reflect one girls-only school. Attendance averaged 72%, so effects for students who actually attended are larger than the intention-to-treat figures. More control than treated students missed the final test, and the authors correct for this statistically. The paper has not been peer-reviewed.

The Harvard trial tested a different question: could a carefully built AI tutor beat a good lesson, not merely no lesson? Kestin and colleagues compared an AI tutor, used at home, with an in-class "active learning" session in an introductory physics course for life-science students in autumn 2023 [@kestin2025ai]. In the crossover design, 194 students each took one lesson in each format, on two topics, so that every student served as their own comparison. Median post-test scores were 4.5 in the AI condition and 3.5 in class, from a pre-test median of 2.75. A regression adjusting for prior knowledge gave an effect of 0.63 SD, which the authors consider an underestimate because many students reached the top of the scale. Students using the tutor spent a median of 49 minutes, against 60 minutes in class, and rated their engagement at 4.1 against 3.6. The tutor was not a general chatbot: it was given step-by-step solutions written by the instructors and a system prompt built on research about how students learn. The material was introductory and new to the students, the lessons targeted understanding and application rather than complex synthesis, and some of the authors developed the active-learning methods used as the comparison, which makes it a demanding benchmark but also gives the team a stake in both arms.

Two further studies extend the range. In Ghana, Rori, a maths tutor that runs on WhatsApp on basic phones, was used by about 1,000 pupils in grades 3 to 9 at 11 schools for two 30-minute sessions a week over eight months. Schools were assigned to treatment or control, and the preprint reports an effect of 0.37 on maths growth scores [@henkel2024effective]. With only 11 schools as the unit of assignment, the uncertainty is larger than the student count suggests. Tutor CoPilot took the opposite route: instead of replacing the tutor, it advised 900 human tutors working online with 1,800 pupils from under-served communities in the United States. Pupils whose tutors had access were 4 percentage points more likely to master topics, and pupils of the lowest-rated tutors gained 9 points. The system cost about US\$20 per tutor per year, and analysis of more than 550,000 messages showed that tutors with access asked more guiding questions and gave away answers less often [@wang2024tutor]. This study, too, is a preprint.

All of these trials were short and measured learning on tests close to what had just been taught, the condition under which Kulik and Fletcher and Cheung and Slavin found the largest effects. None tells us what a full school year with an AI tutor would achieve on an independent examination. Outside trials, students already use general chatbots for learning on a large scale, without the scaffolding the trial tutors had. In a Pew Research Center survey of 1,391 US teenagers in autumn 2024, 26% said they had used ChatGPT for schoolwork, double the 13% recorded a year earlier [@sidoti2025quarter]. A working paper by OpenAI researchers and academic economists, analysing ChatGPT consumer messages, estimated that about 10% of all messages in mid-2025 were requests for tutoring or teaching [@chatterji2025how].

## 10.2 When help harms learning

The Turkish study is the clearest experimental evidence that an AI assistant can raise performance while lowering learning. Bastani and colleagues ran four 90-minute sessions in about fifty classes in grades 9 to 11, covering roughly 15% of the semester's mathematics curriculum [@bastani2025generative]. Each class was randomly assigned to one of three arms. "GPT Base" was a plain GPT-4 chat interface. "GPT Tutor" used the same model with a prompt that told it to give hints instead of answers. Two part-time mathematics teachers supplied, for every problem, one or more correct solutions, common student mistakes and guidance on feedback, which were placed in the prompt so that the model would not mislead students. The control arm used textbooks and notes only. Each session began with the teacher reviewing a topic, followed by assisted practice and then a closed-book, closed-laptop exam in which every problem matched a conceptually similar practice problem.

Figure 10.1 shows the results. During practice, the control group scored an average of 0.284 (the share of points obtained). GPT Base raised this by 0.137, or 48%, and GPT Tutor by 0.361, or 127%. On the unassisted exam, where the control group averaged 0.321, GPT Base students scored 0.054 lower, a statistically significant 17% loss, which takes the average from about 32% of points to about 27%. GPT Tutor students scored 0.004 lower, indistinguishable from the control group [@bastani2025generative]. The authors traced the loss to students using the chatbot as a "crutch": the most common message in the GPT Base arm was a request for the answer. The base model was also unreliable: when the researchers asked it "What is the answer?" ten times for each of the 57 practice problems, it was correct 51% of the time, with logical errors in 42% of cases and arithmetic errors in 8%.

Three further findings sharpen the result. Students misjudged their own learning: those in the GPT Base arm did not perceive that they had performed worse or learned less, and those in the GPT Tutor arm believed they had done significantly better, although their exam scores matched the control group's. Preregistered tests for differences by ability, resources and effort found little heterogeneity, so the harm was not confined to weaker students. And the guarded tutor removed the harm without producing a gain on the exam [@bastani2025generative]. Design can prevent damage; it did not, in this trial, add learning. The study took place when chatbots were new to most students and covered one subject in one school.

The mechanism has a name in cognitive psychology. Cognitive offloading is "the use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements of a task so as to reduce cognitive demand", such as writing a reminder instead of remembering [@risko2016cognitive]. Offloading is often sensible, but learning depends on some of the effort it removes. Psychologists call the useful kind of effort a "desirable difficulty", and the Nigerian programme deliberately built retrieval practice and other such difficulties into its prompts [@desimone2025chalkboards]. Several smaller experiments with university students point the same way. In a laboratory study of 117 students writing an essay with help from ChatGPT, a human expert, writing-analytics tools or nothing, the ChatGPT group improved its essays most but gained no more knowledge and transferred no more of it to new tasks. The authors warn of "metacognitive laziness", a dependence on the tool that weakens learners' regulation of their own learning [@fan2025beware]. In another experiment, 91 students researched a socio-scientific question with either ChatGPT or Google. Those using the chatbot reported lower cognitive load but produced lower-quality reasoning in their final recommendations [@stadler2024cognitive].

Evidence from outside controlled experiments is weaker. A preprint from the MIT Media Lab recorded brain activity with electroencephalography (EEG, sensors on the scalp) while 54 participants wrote essays with ChatGPT, with a search engine or unaided over several sessions [@kosmyna2025brain]. Chatbot users showed the weakest connectivity between brain regions, the least sense of ownership of their essays and difficulty quoting from them. Only 18 participants completed the final session, and EEG connectivity is an indirect proxy for learning. Surveys show associations, not causes. In a survey of 319 knowledge workers who described 936 uses of generative AI, higher confidence in the AI was associated with less critical thinking and higher self-confidence with more [@lee2025impact]. A survey of 666 people found that more frequent AI use correlated with lower scores on a critical-thinking measure, mediated by cognitive offloading [@gerlich2025ai]. People who think less critically may simply rely more on AI, so such correlations cannot show which way the influence runs. Chapter 8 discusses the parallel evidence from medicine, where endoscopists' unaided detection rates fell after AI support was introduced.

Developers have responded with modes that imitate the GPT Tutor design. In July 2025 OpenAI launched a "study mode" for ChatGPT in which the system asks questions to check understanding and may withhold direct answers until the learner engages [@zeff2025openaia]. Such modes rely on instructions to the model and can be switched off by the student, which is the same freedom the GPT Base arm had. Whether optional guardrails change behaviour at scale has not been tested in a published randomised trial.

## 10.3 Cheating, detection and assessment

Universities face the practical side of the same question: how to assess what a student knows when a capable writing assistant is always available. The Higher Education Policy Institute (HEPI) has surveyed full-time UK undergraduates each year since 2024. In its 2026 survey of 1,054 students, polled in December 2025, 95% said they used AI in at least one way and 94% said they used generative AI to help with assessed work [@stephenson2026student]. The most common uses were having concepts explained (61%) and summarising articles (49%). The share who said they had included AI-generated text directly in assessed work rose from 3% in 2024 to 8% in 2025 and 12% in 2026. Teenagers draw distinctions of their own. In the Pew survey, 54% of US teens thought it acceptable to use ChatGPT to research new topics, but only 18% thought it acceptable for writing essays, and 42% thought it was not [@sidoti2025quarter].

One early response was to detect AI-written text automatically. Chapter 9 summarises independent tests of detection tools in general; in education, the decisive problem is whom the errors fall on. Many detectors rely partly on perplexity, a measure of how predictable a text is to a language model: machine-generated text tends to be highly predictable. Liang and colleagues tested seven widely used detectors on 91 essays written by non-native English speakers for the TOEFL exam. The detectors wrongly labelled more than half of them as AI-generated, with an average false-positive rate of 61%, while classifying essays by US eighth-grade students accurately [@liang2023gpt]. A false positive here means a human-written essay flagged as machine-written. The authors attribute the bias to the more limited vocabulary and syntax typical of non-native writers, which lowers perplexity in the same way that model generation does. When they asked ChatGPT to enrich the word choices in the TOEFL essays, the false-positive rate fell to 12%: the tools penalised honest non-native writers and could be evaded with the technology they were meant to detect. OpenAI withdrew its own AI-text classifier in July 2023, citing its "low rate of accuracy" [@coldewey2023openai].

Institutions have therefore shifted attention from detection to the design of assessment. In HEPI's 2026 survey, 65% of students said that assessment at their institution had changed significantly in response to AI [@stephenson2026student]. Common responses include more supervised examinations, oral defences of written work, assignments that require students to document their process, and tasks in which AI use is permitted and itself assessed. Each option has costs: supervised exams test a narrower range of skills, oral assessment is expensive to run at scale, and process documentation can itself be generated by AI. No study has yet shown which combination best protects the validity of degrees.

Monitoring students also raises questions of privacy and fairness, and the law has begun to respond. Since 2 February 2025, the EU AI Act has banned AI systems that infer the emotions of people in education institutions, except for medical or safety reasons [@europeanparliament2024regulation, Articles 5(1)(f) and 113]. It classes as "high-risk" the AI systems used to decide admission, evaluate learning outcomes, assign students to levels of education, or monitor and detect prohibited behaviour during tests [@europeanparliament2024regulation, Annex III(3)]. Automated marking and online proctoring therefore fall under the Act's strictest duties for permitted systems. After a 2026 amendment, these duties apply from 2 December 2027 [@europeanparliament2026regulation]; Chapter 13 describes what they require.

## 10.4 Medical AI: regulation and the evidence base

Medicine has a longer record of AI-based software than education, and a formal system for deciding which tools may be sold. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) maintains a public list of AI-enabled medical devices authorised for marketing. As of the update published in September 2026, it contained 1,614 devices, the earliest from 1995 and the most recent decided in June 2026 [@fda2026artificial]. Radiology accounted for 1,230 of them, about 76%, followed by cardiovascular devices (154) and neurology (73). Figure 10.2 shows how annual authorisations grew from 6 in 2015 to 335 in 2025.

The route by which these devices reached the market matters more than their number. Table 10.2 counts the list by submission type. Almost all were cleared through the 510(k) pathway, in which a manufacturer shows that its device is "as safe and effective, that is, substantially equivalent" to a legally marketed device, known as the predicate [@fda2024premarket]. The FDA's review compares technological characteristics and performance data, which "can include clinical data" but can also consist of bench testing and software validation. A device with no suitable predicate and low-to-moderate risk can use the De Novo route, and the resulting device can then serve as a predicate for later 510(k) submissions [@fda2025novo]. Premarket approval, the most demanding route, is reserved for class III devices, those that support or sustain life or present a potential unreasonable risk [@fda2019premarket].

The count is easy to over-read in other ways too. The FDA describes its list as not comprehensive, because it identifies devices mainly by searching their public summaries for AI-related terms. Authorisation is a judgement about safety and effectiveness for a stated intended use; it is not proof that the device improves patient outcomes. The list does not yet show which devices, if any, use generative AI. The FDA has said it will explore ways to tag devices that incorporate LLMs and other foundation models, and a search of the September 2026 list found no ambient documentation tools of the kind discussed in Section 10.5 [@fda2026artificial]. Machine-learning models also raise a problem the device system was not built for: they can be retrained. The FDA, Health Canada and the UK's Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency have set out principles for "predetermined change control plans", in which a manufacturer specifies in advance the planned modifications, the protocol for implementing and controlling them, and how their impact will be assessed, so that some updates need no new submission [@fda2025predetermined]. In the EU, AI that is part of a medical device covered by EU product-safety law is high-risk under the AI Act, with obligations deferred by the 2026 amendment to 2 August 2028 [@europeanparliament2026regulation]; Table 13.1 gives the dates.

Most published evidence on medical AI comes from diagnostic accuracy studies, which compare a system's outputs with a reference standard on a set of stored cases. Two measures dominate them, and they are easy to confuse.

::: {.box .concept}
**Concept: Sensitivity, specificity and external validation**

Sensitivity is the share of people who have a condition whom a test correctly flags; specificity is the share of people without the condition whom it correctly clears. A smoke alarm that goes off at every slice of burnt toast has high sensitivity and low specificity. In a screening population where few people are ill, even high specificity produces many false alarms: a test with 98.5% specificity used on 10,000 healthy people flags 150 of them. Studies often summarise performance across all possible alarm thresholds with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), where 0.5 is chance and 1.0 perfect ranking. External validation means testing a model on data from a different hospital, population or time period from the data it was built on. It matters because a medical model, unlike a smoke alarm, can perform well where it was trained and poorly elsewhere: differences in equipment, patients and recording practices change the inputs it sees.
:::

A 2019 systematic review in *The Lancet Digital Health* examined studies from 2012 to 2019 that compared deep learning models with health-care professionals on medical images [@liu2019comparison]. In the 14 studies that tested both on the same external sample, pooled sensitivity was 87.0% for the models and 86.4% for clinicians, and pooled specificity was 92.5% and 90.5%. The authors' main conclusion concerned the evidence base: of 82 included studies, only 25 performed any external validation, and poor reporting was widespread. A 2025 meta-analysis of 83 studies of generative AI models on diagnostic tasks found an overall accuracy of 52.1%. This was not significantly different from physicians overall or from non-specialist physicians, and significantly lower than specialists [@takita2025systematic]. Both reviews pool very different tasks and models, so their averages describe the literature more than any single product.

Accuracy on stored images is a step removed from what matters to patients, and randomised trials remain scarce. A systematic review found 41 randomised trials of machine-learning interventions published up to October 2021, with a median of 294 participants; 51% were conducted at a single site, and none adhered to all items of CONSORT-AI, the reporting guideline for trials of AI interventions [@plana2022randomized]. CONSORT-AI, published in 2020, added 14 items to the standard trial-reporting checklist, among them a description of how the AI was integrated into the clinical setting, how inputs and outputs were handled, and an analysis of error cases [@liu2020reporting]. A later scoping review identified 86 randomised trials of AI in clinical practice published between January 2018 and November 2023. Of these, 70 (81%) reported positive primary endpoints, but 54 (63%) were single-centre, 37 (43%) were in gastroenterology, and 46 (54%) had a primary endpoint of diagnostic yield or performance rather than a patient outcome. The authors warn that publication bias probably inflates the share of positive results [@han2024randomised].

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: What makes medical AI evidence strong**

The reviews above suggest a hierarchy of questions. Does the model agree with a reference standard on stored cases (retrospective accuracy)? Does it do so on data from other hospitals, populations and periods (external validation)? Do clinicians reading cases with the model do better than without it (reader studies, such as the vignette trials in Section 10.5)? Does introducing it in real care, compared at random with care without it, change what happens to patients and staff (prospective trials such as MASAI)? And does the effect hold up after deployment, as patient mixes and recording practices drift (post-market monitoring)? Each rung costs more and answers a question closer to patient benefit. Strong evidence also has features that cut across the rungs: an outcome that matters to patients rather than a surrogate; a comparator that reflects current good practice; preregistration; independence from the vendor; multiple sites; reporting of subgroups and error cases; and enough follow-up to count what the tool misses. Most devices on the FDA list reached the market through claims of equivalence to earlier devices (Table 10.2), and the randomised trials cluster in a few specialties [@han2024randomised].
:::

## 10.5 Medical AI in practice: screening, prediction, conversation and paperwork

The most informative trial so far comes from breast screening. In the Swedish MASAI trial, women attending screening at four sites were randomly assigned either to standard double reading by two radiologists or to AI-supported reading. In the AI arm, a commercial system gave each examination a malignancy risk score from 1 to 10; examinations scoring 1 to 9 went to a single radiologist and those scoring 10 to two, with the AI's marks of suspicious areas shown for scores of 8 to 10 [@lang2023artificial]. A prespecified safety analysis of the first 80,033 women found 244 screen-detected cancers in the AI arm against 203 in the control arm, detection rates of 6.1 and 5.1 per 1,000, a difference with a p-value of 0.052. The false-positive rate was 1.5% in both arms. The AI arm needed 46,345 screen readings against 83,231, a 44.3% reduction in radiologists' reading workload. Part of the extra detection was in situ cancer (60 against 38 cases), early lesions of which some would never have caused harm; this is why the trial's primary endpoint was interval cancers.

The final results, covering 105,934 women randomised between April 2021 and December 2022 and followed for two years, were published in *The Lancet* in 2026. There were 82 interval cancers (cancers diagnosed between screening rounds) in the AI arm against 93 in the control arm, rates of 1.55 and 1.76 per 1,000. The ratio of 0.88 had a 95% confidence interval of 0.65 to 1.18, which met the trial's non-inferiority criterion but does not show a significant reduction. Fewer of the AI arm's interval cancers were invasive (75 against 89). Sensitivity was 80.5% against 73.8%, and specificity was 98.5% in both arms [@gommers2026interval; @lunduniversity2026ai]. The trial tested one product in one national programme, with radiologists who knew which arm they were reading. A German observational study of 463,094 women screened in 2021–2023, funded by a different AI vendor, found higher detection without a higher recall rate when radiologists chose to use AI support [@eisemann2025nationwide]. Because radiologists chose when to use it, that study cannot rule out differences between the cases they selected.

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: The Epic Sepsis Model**

Sepsis is a life-threatening response to infection, and early recognition matters. Epic, a large US electronic health record vendor, offered hospitals a proprietary model that scored inpatients' risk of sepsis every 15 minutes, and it was implemented at hundreds of US hospitals before any independent evaluation was published. In 2021, researchers at Michigan Medicine reported an external validation covering 27,697 patients and 38,455 hospitalisations, of which 2,552 involved sepsis [@wong2021external]. The model's AUC was 0.63. At the alert threshold used, it missed 1,709 of the sepsis patients (67%) while generating alerts for 6,971 of all hospitalised patients (18%), a heavy burden of alert fatigue. It identified only 183 sepsis patients (7%) whom clinicians had not already treated with timely antibiotics. An updated version was validated prospectively at four large US health systems, covering 227,091 inpatient encounters between August 2023 and March 2025 and reported in 2026 [@wong2026multicenter]. Discrimination improved, with encounter-level AUC between 0.82 and 0.92, but performance varied between sites, and only 13% to 26% of alerts identified a patient who met sepsis criteria. A model can improve on paper and still impose a high cost in staff attention, and only independent, multi-site evaluation reveals either.
:::

A natural assumption is that a clinician with an AI assistant will outperform either alone. Trials suggest this is not automatic. In a randomised trial in late 2023, 50 US physicians worked through clinical case vignettes with or without access to GPT-4 [@goh2024large]. The median diagnostic reasoning score was 76% with the chatbot and 74% without, a difference that was not statistically significant. In a secondary analysis, GPT-4 working alone scored a median of 92%. A follow-up trial with 92 physicians tested management reasoning, meaning decisions about further testing and the balance of risks. Those using GPT-4 scored 6.5 percentage points higher than those using conventional resources and took about two minutes longer per case; physicians with GPT-4 did no better than GPT-4 alone [@goh2025gpt]. A 2024 meta-analysis of 106 experiments across many fields found that human–AI combinations performed on average worse than the better of the two working alone (Hedges' *g* = −0.23). Combinations lost value in decision tasks and when the AI was the stronger partner, and gained in content creation and when the human was stronger [@vaccaro2024combinations]. Chapter 11 discusses automation bias, the tendency to defer to automated suggestions even when they are wrong, which is one explanation.

Google's AMIE (Articulate Medical Intelligence Explorer) shows how a conversational system has been taken up the evidence ladder. In a randomised, double-blind crossover study of text-based consultations with validated patient-actors across 159 case scenarios, specialists rated AMIE better than 20 primary-care physicians on 30 of 32 axes, including diagnostic accuracy, and patient-actors rated it better on 25 of 26. The authors note that clinicians were working in an unfamiliar text-chat format [@tu2025towards]. In a second study, 20 clinicians worked on 302 difficult published case reports. AMIE alone listed the correct diagnosis among its top ten in 59.1% of cases against 33.6% for unassisted clinicians; clinicians assisted by AMIE reached 51.7%, against 44.4% for those using search engines [@mcduff2025towards]. The first real-world step was a single-centre feasibility study in Boston, published in *The Lancet* in October 2026. There, 98 patients talked with AMIE before an urgent primary-care appointment while physicians supervised every conversation with authority to stop it [@brodeur2026conversational]. No conversation needed to be stopped under the predefined safety criteria; supervisors noted one hallucination and added clinical information in five conversations. Primary-care physicians found AMIE helpful for visit preparation in 33 of the 44 cases in which they had reviewed its transcript beforehand. The study had no control group and was funded by Alphabet. The fastest-spreading clinical use of generative AI involves no diagnosis at all. "Ambient scribes" listen, with consent, to a consultation and draft a clinical note for the doctor to review. The largest randomised trial so far assigned 238 outpatient physicians at UCLA Health, across 14 specialties and about 72,000 encounters, to one of two commercial scribes or to usual practice for two months from late 2024 [@lukac2025ambient]. With one product, time spent writing each note fell by about 9.5% more than in the control group, roughly 41 seconds from a baseline of four and a half minutes; with the other, the reduction was not statistically significant. Both groups reported modest improvements on burnout and workload measures. Drafts occasionally contained clinically significant inaccuracies, mostly omissions, and one mild patient-safety event occurred. A clinician who signs an AI-drafted note remains accountable for its content, so the value of the tool depends on whether checking is quicker and more reliable than writing.

## 10.6 Companions, loneliness and mental health

Not all personal use of AI is practical. Some people use chatbots for conversation, emotional support or companionship, either through general assistants or through apps designed as "AI companions", such as Character.AI and Replika. How common this is depends on the population studied. In the analysis of ChatGPT consumer messages, only 1.9% concerned relationships and personal reflection and 0.4% games and role play [@chatterji2025how]. Among US teenagers the picture differs. A survey of 1,060 teens aged 13 to 17, conducted by NORC for the advocacy organisation Common Sense Media in April and May 2025, found that 72% had used an AI companion at least once and 52% did so at least a few times a month [@robb2025talk]. A third of users said they had chosen to discuss something important with an AI instead of a person, yet 67% of all respondents found AI conversations less satisfying than those with friends. The survey's definition included general chatbots used as companions, and 341 of its respondents came from a non-probability panel, so the figures are indicative.

The most detailed experimental evidence comes from two linked preprints by researchers at the MIT Media Lab and OpenAI. In the first, 981 participants used ChatGPT daily for four weeks under randomly assigned conditions that varied the chatbot's voice (text, a neutral voice or an engaging voice) and the type of conversation (open, personal or non-personal) [@fang2025ai]. The assigned conditions had no significant effect on loneliness, social contact with other people, emotional dependence on AI or problematic use. The strongest pattern was correlational: participants who chose to use the chatbot more had worse outcomes on all four measures, whatever their condition. The second study combined this trial with an automated analysis of more than three million conversations and a survey of over 4,000 users; a small number of very heavy users accounted for a disproportionate share of emotionally laden conversations [@phang2025investigating]. A separate preprint surveyed 1,131 US adult users of Character.AI and analysed 4,664 chat sessions donated by 237 of them. People with smaller social networks were more likely to name companionship as their main use, and companionship use was associated with lower well-being, more strongly when interactions were intensive and highly self-disclosing [@zhang2025rise]. Because OpenAI researchers co-authored the ChatGPT studies, independent replication matters more than usual.

All of these designs struggle with the same problem. Loneliness and dependence are measured by questionnaires, which shift with mood and expectation, and four weeks is short for effects on relationships to appear. Above all, heavy users are not a random sample. Someone already isolated may both use a chatbot more and feel lonelier, so an association between use and loneliness does not show which causes which. Randomised trials can assign conditions, but not how much people choose to use a tool, which is the variable that seems to matter most.

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Do AI companions relieve loneliness or deepen it?**

The case for relief rests on experiments. In five studies published in the *Journal of Consumer Research*, De Freitas and colleagues found that a session with an AI companion reduced self-reported loneliness about as much as chatting with another person, and more than watching online videos. It produced reductions after use throughout a week of daily use, and the effect depended mainly on whether users felt heard [@defreitas2025ai]. These were short-term measurements, however, and say little about months of use. The case for harm is argued at book length by the MIT psychologist Sherry Turkle in *Artificial Intimacy* (2026): chatbots offer a performance of empathy that may weaken people's capacity for the demands of human relationships and leave them lonelier over time [@turkle2026artificial]. Cross-sectional data linking heavy companionship use with lower well-being [@fang2025ai; @zhang2025rise] fit this view, but they fit equally well the possibility that lonelier people seek out companions. A decisive study would randomise access, follow people for months, measure their offline relationships rather than only their feelings, and model why some participants come to use the chatbot heavily.
:::

Mental-health applications sit between companionship and medicine. The first randomised trial of a generative-AI therapy chatbot was published in *NEJM AI* in 2025. It enrolled 210 adults with clinically significant symptoms of major depression, generalised anxiety or a high risk of eating disorders. They were assigned to four weeks of access to Therabot, a research system fine-tuned by a team at Dartmouth College (106 participants), or to a waiting list without access until after the eight-week follow-up (104) [@heinz2025randomized]. Participants with access reported larger reductions in symptoms than the control group. According to the university, average symptom scores among Therabot users fell by 51% for depression and 31% for anxiety, users spent an average of about six hours with the chatbot, and they rated their working relationship with it at levels comparable to those reported for human therapists [@kelly2025first]. Those percentages are within-group changes, not differences from the control group. The research team reviewed conversations during the trial and was ready to intervene in an emergency, and the lead author stated that no generative AI system was ready to operate fully autonomously in mental health. A waiting-list design shows that access helped compared with no help; it cannot show whether the chatbot is better or worse than a human therapist or an established digital programme.

General chatbots meet people in crisis far more often than research systems do. In October 2025 OpenAI estimated that in a given week 0.15% of ChatGPT's active users had conversations including "explicit indicators of potential suicidal planning or intent", which, with more than 800 million weekly users, implies over a million people a week. According to the company, its updated model behaved as intended in 91% of cases in an evaluation of such conversations, against 77% for the previous version [@zeff2025openaic]. Families have brought lawsuits against chatbot companies following the deaths of teenagers who had held long conversations with chatbots, and these cases shaped the policy response [@bellan2025california]. In September 2025 the US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) used its study powers to order seven companies, including Alphabet, Meta, OpenAI, Character Technologies and xAI, to report how they measure and limit harms from chatbots acting as companions, particularly to children [@ftc2025launches]. In October 2025 Character.AI announced that it would end open-ended chat for users under 18 by 25 November, with a daily limit starting at two hours in the meantime and age checks combining its own age-estimation model with third-party tools [@characterai2025taking].

Legislation has concentrated on disclosure, crisis procedures and minors (Table 10.3). California's SB 243, in force from January 2026, requires operators of companion chatbots to disclose that the chatbot is artificial wherever a reasonable person could be misled into thinking it is human, and to maintain and publish a protocol for responding to users who express suicidal thoughts [@california2025sb243; @gluck2025understanding]. For users known to be minors, they must disclose that the user is talking to an AI and give a reminder to take a break at least every three hours, and from July 2027 they must report annually on crisis referrals. A New York law enacted in 2025 requires disclosure at the start of each interaction and at least every three hours, together with reasonable efforts to detect and address self-harm [@gluck2025understanding].

Data protection is a live issue in this area. In 2023 the FTC ordered the online counselling service BetterHelp to pay US\$7.8 million after finding that, despite promising not to, it had shared users' email addresses, IP addresses and health questionnaire information with Facebook, Snapchat and other advertising platforms [@ftc2023ftc]. Italy's data-protection authority blocked the companion app Replika in February 2023 and in May 2025 fined its developer, Luka Inc., €5 million for processing users' data without a legal basis, with an inadequate privacy policy and without any means of verifying users' ages. It also opened a separate investigation into how the model behind the app was trained [@garante2025replika]. These measures target disclosure, data handling and process; the research reviewed above does not yet identify which design features, if any, cause harm.

## 10.7 Privacy: what happens to personal conversations

Tutors, clinical tools and companions all collect personal information, and the protection it receives depends on who holds it. Under the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), data concerning health, together with genetic and biometric data and data revealing political opinions or religious beliefs, belong to "special categories" of personal data. Processing them is prohibited unless one of a list of exceptions applies, such as the person's explicit consent for specified purposes [@europeanparliament2016regulation, Article 9]. In the United States, the main health-privacy law, HIPAA, applies to "covered entities" (health plans, health-care clearinghouses and health-care providers that conduct standard transactions electronically) and in part to their business associates. Companies outside it that collect health information are governed by the general consumer-protection law enforced by the FTC [@ftc2024collecting]. A diagnosis typed into a hospital's patient portal is therefore protected by HIPAA; the same words typed into a general chatbot usually are not.

The difference matters because people increasingly take health questions to general chatbots. In January 2026 OpenAI launched ChatGPT Health, a separate space for health conversations that can draw on data from wellness apps. According to the company, more than 230 million people asked ChatGPT health and wellness questions each week, and conversations in the new space would not be used to train its models [@silberling2026openai]. For a US consumer product, such promises are enforced mainly through the FTC's powers against deceptive practices, as in the BetterHelp case.

What developers do with conversations by default is the first question. King and colleagues (2025) analysed the privacy policies of six US developers: Amazon, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Microsoft and OpenAI. All six appeared to use users' chats to train their models by default, some kept chat data indefinitely, four appeared to include children's chats in training, and the policies often lacked essential information about these practices [@king2025user]. Policies also change. In August 2025 Anthropic asked users of its consumer Claude plans to choose by 8 October whether their chats and coding sessions could be used to train models; for those who agreed, retention was extended from 30 days to five years, while business, education and API services were excluded [@anthropic2025updates]. Retention can also be dictated by courts. In the copyright case brought by The New York Times, a US court ordered OpenAI in 2025 to preserve consumer ChatGPT and API conversation data that would otherwise have been deleted; according to OpenAI, the obligation, which excluded its Enterprise and Edu customers, ended on 26 September 2025, although it said it would keep storing a limited set of data from April to September 2025 that the newspaper still sought [@openai2025response].

Training on conversations matters because language models can memorise. Carlini and colleagues (2021) showed that by querying GPT-2 they could extract hundreds of verbatim sequences from its training data, including publicly available names, phone numbers and email addresses that had appeared in only one document, and that larger models were more vulnerable [@carlini2021extracting]. Assistant "memory" adds a second channel. Since 2024 OpenAI has rolled out a function that carries details between ChatGPT conversations; users can view or delete memories, turn the function off or use a temporary chat [@wiggers2024chatgpt]. In April 2025 it extended this so that the assistant could refer to a user's past conversations as a whole, enabled by default for users who already had memory switched on. The feature was initially not offered in the European Union, the United Kingdom and some neighbouring countries, where the company said additional regulatory review was needed [@zeff2025openaib].

Memory creates a risk that a stateless chatbot does not: information shared in one setting, such as a health worry, may resurface in another where the user would not expect it. Researchers have tested this with "contextual integrity", a framework holding that a flow of information is appropriate only if it matches the norms of the context in which the information was shared. On the hardest tasks in one benchmark, GPT-4 and ChatGPT revealed private information in situations where humans would not 39% and 57% of the time, even when instructed to protect privacy [@mireshghallah2024can]. For a pupil whose tutor remembers a disclosed family problem, or a patient whose general assistant links a symptom to their shopping requests, the question is not whether data are stored securely but whether they flow where the person would expect.

European regulators have applied the GDPR to model training itself. Section 13.6 describes a 2024 opinion of the EU's data-protection authorities on when models trained on personal data can be lawful, and an Italian fine on OpenAI against which a Rome court upheld the company's appeal in 2026 [@edpb2024opinion; @garante2024chatgpt]. For students, patients and companion users alike, the protection their conversations receive depends on the provider's defaults and settings, the product tier they use, the jurisdiction they live in and the outcome of cases still being decided.

## 10.8 Accessibility

For some users, AI tools change what they can do independently. The World Health Organization estimates that 1.3 billion people, 16% of the world's population, experience a significant disability [@who2023disability]. Machine translation is one example of reach. Meta's No Language Left Behind project, published in *Nature* in 2024, trained a single model to translate between 200 languages, including many with little available training text. It reported an average improvement of 44% over the previous best systems on BLEU, a standard automatic metric that counts overlapping word sequences with reference translations [@nllbteam2024scaling]. A follow-up model translates speech and text across up to 100 languages [@seamlesscommunicationteam2025joint]. Automatic metrics are imperfect proxies for whether a translation is usable in a hospital, court or classroom, and the parallel text needed to train such systems is unevenly available across the world's more than 7,000 languages.

Speech recognition is a second case. The Speech Accessibility Project at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, supported by Amazon, Apple, Google, Meta and Microsoft, collects recordings from people with conditions such as Parkinson's disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), cerebral palsy, Down syndrome and stroke, to improve recognition of their speech; about 2,000 participants had submitted recordings by the end of September 2025. According to the project, Microsoft reported accuracy gains of 18% to 60% from using the data [@speechaccessibility2025speech]. At the far end of the range, implanted brain–computer interfaces now use machine-learning decoders to turn attempted speech into text. In a 2023 study, a participant with ALS who could no longer speak intelligibly reached 62 words per minute, with a 23.8% word error rate on a 125,000-word vocabulary [@willett2023highperformance]. In a 2024 study of another man with ALS, the system reached 97.5% accuracy over 8.4 months and he used it for more than 248 hours of conversation at about 32 words per minute [@card2024accurate]. These are single-participant research studies requiring brain surgery, far from a product.

Visual assistance shows the gap between demonstration and dependable aid. In March 2023 Be My Eyes, an app that connects blind and low-vision people with sighted volunteers through a phone camera, announced a GPT-4-based "virtual volunteer". According to the company, it describes images and answers questions about them, with the option of calling a human volunteer [@bemyeyes2023introducing]. Independent evaluation is limited. In a two-week diary study of 16 blind and low-vision participants presented at the CHI 2024 conference, participants used AI scene descriptions for a range of everyday tasks, rated their satisfaction at an average of 2.76 out of 5 and their trust at 2.43 out of 4, and the authors concluded that descriptions needed significant improvement before they were trustworthy [@gonzalezpenuela2024investigating]. A description that is right most of the time can still mislead at a moment that matters, the same problem that separates accuracy studies from clinical trials in medicine and practice scores from exam scores in the classroom.
