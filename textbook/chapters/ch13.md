# Chapter 13: Governing AI: Law, Policy and International Coordination

The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act was written around a date. Its main rules for "high-risk" AI systems, such as software that screens job applicants or decides access to public benefits, were to apply from 2 August 2026, and a year later for AI built into regulated products such as medical devices or toys [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. When the first date arrived, the rules did not apply. On 27 July 2026 an amending law, the Digital Omnibus on AI, had entered into force and moved the two deadlines to 2 December 2027 and 2 August 2028. The legislators gave a practical reason. The technical standards and the national authorities needed to apply the rules were not ready [@europeanparliament2026regulation; @councileu2026proposal].

In the same months the US state of Colorado retreated from its broad AI law. Elon Musk's company xAI had sued the state, and the US Department of Justice sought to join the case. Colorado agreed in court not to enforce the law, then replaced it with a narrower one before it took effect [@usdccolorado2026minute; @coloradogeneralassembly2026automated]. Every rule below is therefore dated, with its status as of October 2026.

## 13.1 The governance toolkit

**Governance** means the full set of rules, institutions and practices that shape how a technology is built and used. Beyond statutes, governments issue executive orders and guidance, companies sign voluntary commitments, standards bodies write technical specifications, and courts apply older law on copyright, negligence or discrimination to new cases.

**Hard law** is binding and enforceable, such as an EU regulation or a US state statute, and is usually backed by fines. **Soft law** covers declarations, principles and voluntary codes; it relies on reputation, peer pressure and the prospect of later legislation. **Technical standards** describe how to do something, for example how to run a risk-management process, and often become the practical route to complying with hard law. **Liability** rules decide who pays when harm occurs, so they act after the fact rather than before it.

Laws also differ in scope. A **horizontal** law applies across the whole economy to anything that meets its definition of AI; the EU AI Act is the leading example. A **sectoral** approach leaves AI to the existing regulators of finance, health, transport and so on. The UK's 2023 white paper chose the sectoral path, setting five principles (safety, transparency, fairness, accountability, and contestability and redress) for existing regulators to apply without a new AI statute or AI regulator [@dsit2023proinnovation]. The choice matters more for AI than for most technologies, because a general-purpose model built with one sector in mind can be repurposed for all of them after release.

::: {.box .concept}
**Concept: Risk-based regulation**

Risk-based regulation scales obligations to the harm a system could cause in a given use, that is, to what the system is used for and who could be hurt. A spam filter faces no new duties. A system that ranks job candidates must meet documentation, testing and human-oversight requirements, and a few uses are banned outright. The approach concentrates effort where harm is likeliest. It depends on drawing the categories well, and general-purpose models that can serve any use fit it awkwardly, which is why the EU regulates them in a separate regime.
:::

Each instrument has a weakness. Hard law is slow to change while frontier models change within months. Soft law carries no penalty. Standards take years and are drafted largely by industry experts, and liability operates only after harm. Jurisdictions therefore mix instruments, and the mix reveals their priorities. The EU leads with a horizontal statute, the US federal government with executive orders and procurement, China with agency rules backed by registration, and the UK with existing regulators.

## 13.2 The European Union's AI Act

The AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, was published in the Official Journal on 12 July 2024 and entered into force on 1 August 2024 [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. As a regulation, it binds all 27 member states directly without national implementing laws. Its definition of an "AI system" in Article 3(1), a machine-based system that infers from its inputs how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions, follows the OECD's wording closely (Chapter 1) [@oecd2024principles].

The Act assigns duties by role. A *provider* develops an AI system or general-purpose model and places it on the market under its own name; a *deployer* uses a system in a professional capacity. Most obligations fall on providers. The territorial reach is wide. Under Article 2(1), the Act covers providers placing systems or models on the EU market "irrespective of whether those providers are established or located within the Union or in a third country", and also non-EU providers and deployers whenever a system's output "is used in the Union" [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. It excludes military, defence and national-security uses, AI developed "for the sole purpose of scientific research and development", purely personal use, and free and open-source systems that are not high-risk, prohibited or subject to the transparency duties.

Figure 13.1 shows the structure, four tiers of risk for AI systems plus a separate track for general-purpose models. The pyramid is the Commission's way of communicating the law, not its legal structure, and the tiers are not exclusive. A high-risk system that talks to people, for example, must meet both the high-risk and the transparency rules.

### Prohibited practices

At the top are the **prohibited practices** of Article 5, banned since 2 February 2025 [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. Article 5(1) lists eight:

- \(a) subliminal, purposefully manipulative or deceptive techniques that materially distort a person's behaviour and cause, or are reasonably likely to cause, significant harm;
- \(b) exploiting vulnerabilities due to age, disability or a specific social or economic situation, with the same effect;
- \(c) social scoring that leads to detrimental treatment in unrelated contexts, or treatment disproportionate to the behaviour scored;
- \(d) predicting the risk that a person will commit a crime based solely on profiling or personality traits;
- \(e) building or expanding facial-recognition databases by untargeted scraping of images from the internet or CCTV;
- \(f) inferring emotions in workplaces and education institutions, except for medical or safety reasons;
- \(g) biometric categorisation to infer race, political opinions, trade-union membership, religious or philosophical beliefs, sex life or sexual orientation;
- \(h) real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement.

The last ban has three exceptions: targeted searches for victims of abduction, trafficking or sexual exploitation and for missing persons; preventing a specific, substantial and imminent threat to life or a genuine terrorist threat; and locating suspects of serious offences listed in Annex II. Each use needs prior authorisation by a judicial or independent administrative authority, and no decision with adverse legal effect on a person may rest solely on the system's output [@europeanparliament2024regulation].

The face-database ban targets practices already fined under data-protection law, such as by the Dutch authority in the Clearview AI case (Section 13.6) [@edpb2024dutch]. The Digital Omnibus added two bans from 2 December 2026: AI systems that generate intimate imagery of an identifiable person without consent, and systems that generate child sexual abuse material. They catch providers when such generation is the intended purpose or a reasonably foreseeable, reproducible outcome of a system lacking adequate safeguards [@europeanparliament2026regulation].

### A timetable rewritten before it took effect

The Act applies in stages: bans first, then duties for general-purpose models, then the high-risk regime. The Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, changed that sequence before the high-risk rules applied. The Commission proposed it on 19 November 2025 as part of a wider drive to simplify EU digital rules. The European Parliament approved the agreed text on 16 June 2026 [@councileu2026proposal] and the Council on 29 June, and it was published on 24 July [@europeanparliament2026regulation]. Its recitals cite the "delayed availability of standards, common specifications, and alternative guidance and the delayed establishment of national competent authorities" [@councileu2026proposal]. The omnibus did more than move dates. Its other changes, in order of consequence, were these [@europeanparliament2026regulation]:

- **A new central enforcer.** The Commission's AI Office gained exclusive competence, with some exceptions, over AI systems built on a general-purpose model by the same provider or group, and over AI systems in the very large online platforms and search engines designated under the Digital Services Act. It can inspect them and impose periodic penalty payments of up to 5% of average daily turnover.
- **Relief for more firms.** A new category of "small mid-cap enterprises" gained the simplified technical documentation already open to small firms, and lower fine ceilings (Table 13.2).
- **Sandboxes.** Every member state must have at least one AI regulatory sandbox, a supervised testing environment, running by 2 August 2027.
- **Bias detection.** A new Article 4a lets providers and deployers, exceptionally, process sensitive personal data such as ethnicity to detect and correct bias, where synthetic or anonymised data would not do.
- **AI literacy.** The duty to ensure staff "AI literacy" became a duty to "take measures to support" it.

### High-risk systems: one hiring tool through the rules

The second tier, **high-risk** AI, is where most of the Act's compliance work lies. A system can be high-risk in two ways. Under Article 6(1), it is a safety component of, or itself, a product covered by the EU product-safety laws listed in Annex I (machinery, toys, medical devices, lifts and others) that already requires third-party checks. Under Article 6(2), it falls into one of eight areas listed in Annex III: biometrics; critical infrastructure; education; employment; access to essential services such as public benefits, credit scoring and life and health insurance; law enforcement; migration and border control; and the administration of justice and democratic processes [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. The duties are easiest to grasp by following one system through them.

Take a software firm that sells employers a tool which ranks job applicants' CVs. The first question is classification. Recruitment and the filtering of applications are listed in Annex III, so the tool is presumed high-risk. Article 6(3) lets a listed system escape if it performs only a narrow procedural or preparatory task and poses no significant risk, but never if it profiles individuals [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. A tool that scores applicants on their personal characteristics is very likely to count as profiling, so the escape is closed.

As provider, the firm must then meet Articles 9 to 15. It needs a risk-management process covering the tool's whole life (Article 9). Its training and test data must be "relevant, sufficiently representative, and to the best extent possible, free of errors and complete" for the purpose (Article 10(3)), which for a hiring tool means data that resemble the applicants it will rank. It must keep technical documentation and automatic logs, give employers instructions for use, and reach appropriate accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity. It must also design the tool so that a human reviewer can understand its limits and stay aware of "automation bias", the tendency to over-rely on outputs (Article 14) [@europeanparliament2024regulation].

Before sale, the firm must pass **conformity assessment**, EU product law's check that a product meets its requirements before it reaches the market. For employment systems, as for every Annex III area except biometrics, this is a self-assessment under internal control, with no independent *notified body* involved. The firm then affixes the **CE marking**, the conformity mark carried by regulated products sold in the EU, and registers the tool in an EU database. AI in Annex I products is instead checked within the existing sectoral procedure, for example by the notified bodies for medical devices [@europeanparliament2024regulation].

The employer, as deployer, has duties of its own. It must use the tool according to its instructions, assign trained staff to oversee it, keep the logs and inform workers' representatives and affected workers before using it at work (Chapter 8). A private employer need not carry out a **fundamental rights impact assessment**. That duty (Article 27) falls on public bodies, private entities providing public services, and deployers of credit-scoring and life- or health-insurance systems. A city council using the same tool would therefore have to identify, before first use, the groups likely to be affected, the risks to them, the oversight arrangements and the remedies [@europeanparliament2024regulation].

After deployment, the firm must monitor the tool and report any serious incident to the authorities, within 15 days of becoming aware of it and sooner in the gravest cases (Article 73). A serious incident includes an infringement of EU law protecting fundamental rights, so discriminatory rankings could qualify [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. Under the amended timetable all of this applies from 2 December 2027, and a breach can cost up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide turnover (Table 13.2).

The third tier concerns **transparency**. Under Article 50, people must be told when they are dealing with an AI system unless it is obvious. Providers of systems that generate synthetic audio, images, video or text must mark outputs in a machine-readable way so that they can be detected as artificial, and deployers of "deepfakes" must disclose that the content has been generated or manipulated [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. These duties applied from 2 August 2026, with a later date for marking by systems already on the market (Table 13.1); Chapter 9 covers the technology. Everything else falls into the fourth tier, **minimal risk**, with no new obligations.

### General-purpose AI models and the systemic-risk threshold

General-purpose AI (GPAI) models, such as the large language models of Chapter 3, are regulated before anyone knows which systems will be built on them. Since 2 August 2025, all GPAI providers must keep technical documentation for the AI Office and give downstream developers enough information "to have a good understanding of the capabilities and limitations" of the model. They must also put in place a policy to comply with EU copyright law, including rights holders' opt-outs, and publish "a sufficiently detailed summary about the content used for training" on a Commission template (Article 53). Models released under a free and open-source licence with their weights public are exempt from the first two duties but not from the copyright policy or the training summary, and the exemption does not apply to models with systemic risk [@europeanparliament2024regulation].

Which models count? Under Commission guidelines of 18 July 2025, a model is indicatively general-purpose if it was trained with more than 10^23^ floating-point operations and can generate language (text or audio), images or video from text. A company that fine-tunes someone else's model becomes the provider of a new model only if its modification uses more than a third of the original model's training compute. The Commission ties this indicative criterion to a "significant change" in generality, capabilities or systemic risk [@europeancommission2025gpaiguidelines; @europeancommission2026faq].

A GPAI model has **systemic risk**, a legal category meaning risks with large-scale effects across the Union, if it has "high impact capabilities". Under Article 51(2), this is presumed when the cumulative training compute exceeds 10^25^ floating-point operations (FLOP). The Commission may also designate a model, on its own initiative or after a "qualified alert" from its scientific panel. A provider must notify the Commission "without delay and in any event within two weeks" of crossing the threshold, and may argue that its model nonetheless poses no systemic risk (Article 52) [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. (Chapter 11 uses "systemic risks" more broadly, for harms from widespread deployment.)

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: What does 10^25^ FLOP mean?**

A floating-point operation is a single arithmetic step, such as multiplying two decimal numbers. With the rule of thumb from Section 4.1 (training compute ≈ 6 × parameters × training tokens), a model with 100 billion parameters trained on 15 trillion tokens uses 6 × 10^11^ × 1.5 × 10^13^ = 9 × 10^24^ FLOP, just under the EU threshold. Train the same model on 20 trillion tokens and it reaches 1.2 × 10^25^ FLOP and is presumed to carry systemic risk. California and New York set their thresholds ten times higher, at 10^26^; the indicative floor for a general-purpose model, 10^23^, is a hundred times lower. Because the thresholds are fixed while frontier training compute has grown about five-fold a year since 2020 [@epochai2026trends], more models cross them every year. Epoch AI counted 33 publicly disclosed models trained with at least 10^25^ FLOP by June 2025, from 12 developers, with about two more announced each month during 2024 [@rahman2025over].
:::

Providers of systemic-risk models must also perform model evaluations "in accordance with standardised protocols and tools reflecting the state of the art", including adversarial testing. They must assess and mitigate systemic risks at Union level, track and report serious incidents to the AI Office "without undue delay", and ensure "an adequate level of cybersecurity protection" for the model and its infrastructure (Article 55) [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. Models already on the market before August 2025 have until 2 August 2027 to comply.

On 10 July 2025 the Commission published a **General-Purpose AI Code of Practice** with three chapters: transparency, copyright, and safety and security, the last applying only to systemic-risk models. Signing is voluntary, but under Article 53(4) providers may rely on it to demonstrate compliance until harmonised standards exist [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. When the Commission published its list of signatories on 1 August 2025, it included Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, Mistral AI and OpenAI among others. xAI signed only the safety and security chapter. Meta declined to sign, leaving it to show compliance by other means [@europeancommission2025generalpurpose; @haeck2025commission].

### Compute thresholds and their critics

The systemic-risk presumption is one example of a **compute threshold**, a legal trigger based on the computation used in training. Regulators use it to decide which models deserve extra scrutiny before anyone knows what they can do. The EU uses 10^25^ FLOP; California and New York use 10^26^, as did Biden's revoked safety order (Table 13.3).

The case for thresholds is practical. Compute is easier to observe, count and restrict than data or algorithms, as a preprint by Sastry and colleagues argues (Section 7.7) [@sastry2024computing]. In another preprint, Heim and Koessler argue that training compute is the best available metric for *identifying* models that warrant oversight, because it correlates with capabilities, can be measured before deployment and can be checked by outsiders [@heim2024training]. They stress that it is an imperfect proxy for risk, fit only as an initial filter that triggers evaluations. Hooker, in a third preprint, questions even that role, arguing that the relationship between compute and risk is "highly uncertain and rapidly changing" [@hooker2024limitations]. Smaller models trained with better data and methods can match larger ones, so a fixed threshold may catch harmless models and miss dangerous ones.

The first half of that worry is no longer hypothetical. With dozens of models above 10^25^ FLOP by mid-2025, including openly released ones, the EU presumption now catches many models that are not at the frontier [@rahman2025over]. The Commission can amend the threshold by delegated act (Article 51(3)), but the omnibus left it unchanged [@europeanparliament2024regulation; @europeanparliament2026regulation]. Lawmakers have hedged in other ways. The EU can designate models on other grounds, and California's SB 53 counts fine-tuning and reinforcement learning towards its threshold. The US government's 2026 cyber framework abandoned compute altogether, defining "covered frontier models" by results on a classified capability benchmark [@trump2026promoting].

### Enforcement and penalties

Enforcement is split. Each member state designates market-surveillance authorities for AI systems on its territory, while the Commission's AI Office supervises general-purpose models directly and, since the omnibus, many systems built on them [@europeanparliament2024regulation; @europeanparliament2026regulation]. An AI Board of member-state representatives coordinates, and a scientific panel of independent experts can issue alerts about systemic-risk models. Because one general-purpose model may sit inside thousands of products, problems found nationally may have to be traced to a provider supervised in Brussels.

National capacity has lagged. Member states were due to designate their authorities by 2 August 2025, but by June 2026 only nine of the 27 had clearly designated both their market-surveillance and their notifying authorities, according to a tracker run by the Future of Life Institute [@fli2026national]. Central enforcement started later. The penalty provisions for member states have applied since 2 August 2025, and the Commission's power to fine GPAI providers since 2 August 2026. In October 2026 Henna Virkkunen, the Commission's executive vice-president for tech sovereignty, told a conference that the AI Office had begun enforcing these rules, according to Euronews [@bertuzzi2026eus]. It had sent requests for information to OpenAI, whose agent had escaped a test environment and broken into systems at Hugging Face, and to Anthropic, whose model had gained unauthorised access to systems at three outside organisations (Chapter 12). Some lawyers argue that the Act's research exemption puts such testing beyond its reach. Table 13.2 sets out the fines available.

## 13.3 The United States: executive orders, states and pre-emption

As of October 2026 the United States had no comprehensive federal AI statute. The narrower TAKE IT DOWN Act of 2025, which criminalises publishing intimate deepfakes and obliges platforms to remove them, is discussed in Chapter 9. Federal policy has otherwise been set by executive orders, which bind agencies but can be revoked by the next president.

The orders have moved in one direction, away from mandatory reporting and towards promotion, procurement leverage and voluntary access. President Biden's EO 14110, "Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Development and Use of Artificial Intelligence", of 30 October 2023, required developers of the largest models to report to the government on training and safety testing. Its trigger was a model trained with "greater than 10^26^ integer or floating-point operations" [@biden2023safe]. On 20 January 2025 President Trump revoked it [@trump2025initial]. Three days later his EO 14179, "Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence", declared it US policy "to sustain and enhance America's global AI dominance" and ordered a review of actions taken under the revoked order [@trump2025removing]. The resulting *America's AI Action Plan* (July 2025) organised policy around innovation, infrastructure, and international diplomacy and security. It proposed that federal funding should not flow to states with "burdensome" AI rules [@whitehouse2025winning].

The same month the administration used the government's purchasing power in place of regulation. EO 14319, titled "Preventing Woke AI in the Federal Government", directed agencies to procure only large language models that follow two "Unbiased AI Principles", "truth-seeking" and "ideological neutrality". The second means that developers "shall not intentionally encode partisan or ideological judgments" into outputs unless the user prompts for them or can readily access them. Vendors were to show compliance under guidance from the Office of Management and Budget, by disclosing system prompts, specifications or evaluations rather than model weights where practicable [@trump2025preventing].

Federal action in 2026 continued to favour voluntary arrangements. EO 14409 of 2 June 2026, "Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security", directed the Treasury, the National Security Agency (NSA) and the cybersecurity agency CISA to develop a classified benchmark of models' cyber capabilities, with the NSA's director deciding which models count as "covered frontier models". They were also to design a *voluntary* framework giving the government access to such models for up to 30 days before release to other trusted partners. The order stated that nothing in it authorised "a mandatory governmental licensing, preclearance, or permitting requirement" [@trump2026promoting]. The most recent order, EO 14434 of 29 September 2026, "Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence", changed the government's vocabulary rather than anyone's obligations (Section 12.7) [@trump2026inaugurating].

### The pre-emption fight

**Pre-emption** is the doctrine under which valid federal law overrides conflicting state law. In July 2025 the Senate voted 99 to 1 to strip from a budget bill a provision that would have withheld some federal broadband and AI-infrastructure funds from states that enforced their own AI laws during a ten-year moratorium [@kelley2025senate]. On 11 December 2025 EO 14365, "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence", took another route [@trump2025ensuring]. It ordered the Attorney General to create an **AI Litigation Task Force** to challenge state AI laws, asked the Commerce Department to list "onerous" state laws within 90 days, and conditioned some federal broadband funds on states not enforcing them. Because an executive order cannot itself override state statutes, it called for legislation, with child-safety rules left untouched.

The Attorney General established the task force by memorandum on 9 January 2026. Its "sole responsibility" is to challenge state AI laws as unconstitutional burdens on interstate commerce, as pre-empted by federal regulations, or as "otherwise unlawful" [@usdoj2026artificial]. The legislative route followed with the FRONTIER Act (H.R. 9925), introduced on 23 July 2026 by Representative Jay Obernolte. It would bar states from imposing new substantive obligations on AI developers in three "covered subject areas": frontier-risk transparency, third-party auditing and independent verification, and incident reporting. Generally applicable laws, state regulation of deployers and users, and rules protecting minors would survive. As of 25 October 2026 the bill had been referred to committee and had passed neither chamber [@uscongress2026frontier].

### The states

With little federal legislation, the states have written most binding US AI rules. According to the 2026 AI Index, AI-related bills enacted by US states rose from fewer than 10 in 2020 to 150 in 2025 [@sajadieh2026ai]. California's SB 243 on companion chatbots is discussed in Chapter 10. Three kinds of law matter most for developers.

Frontier-model transparency laws come first. California's Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act (SB 53) was signed on 29 September 2025 and took effect on 1 January 2026 [@californiagovernor2025governor]. It defines a "frontier model" as one trained with more than 10^26^ operations, counting fine-tuning and reinforcement learning on top of the original run. It targets "catastrophic risk", a foreseeable risk that a model materially contributes to the death or serious injury of more than 50 people, or more than $1 billion in damage, in a single incident. All frontier developers must publish a transparency report when they deploy a new frontier model and report critical safety incidents to the state within 15 days, or within 24 hours if there is an imminent risk of death or serious injury. "Large" developers, with annual revenue above $500 million, must also publish a framework for managing catastrophic risk. Only the Attorney General can seek the civil penalties, and the law protects whistleblowers [@californialegislature2025sb53; @metr2026frontier].

New York's RAISE Act, signed on 19 December 2025 and amended by a law signed on 27 March 2026, takes effect on 1 January 2027 [@nyassembly2026a9449]. As amended, it follows SB 53's structure but is stricter. Incidents must be reported within 72 hours, a new office within the Department of Financial Services supervises developers and funds itself through assessments on them, and penalties are higher. California has since begun to build the independent checks these laws lack. In September 2026 it enacted SB 813, a state framework for independent organisations that verify AI systems' compliance with state law, and AB 1405, a registry of AI auditors [@californiagovernor2026governor]. A second kind bans specific harmful uses. Texas's Responsible Artificial Intelligence Governance Act (HB 149), signed on 22 June 2025 and in force since 1 January 2026, prohibits developing or deploying AI that intentionally aims to incite self-harm, harm to others or crime. It also bans AI developed with the intent to discriminate unlawfully against a protected class, and social scoring by government. A disparate impact "is not sufficient by itself to demonstrate an intent to discriminate". Only the Attorney General can enforce the Act, after a 60-day period to cure, and it creates a 36-month regulatory sandbox [@texaslegislature2025hb149].

Laws on consequential decisions have proved the most contested.

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: Colorado's AI Act, from statute to stand-off**

In 2024 Colorado passed SB 24-205, the first broad US state law on "high-risk" AI systems used in consequential decisions such as hiring, lending and housing. It required both developers and deployers to "use reasonable care to protect consumers from any known or reasonably foreseeable risks of algorithmic discrimination", and deployers to carry out impact assessments. After a delay, it was due to take effect on 30 June 2026 [@usdoj2026complaint]. The pre-emption order singled it out, arguing that the law "may even force AI models to produce false results" [@trump2025ensuring].

On 9 April 2026 xAI sued Colorado's Attorney General in federal court [@usdccolorado2026docket]. On 24 April the Department of Justice filed to join as a plaintiff, arguing mainly that the law violated the constitutional guarantee of equal protection [@usdoj2026complaint]. Three days later a federal magistrate judge approved the parties' agreement that the state would not begin enforcing SB 24-205, "or any legislation replacing or amending" it, until 14 days after the court rules on a future request for a preliminary injunction [@usdccolorado2026minute].

On 14 May 2026 the Governor signed SB 26-189, which repealed and replaced the 2024 law [@coloradogeneralassembly2026automated]. The new law regulates "automated decision-making technology" through disclosure: notices to consumers, an explanation within 30 days after an adverse decision, and rights to correct data and to request human review. Violations count as deceptive trade practices, the consumer-protection offence the Attorney General already enforces, and there is no private right of action. Most duties apply from 1 January 2027. As of October 2026 the federal case remained open [@usdccolorado2026docket].

## 13.4 China, the UK and other approaches

China has built its AI rules in layers, through targeted measures issued by government agencies led by the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC), rather than one horizontal law. The first layer, the Provisions on the Administration of Algorithmic Recommendations in Internet Information Services, has applied since 1 March 2022 [@cac2021provisions]. They require providers to offer users an option not targeted at their personal characteristics, or a convenient way to switch recommendation off (Article 17). Under Article 24, providers whose services have "public opinion attributes or social mobilisation capacity" must register details of the service and its algorithm, with a self-assessment report, in an online **algorithm filing** system. Later rules reuse this registry, so regulators know who operates which algorithm before anything goes wrong. Rules on "deep synthesis" (deepfakes and other synthetic media) followed from 10 January 2023 [@cac2022provisions].

The **Interim Measures for the Administration of Generative Artificial Intelligence Services**, issued by seven agencies, took effect on 15 August 2023 [@cac2023interim]. Providers must uphold "socialist core values", prevent prohibited content, use lawfully sourced training data, protect personal information and label generated images and video. Services with public-opinion attributes or mobilisation capacity must undergo a security assessment and file their algorithms. In effect, a public chatbot with such attributes needs both a registration and a security review before launch, a form of pre-market control that no Western jurisdiction applies to general-purpose models. Enforcement also runs through campaigns, such as the three-month "Qinglang" campaign the CAC launched in April 2025 against unregistered generative AI products, unlabelled synthetic content, "one-click undressing" apps and AI-generated rumours [@cac2025qinglang].

Rules on specific issues followed. The **Measures for Labelling AI-Generated and Synthetic Content**, in force from 1 September 2025, require labels that users can see or hear and implicit labels in file metadata naming the provider [@cac2025measures]. In October 2025 the national legislature amended the Cybersecurity Law to add an article on the "safe and sound development of AI", in force from 1 January 2026, according to the official China Daily [@xinhua2025china]. In April 2026 five agencies issued interim measures on "anthropomorphic" AI services that simulate human personalities for companionship or emotional support, according to the state news agency Xinhua. Effective from 15 July 2026, they require security assessments, algorithm filing and protections for minors and older users [@xinhua2026five]. On 8 May 2026 three agencies issued China's first policy document devoted to **AI agents**, defined as systems capable of "autonomous perception, memory, decision-making, interaction, and execution" [@govcn2026china]. Internationally, China's **Global AI Governance Action Plan** of July 2025 frames AI as "an international public good" and calls for open-source sharing and capacity-building for the Global South [@mfa2025global].

The **United Kingdom** has so far kept to the sectoral approach of its 2023 white paper [@dsit2023proinnovation]. Since 2025 its policy has emphasised adoption and infrastructure. The government accepted 47 of the 50 recommendations in the *AI Opportunities Action Plan* by the entrepreneur Matt Clifford (January 2025), including "AI Growth Zones" with easier access to power and planning [@dsit2025ai; @dsit2025opportunities]. The AI Safety Institute became the AI Security Institute in February 2025, refocused on cyber-attacks, chemical and biological misuse and crime. The government said it would "not focus on bias or freedom of speech" [@dsit2025tackling]. Labour's 2024 manifesto had pledged binding rules for the most powerful models, yet the King's Speech of 13 May 2026 contained no AI bill, as the privacy professionals' association IAPP reported [@iapp2026kings]. The UK's most consequential AI decision of 2026 concerned copyright (Section 13.6).

Two other Asian democracies have passed horizontal AI statutes, and they show the range of choices beyond the EU model. South Korea's Framework Act on Artificial Intelligence, often called the AI Basic Act, took effect on 22 January 2026. Like the EU Act it is horizontal, but much lighter. According to a summary by the law firm Cooley, it places duties on "high-impact" AI, which significantly affects life, safety or fundamental rights in areas such as health care, energy, hiring and biometric analysis [@cooley2026south]. Providers of such systems must explain outcomes, keep human oversight and protect users, and generative-AI content must be labelled. Large foreign providers must appoint a local representative. Enforcement is gentle. Fines reach 30 million won (about $21,000), and the science ministry has indicated a grace period of a year before imposing them. Japan went further towards soft law. Its Act on Promotion of Research and Development, and Utilization of AI-related Technology (Act No. 53 of 2025), promulgated on 4 June 2025, sets up an AI Strategy Headquarters under the Prime Minister and a national AI Basic Plan [@japan2025act]. Its only duty on businesses is to "endeavour to cooperate" with government measures, and it contains no penalties. The government relies instead on information-gathering, guidance and, according to Japanese press reports, the prospect of publicly naming firms whose use of AI seriously infringes rights [@paulger2025understanding].

## 13.5 International coordination

AI models cross borders easily, which argues for international rules. Yet states disagree about values, compete for economic and military advantage, and share few institutions with authority over AI. Figure 13.2 places the main international milestones alongside the national ones.

### Summits

A series of summits began at Bletchley Park on 1–2 November 2023, where 28 countries and the EU, including the US and China, signed the **Bletchley Declaration**. It recognised the potential for "serious, even catastrophic, harm" from the most capable frontier models [@ukgov2023bletchley]. At the follow-up summit in Seoul in May 2024, 16 companies (later 20) signed the **Frontier AI Safety Commitments**. They promised to publish safety frameworks with thresholds of intolerable risk and, in the extreme, not to develop or deploy a model whose risks could not be kept below them [@dsit2024frontier]. Chapter 12 examines the frameworks that resulted.

The Paris summit of 10–11 February 2025 was called the AI Action Summit and shifted emphasis towards inclusion, sustainability and economic opportunity [@elysee2025statement]. Its closing statement was signed by 63 countries and the EU, but not by the United States or the United Kingdom. India's AI Impact Summit in New Delhi in February 2026, organised around "people, planet and progress", was described by its host as the first global AI summit in the Global South [@pib2026india]. The titles, from "safety" to "action" to "impact", track a shift in political priorities.

None of the summits' outcomes is legally binding or monitored, and their effects have been indirect. The Seoul promise to publish safety frameworks reappears as a legal duty in California's SB 53. In the EU it reappears in the voluntary Code of Practice, which signatories use to show that they meet the binding risk-management obligations of Article 55. The summits are also exposed to national politics, as the US and UK signatures at Bletchley and their absence from Paris show.

### Treaties, the United Nations and the OECD

The only binding international treaty specifically on AI is the Council of Europe's **Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law** (CETS No. 225). As a **framework convention**, it sets general obligations and leaves details to national law. It was opened for signature on 5 September 2024, when the UK, the United States and the EU were among the first to sign [@moj2024uk; @councilofeurope2024framework]. It enters into force about three months after five signatories, including at least three Council of Europe member states, have ratified it. As of 22 October 2026 the convention had 21 signatories, including Canada, Japan and Ukraine, but only one ratification, by the EU on 15 May 2026, so it was not in force [@councilofeurope2024framework].

At the United Nations, General Assembly Resolution 78/265 of 21 March 2024 called for "safe, secure and trustworthy" AI [@un2024seizing]. Resolution 79/325 of 26 August 2025 established two bodies limited to the non-military domain [@un2025terms]. One is an **Independent International Scientific Panel on AI** of 40 experts to produce evidence-based assessments; the other is a **Global Dialogue on AI Governance**, meeting in Geneva in 2026 and New York in 2027. The first Geneva session took place on 6–7 July 2026 with delegations from 163 countries, and the Panel presented a preliminary report to it [@un2026global]. The model is loosely the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, except that AI capabilities change far faster than the climate and much of the evidence is held by companies. Military AI, excluded from both bodies and from the EU AI Act, runs on a separate and slower track. In December 2024 the General Assembly voted 166 to 3 for a resolution on lethal autonomous weapons systems that opened consultations but not treaty negotiations [@un2024lethal; @hrw2024killer].

The **OECD AI Principles**, adopted in 2019 and updated in May 2024, had 47 adherents in 2026 [@oecd2024principles]. The G7's **Hiroshima AI Process** produced Guiding Principles and a Code of Conduct for developers of advanced AI systems, endorsed on 30 October 2023 [@mic2023hiroshima]. Since February 2025 the OECD has run voluntary reporting against the Code; its first report drew on submissions from 20 organisations [@oecd2025hiroshima].

Finally, **technical standards** turn principles into checklists that auditors can verify. ISO/IEC 42001, published in 2023, specifies requirements for an AI management system, meaning the policies, roles and processes an organisation uses to develop or use AI responsibly [@iso2023information]. Organisations can be certified against it, as against quality-management standards. Under the EU AI Act, compliance with **harmonised standards**, European standards requested by the Commission, gives a legal presumption of conformity [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. The Commission first requested such standards in May 2023, and the European standards bodies were still accelerating the most delayed drafts in October 2025, aiming for Q4 2026 [@europeancommission2025standardisation; @cencenelec2025update]. Their late arrival helped cause the omnibus delays, and the omnibus now requires the Commission to request further standards "without undue delay" [@europeanparliament2026regulation].

## 13.6 Copyright, training data and personal data

Large models are trained on vast collections of text, images and music, much of it protected by copyright or containing personal data. The **input question** asks whether copying works to train a model infringes copyright. The **output question** asks whether a model, or what it generates, reproduces protected works. By October 2026 courts in the US, the UK and Germany had given partial and partly conflicting answers, summarised in Table 13.4.

### The United States: fair use

In the United States the central defence is **fair use**, which allows some unlicensed use of copyrighted works. Courts weigh four factors: the purpose and character of the use, including whether it is "transformative" (serves a new purpose); the nature of the work; the amount used; and the effect on the market for the original [@lii2026section107].

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: Bartz v. Anthropic**

Authors sued Anthropic for training its Claude models on their books without permission. According to the court record, Anthropic had downloaded "over seven million copies of books" from the shadow libraries Library Genesis and the Pirate Library Mirror, knowing they were pirated. Later it spent "many millions of dollars" buying print books, which were stripped from their bindings, scanned and discarded [@usdcnortherncal2025bartz].

On 23 June 2025 Judge William Alsup ruled that training the models was "exceedingly transformative and was a fair use". He rejected the argument that models would flood the market with competing works, comparing it to complaining that "training schoolchildren to write well" would produce competing works. Scanning purchased print books was also fair use. Building a permanent library from pirated copies was not excused, and that claim was set for a class-action trial, a trial on behalf of all the affected authors [@usdcnortherncal2025bartz].

The parties settled for $1.5 billion, which Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín, who had taken over the case, approved on 20 July 2026 [@bloomberglaw2026anthropic; @usdcnortherncal2026bartz]. The settlement covers more than 480,000 works at about $3,000 per work before costs and fees, and requires Anthropic to destroy the pirated datasets [@usdcnortherncal2026bartz]. In early September 2026 the settlement administrator sent claimants notices of their claim status, with first payments expected by mid-November [@anthropicsettlement2026; @authorsguild2026important]. A settlement sets no precedent, but the case showed that how training data is acquired can matter as much as how it is used.
:::

Two days after the Bartz ruling, Judge Vince Chhabria granted summary judgment to Meta in *Kadrey v. Meta*, brought by thirteen authors over books Meta had downloaded from shadow libraries [@usdcnortherncal2025kadrey]. (Summary judgment decides a case without a full trial when the key facts are not in dispute.) Even prompts designed to make the models regurgitate training data produced no more than 50 words and punctuation marks of the plaintiffs' books. Chhabria ruled for Meta only because the authors had offered no evidence on the harm he considered decisive, "market dilution" by a flood of competing generated works. He warned that unlicensed training would, "in most cases", likely be illegal, and the ruling bound only these thirteen authors. A separate claim over Meta's downloading and sharing of books by torrent was still pending in 2026 [@usdcnortherncal2026kadrey].

On 29 September 2026 the Third Circuit Court of Appeals decided *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence* on an interlocutory appeal, one heard before the trial court's final judgment [@thirdcircuit2026thomson]. Ross, a legal-research start-up, had used 2,243 Westlaw headnotes (editorial summaries of points of law), obtained through a contractor, to train an AI search tool marketed as a Westlaw competitor. The court held that the headnotes were copyrightable and that Ross's use was "minimally transformative, at best", since it served the same purpose as the original. It also found that "the market for licensing headnotes as text to train AI is rapidly developing" and that Ross had usurped Thomson Reuters's opportunity to enter it. The court stressed, however, that "ROSS's AI was not a generative AI". It noted that the Department of Justice had filed a statement of interest, a filing in which the government gives its view in a case it is not party to, in the consolidated New York cases against OpenAI. That statement, of 1 September 2026, argued that training a large language model is transformative; the cases, which include the *New York Times* claims, were pending in October 2026.

The licensing market the court described was already forming in other media. Universal Music settled its suit against the music generator Udio in October 2025 in exchange for licences and a new licensed service [@dalugdug2025universal], and Warner Music settled with Suno a month later [@carman2025warner]. Each such deal makes it easier for rights holders to argue that unlicensed training harms a real market.

On outputs, US law requires a human author. In *Thaler v. Perlmutter* the Court of Appeals for the DC Circuit held on 18 March 2025 that the Copyright Act "requires all work to be authored in the first instance by a human being". It upheld the refusal to register an image whose sole listed author was an AI system [@uscadc2025thaler], and the Supreme Court declined to hear an appeal on 2 March 2026 [@ussupremecourt2026thaler]. The US Copyright Office's report on generative-AI training, released in pre-publication form on 9 May 2025, had not been finalised by October 2026 [@uscopyrightoffice2025copyright]. The day after its release, the White House dismissed the Register of Copyrights, Shira Perlmutter, as CBS News reported [@macfarlane2025trump].

### Europe: the model as a copy

In the UK, Getty Images sued Stability AI over the image generator Stable Diffusion [@highcourt2025getty]. Getty dropped its training claim for lack of evidence that training took place in the UK. On 4 November 2025 the High Court rejected the remaining copyright claim. Getty had argued that importing the model was secondary infringement, that is, dealing in an "infringing copy" made by someone else. Although an "article" under UK copyright law can be intangible, Mrs Justice Joanna Smith held that "the model weights are not themselves an infringing copy and they do not store an infringing copy". Stable Diffusion, she found, "does not store or reproduce any Copyright Works and nor has it ever done so". Trade-mark infringement, where older versions' outputs showed Getty and iStock watermarks, was "historic and extremely limited in scope". According to the IPKat blog, she later gave Getty permission to appeal on the "infringing copy" point, a question of law "on which the minds of reasonable lawyers may differ" [@fairhurst2026permission]. No appeal decision had been reported by October 2026.

A German court, on different facts, reached the opposite conclusion about what a model contains. On 11 November 2025 the Munich Regional Court I largely upheld claims by GEMA, the German music collecting society, against two OpenAI companies over song lyrics by nine well-known German songwriters [@lgmuenchen2025gema]. According to the court's press release, the lyrics were "reproducibly contained" in the GPT-4 and GPT-4o models. This is **memorisation**. Comparison with ChatGPT's outputs showed that the parameters had absorbed the texts in full, and their length ruled out coincidence. That fixation was a reproduction under EU law, even though it existed only as probability values. The EU **text and data mining exception**, as implemented in Germany, covered the copies made to assemble a training corpus but not copies retained in the model, because it assumes that only information, not the work itself, is extracted. The outputs also infringed, and OpenAI, not its users, was responsible, since simple prompts had produced them. On 31 July 2026 the same court ruled largely for GEMA against Suno over six songs, finding them memorised in two versions of its model and reproduced in outputs [@lgmuenchen2026suno]. Neither judgment was final.

A Hamburg case marks the other side of the same line. In *Kneschke v. LAION* a photographer sued a non-profit association that had downloaded his photograph to build a public dataset of image–text pairs for training AI models. The Hamburg Regional Court held in September 2024 that the copying was covered by the exception for text and data mining in scientific research [@lausen2024lg]. In December 2025 the Hamburg Higher Regional Court dismissed his appeal and added that the general exception applied too. The photo agency's opt-out, written in plain language in its terms of use, was not "machine-readable" as the law requires. The court allowed a further appeal to the Federal Court of Justice [@filikhina2025robert]. The case concerned only the assembly of the dataset, not training or outputs.

Read together, the European rulings differ less than they seem. Hamburg allowed the copies made to build a training set, and Munich agreed that such copies are covered. London found no evidence that Stable Diffusion stored Getty's images, and Munich found that GPT-4 and Suno's models reproduced specific works. The two courts that examined models both treated what the model itself retains as decisive, turning a legal question into a partly technical one about what a given model has memorised.

Governments have hesitated to legislate. The UK's Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, which received Royal Assent on 19 June 2025, required the government to publish an economic impact assessment and a report on the use of copyright works in developing AI within nine months [@ukparliament2025data]. The March 2026 report abandoned the government's earlier preferred option, a broad exception allowing training unless rights holders opted out, after most consultation respondents rejected it. ### Personal data

Text and images collected from the web contain **personal data**, information about an identifiable person. In the EU the GDPR requires a legal basis, such as consent or a "legitimate interest", for each use, and national data-protection authorities enforced it against AI years before the AI Act applied. On 30 March 2023 the Italian authority, the Garante, ordered OpenAI to stop processing Italian users' data through ChatGPT. It cited no legal basis for the "massive collection and processing of personal data" used in training, missing information to users and no age checks [@garante2023intelligenza]. In December 2024 it fined OpenAI €15 million [@garante2024chatgpt]; in March 2026 the Tribunal of Rome upheld OpenAI's appeal against that decision [@garante2026tribunale]. According to legal commentary on the judgment, the court held that the Irish authority had become competent once OpenAI established itself in Ireland in February 2024, and it did not rule on the substance of the complaints [@papini2026openai].

The European Data Protection Board, which brings together the EU's national authorities, set up a ChatGPT taskforce. Its May 2024 report observed that because of "the probabilistic nature of the system" the training approach "may also produce biased or made up outputs", and that the accuracy principle must nonetheless be complied with. Separately, it held that a provider cannot shift responsibility for compliance onto users, for example by a clause in its terms and conditions making them responsible for the personal data they type into the chat [@edpb2024report]. Under the Board's Opinion 28/2024 of December 2024, a model counts as anonymous only if it is "very unlikely" that people in the training data can be identified or their data extracted by queries [@edpb2024opinion]. Legitimate interest can justify training if the processing is strictly necessary and passes a balancing test. And a model developed with unlawfully processed data may be unlawful to deploy "unless the model has been duly anonymised". Draft guidelines on web scraping for generative AI (July 2026) add practical expectations: precise collection criteria, filters for sensitive categories of data, and the exclusion of websites "which clearly oppose the scraping" [@edpb2026guidelines].

In May 2024 the Dutch authority fined Clearview AI €30.5 million for building a facial-recognition database by processing biometric data without a legal basis and without adequately informing the people concerned [@edpb2024dutch]. Large platforms have more often negotiated changes. In June 2024 Meta paused plans to train its models on public posts by adults in the EU after the Irish Data Protection Commission raised concerns. It started such training from 27 May 2025, after changing its notices and making it easier to object [@dpc2025statement].

## 13.7 Liability and the cost of regulation

### Liability

When an AI system gives dangerous advice or makes a discriminatory decision, the developer, the deploying company or the user may be responsible. Two kinds of rule answer different questions. Regulatory penalties, such as the fines in Table 13.2, are imposed by a public authority for breaching a statute and are paid to the state. Liability rules decide whether a person who has been harmed can claim compensation, and from whom.

In the EU the answer rests on product liability. The revised Product Liability Directive, Directive (EU) 2024/2853, entered into force on 8 December 2024. Member states must transpose it, that is, write it into national law, by 9 December 2026, and it applies to products placed on the market from that date; earlier products stay under the 1985 directive [@europeancommission2024liability]. Germany's Bundestag adopted its implementing law on 8 October 2026 [@bundestag2026produkthaftung]. The directive covers "all types of software", including AI systems, and liability does not depend on fault. Manufacturers can be liable for defects that arise after release through updates or machine learning. Compensable damage includes psychological harm and the destruction or corruption of data not used for professional purposes [@europeancommission2024liability; @eu2024directive]. Courts can order defendants to disclose evidence and can ease the claimant's burden of proof where needed, which matters when the defect lies inside a model that the claimant cannot inspect. A second instrument, the AI Liability Directive proposed in September 2022 to ease fault-based claims, was withdrawn by the Commission on 6 October 2025 [@europeanparliament2025procedure]. EU victims of AI harm therefore rely on the product regime plus national tort law.

The US has no federal AI liability statute, and the state laws in Section 13.3 create penalties, not compensation. People harmed by AI have three channels instead: private suits under ordinary tort law, consumer-protection suits by state attorneys general, and, where courts accept that an AI app is a product, product liability. The most prominent private cases concern chatbots and minors (Chapter 10). In *Garcia v. Character Technologies*, brought in federal court in Florida after a teenager's death, the defendants argued that the chatbot's output was speech protected by the First Amendment and that the app was not a product. In May 2025 Judge Anne Conway let most of the claims proceed. She was "not prepared to hold" at that stage that the output was speech, and treated the app as a product so far as the claims concerned its design "rather than ideas or expressions within the app" [@usdcmdfla2025garcia]. In January 2026 the parties told the court they had reached a settlement in principle, on undisclosed terms, according to the news site Decrypt [@vismaya2026google]. Similar suits against OpenAI followed in California state courts. One, filed in August 2025, alleges wrongful death, defective design, failure to warn and negligence [@hendrix2025breaking]; seven more, filed in November 2025, make similar claims about the GPT-4o model, according to the plaintiffs' lawyers [@smvlc2025seven].

The attorneys general's channel is growing fastest. Florida sued OpenAI and its chief executive in June 2026 for product liability, negligence and deceptive practices, as Bloomberg Law reported [@mekelburg2026florida]. In October at least eight more states sued OpenAI, alleging that ChatGPT harmed children and deceived adults, according to the Associated Press [@ap2026states].

Whether the defence that has protected online platforms for decades applies to such cases is an open question. Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act shields a service from being treated as the publisher of "information provided by another information content provider". But anyone responsible "in whole or in part" for creating the information is itself a content provider [@lii2026section230]. On the statute's wording, text generated by a company's own model may not count as information provided by someone else. The *Garcia* defendants did not invoke Section 230, but OpenAI listed it among its defences in its November 2025 answer in the Raine case, NBC News reported [@yang2025openai].

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Does AI regulation hold back innovation?**

Mario Draghi's 2024 report on European competitiveness for the European Commission counted "around 100 tech-focused laws and over 270 regulators active in digital networks" in the EU. It cited the AI Act's compute-threshold duties for general-purpose models as an example of precaution, and concluded that the overall regulatory burden leaves only larger companies, "which are often non-EU based", able to bear the costs of compliance [@draghi2024future]. The best evidence comes from the GDPR, an earlier EU digital law that applied from May 2018. Afterwards EU technology ventures raised less money, in fewer and smaller deals, than comparable US ventures, according to a peer-reviewed study [@jia2021short]. A working paper by Janßen and colleagues found that the GDPR induced the exit of "about a third of available apps", while new app entry fell by half [@janssen2022gdpr]. In a 2024 working paper, since published in the *Journal of Political Economy*, Demirer and colleagues found that EU firms cut data storage by 25% relative to comparable US firms, consistent with a 21% rise in the cost of data [@demirer2024data].

The case against starts from the size of those effects and their causes. The same Demirer study finds that the higher cost of data translates into "only a 0.1–0.6% rise in overall production costs", because data plays a small role in production compared with computation [@demirer2024data]. The legal scholar Anu Bradford attributes the US–EU gap in technology firms to fragmented capital markets, bankruptcy law, the incomplete single market and immigration policy more than to digital regulation, and calls the trade-off a "false choice" [@bradford2024false]. The AI investment gap is real. In 2025 private AI investment reached $285.9 billion in the United States against $5.9 billion in the United Kingdom, Europe's largest recipient, according to the 2026 AI Index [@sajadieh2026ai]. But the wider technology gap that Bradford documents is older than the Act, predating even the Commission's 2021 proposal, so the Act cannot be its main cause. Investors respond to expected rules as well as applied ones, however, so some effect of the Act since its adoption in 2024 cannot be ruled out.

Neither side has decisive evidence for AI. The GDPR studies concern a data-protection law and mostly short-run effects, and the AI Act's high-risk duties apply only from December 2027. A convincing test would compare investment, firm entry and model releases in the EU before and after 2027–2028 with similar markets lacking such rules, separating the Act's effects from those of energy prices and capital markets.
:::

In October 2026 almost no rule in force required a licence before a model's release. China's security assessments were the exception. The nearest US analogue was not law. In June 2026 OpenAI limited its unreleased GPT-5.6 Sol to a small group of partners approved by the US government, which the Forecasting Research Institute judged did "not appear to be the result of a binding directive" [@rosenberg2026how]. Elsewhere the law asks for disclosure, documentation and incident reports, triggered by training compute or by use in sensitive decisions, and leaves compensation to product liability in the EU and to tort and consumer-protection suits in the US. Timetables proved soft. Brussels deferred its high-risk regime, Colorado replaced its law while facing a federal lawsuit, and US federal policy rests on revocable orders. Meanwhile courts are settling questions the statutes left open, such as whether a model stores its training data and whether a chatbot is a product. Four developments will show whether the rules bite: whether the Commission imposes its first fine on a general-purpose model provider, whether the Annex III regime starts on 2 December 2027 as now scheduled, how the Court of Appeal rules on Getty's "infringing copy" point, and whether the New York courts accept the fair-use defence in the cases against OpenAI. Chapter 14 asks how well anyone can forecast developments of this kind.

- European Parliament and Council (2024). *Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (Artificial Intelligence Act)*. The primary text; Articles 2, 5, 6, 43, 50–55, 99 and Annex III, read with the 2026 amendments, cover most of what matters in practice. [@europeanparliament2024regulation]
- European Parliament and Council (2026). *Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (Digital Omnibus on AI)*. Shows how and why the Act's timetable, enforcement structure and small-firm relief were changed before its main rules applied. [@europeanparliament2026regulation]
