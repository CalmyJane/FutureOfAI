# Chapter 13: Governing AI: Law, Policy and International Coordination

The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act was written around a date. Its main rules for "high-risk" AI systems, such as software that screens job applicants or decides access to public benefits, were to apply from 2 August 2026 [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. When the date arrived, they did not. On 27 July 2026 an amending law, the Digital Omnibus on AI, had entered into force and moved the deadline to 2 December 2027 for most of these systems, and to 2 August 2028 for AI built into products such as medical devices or toys. The legislators gave a practical reason: the technical standards and the national authorities needed to apply the rules were not ready [@europeanparliament2026regulation; @councileu2026proposal].

In the same months the US state of Colorado retreated from its broad AI law. Elon Musk's company xAI had sued the state, and the US Department of Justice sought to join the case. Colorado agreed in court not to enforce the law, then replaced it with a narrower one before it took effect [@usdccolorado2026minute; @coloradogeneralassembly2026automated]. Every rule below is therefore dated, with its status as of October 2026.

## 13.1 The governance toolkit

**Governance** means the full set of rules, institutions and practices that shape how a technology is built and used. Beyond statutes, governments issue executive orders and guidance, companies sign voluntary commitments, standards bodies write technical specifications, and courts apply older law on copyright, negligence or discrimination to new cases.

**Hard law** is binding and enforceable, such as an EU regulation or a US state statute, and is usually backed by fines. **Soft law** covers declarations, principles and voluntary codes; it relies on reputation, peer pressure and the prospect of later legislation. **Technical standards** describe how to do something, for example how to run a risk-management process, and often become the practical route to complying with hard law. **Liability** rules decide who pays when harm occurs, so they act after the fact rather than before it.

A second distinction is between horizontal and sectoral regulation. A **horizontal** law applies across the whole economy to anything that meets its definition of AI; the EU AI Act is the leading example. A **sectoral** approach leaves AI to the existing regulators of finance, health, transport and so on. The UK's 2023 white paper chose the sectoral path. It set five principles (safety, transparency, fairness, accountability, and contestability and redress) and asked existing regulators to apply them, without a new AI statute or AI regulator [@dsit2023proinnovation]. The choice matters more for AI than for most technologies, because a general-purpose model built with one sector in mind can be repurposed for all of them after release.

::: {.box .concept}
**Concept: Risk-based regulation**

Risk-based regulation scales obligations to the harm a system could cause in a given use: what the system is used for and who could be hurt. A spam filter faces no new duties; a system that ranks job candidates must meet documentation, testing and human-oversight requirements; a few uses are banned outright. The approach concentrates effort where harm is likeliest. It depends on drawing the categories well, and general-purpose models that can serve any use fit it awkwardly, which is why the EU regulates them in a separate regime.
:::

Each instrument has typical weaknesses. Hard law is slow to change while frontier models change within months; soft law carries no penalty; standards take years and are drafted largely by industry experts; liability operates only after harm. Jurisdictions therefore mix instruments, and the mix reveals their priorities: the EU leads with a horizontal statute, the US federal government with executive orders and procurement, China with agency rules backed by registration, and the UK with existing regulators.

## 13.2 The European Union's AI Act

The AI Act is Regulation (EU) 2024/1689. It was published in the Official Journal on 12 July 2024, entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in stages [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. As a regulation rather than a directive, it binds all 27 member states directly without national implementing laws. Its definition of an "AI system" in Article 3(1), a machine-based system that infers from its inputs how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions, follows the OECD's wording closely (Chapter 1).

The Act assigns duties by role. A *provider* develops an AI system or general-purpose model and places it on the market under its own name; a *deployer* uses a system in a professional capacity. Most obligations fall on providers, but deployers of high-risk systems carry their own. The territorial reach is wide. Under Article 2(1), the Act covers providers placing systems or models on the EU market "irrespective of whether those providers are established or located within the Union or in a third country", and also non-EU providers and deployers whenever a system's output "is used in the Union" [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. It excludes military, defence and national-security uses, AI developed "for the sole purpose of scientific research and development", purely personal use, and free and open-source systems that are not high-risk, prohibited or subject to the transparency duties.

### Prohibited practices

At the top are the **prohibited practices** of Article 5, banned since 2 February 2025 [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. Article 5(1) lists eight:

- (a) subliminal, purposefully manipulative or deceptive techniques that materially distort a person's behaviour and cause, or are reasonably likely to cause, significant harm;
- (b) exploiting vulnerabilities due to age, disability or a specific social or economic situation, with the same effect;
- (c) social scoring that leads to detrimental treatment in unrelated contexts, or treatment disproportionate to the behaviour scored;
- (d) predicting the risk that a person will commit a crime based solely on profiling or personality traits;
- (e) building or expanding facial-recognition databases by untargeted scraping of images from the internet or CCTV;
- (f) inferring emotions in workplaces and education institutions, except for medical or safety reasons;
- (g) biometric categorisation to infer race, political opinions, trade-union membership, religious or philosophical beliefs, sex life or sexual orientation;
- (h) real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement.

The last ban carries three exceptions: targeted searches for victims of abduction, trafficking or sexual exploitation and for missing persons; preventing a specific, substantial and imminent threat to life or a genuine terrorist threat; and locating suspects of serious offences listed in Annex II that carry a maximum sentence of "at least four years". Each use needs prior authorisation by a judicial or independent administrative authority. In urgent cases police may start without it but must apply within 24 hours; if authorisation is refused, use must stop and the data be deleted. No decision with adverse legal effect on a person may rest solely on the system's output [@europeanparliament2024regulation].

The ban on building face databases targets practices that had already drawn fines under existing data-protection law, such as the Dutch fine on Clearview AI (Section 13.6). The Digital Omnibus added two bans from 2 December 2026: AI systems that generate intimate imagery of an identifiable person without consent, and systems that generate child sexual abuse material. They catch providers when such generation is the intended purpose or a reasonably foreseeable, reproducible outcome of a system lacking adequate safeguards [@europeanparliament2026regulation].

### A timetable rewritten before it took effect

The Act applies in stages: bans first, then duties for general-purpose models, then the high-risk regime. The Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, changed that sequence before the high-risk rules applied. The Commission proposed it on 19 November 2025 as part of a wider drive to simplify EU digital rules. The European Parliament approved the agreed text on 16 June 2026 and the Council on 29 June [@councileu2026proposal]. The regulation was signed on 8 July, published on 24 July and in force from 27 July 2026 [@europeanparliament2026regulation]. Its recitals blame the "delayed availability of standards, common specifications, and alternative guidance and the delayed establishment of national competent authorities" [@councileu2026proposal]. The omnibus did more than move dates. It softened the duty to ensure staff "AI literacy" into a duty to "take measures to support" it. It added a new Article 4a that allows providers and deployers, exceptionally, to process sensitive personal data such as ethnicity to detect and correct bias, but only where synthetic or anonymised data would not do, with deletion once the bias is corrected. It extended relief for small firms to a new category of "small mid-cap enterprises". These firms gain simplified technical documentation and, for all fines except those for prohibited practices, a cap at the lower of the fixed amount and the percentage. It required every member state to have at least one AI regulatory sandbox, a supervised testing environment, running by 2 August 2027. And it gave the Commission's AI Office exclusive competence, with some exceptions, over AI systems built on a general-purpose model by the same provider or group and over AI systems integrated into the very large online platforms and search engines designated under the Digital Services Act. The AI Office gained powers of inspection and the ability to impose periodic penalty payments of up to 5 per cent of average daily turnover [@europeanparliament2026regulation].

### High-risk systems

The second tier, **high-risk** AI, comes in two kinds. Under Article 6(1), an AI system is high-risk if it is a safety component of, or itself, a product covered by the EU product-safety laws listed in Annex I (machinery, toys, medical devices, lifts and others) that already requires third-party conformity assessment. Under Article 6(2), it is high-risk if it falls into one of eight areas in Annex III [@europeanparliament2024regulation]: biometrics (remote identification, but not one-to-one verification such as unlocking a phone; categorisation by sensitive attributes; emotion recognition); safety components of critical infrastructure such as road traffic and the supply of water, gas, heating and electricity; education (admission, grading, deciding access to levels of education, detecting cheating); employment (recruitment, filtering applications, decisions on promotion, termination, task allocation and performance monitoring); essential services (eligibility for public benefits, credit scoring but not fraud detection, pricing of life and health insurance, triage of emergency calls); law enforcement (risk assessments, polygraph-like tools, evaluating evidence, profiling); migration, asylum and border control; and the administration of justice and democratic processes, including systems intended to influence elections.

Under Article 6(3), a system in these areas escapes the high-risk regime if it poses no significant risk because it performs "a narrow procedural task", improves the result of a completed human activity, detects deviations from prior decision patterns without replacing human review, or performs a preparatory task. The derogation never applies to a system that profiles individuals. A provider relying on it must document its assessment before placing the system on the market and show it to authorities on request [@europeanparliament2024regulation].

A provider of a high-risk system must meet the requirements of Articles 9 to 15. It must run a risk-management system across the system's lifecycle (Article 9). Its training, validation and test data must be "relevant, sufficiently representative, and to the best extent possible, free of errors and complete" for the intended purpose (Article 10(3)). It must keep technical documentation and automatic logs, give deployers instructions for use, and achieve appropriate accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity. Human oversight (Article 14) must enable the people supervising the system to understand its limits and to remain aware of "automation bias", the tendency to over-rely on outputs. For remote biometric identification, no action may be taken on a match unless it has been "separately verified and confirmed by at least two natural persons", except in law enforcement, migration, border control and asylum where national or EU law deems this disproportionate [@europeanparliament2024regulation].

Compliance is shown through **conformity assessment**, the procedure EU product law uses to check that a product meets its requirements before sale. For seven of the eight Annex III areas, the provider assesses itself under the internal-control procedure of Annex VI, "which does not provide for the involvement of a notified body". Only for biometric systems may an independent *notified body* be required: the provider may choose one, and must use one where harmonised standards or common specifications do not exist or have not been fully applied. AI in Annex I products is checked within the existing sectoral procedure, often by the notified bodies already designated for, say, medical devices. A substantial modification after release triggers a new assessment, but changes to a continuously learning system that were planned and documented in advance do not [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. The system then receives a CE marking and, for Annex III systems, is registered in an EU database.

Deployers have duties of their own: using the system according to its instructions, assigning trained staff to oversee it, keeping logs and informing workers before workplace use (Chapter 8). Public bodies, private entities providing public services, and deployers of credit-scoring and life- or health-insurance systems must also carry out a **fundamental rights impact assessment** before first use (Article 27). It must identify the groups likely to be affected, the specific risks to them, the oversight arrangements and the remedies if risks materialise [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. After deployment, providers must monitor performance and report serious incidents: within 15 days of becoming aware of one, within 10 days if a person has died, and within two days for a widespread infringement or a serious and irreversible disruption of critical infrastructure (Article 73).

The third tier concerns **transparency**. Under Article 50, people must be told when they are interacting with an AI system unless it is obvious. Providers of systems that generate synthetic audio, images, video or text must mark outputs in a machine-readable way so that they can be detected as artificial, and deployers of "deepfakes" must disclose that the content has been generated or manipulated [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. These duties applied from 2 August 2026, with the omnibus giving generative systems already on the market until 2 December 2026 to add marking (Table 13.1); Chapter 9 covers the technology. Everything else falls into the fourth tier, **minimal risk**, with no new obligations.

### General-purpose AI models and the systemic-risk threshold

General-purpose AI (GPAI) models, such as the large language models of Chapter 3, are regulated as models, before anyone knows which systems will be built on them. Since 2 August 2025, all GPAI providers must keep technical documentation for the AI Office, give downstream developers enough information "to have a good understanding of the capabilities and limitations" of the model, put in place a policy to comply with EU copyright law, including rights holders' opt-outs, and publish "a sufficiently detailed summary about the content used for training" on a Commission template (Article 53). Models released under a free and open-source licence with their weights public are exempt from the first two duties but not from the copyright policy or the training summary, and the exemption does not apply to models with systemic risk [@europeanparliament2024regulation].

Which models count? Under Commission guidelines published on 18 July 2025, a model is indicatively general-purpose if it was trained with more than 10^23 floating-point operations and can generate language (text or audio), images or video from text. A company that fine-tunes someone else's model becomes the provider of a new model only if its modification uses more than a third of the original model's training compute, an indicative criterion the Commission ties to a "significant change" in generality, capabilities or systemic risk [@europeancommission2026faq; @fli2025overview].

A GPAI model has **systemic risk**, a legal category meaning risks with large-scale effects across the Union, if it has "high impact capabilities". Under Article 51(2), this is presumed when the cumulative training compute exceeds 10^25 floating-point operations (FLOP). The Commission may also designate a model on its own initiative or after a "qualified alert" from its scientific panel, and may change the threshold by delegated act. A provider must notify the Commission "without delay and in any event within two weeks" of crossing the threshold, and may argue that its model nonetheless poses no systemic risk; a designated provider can ask for reassessment after six months (Article 52) [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. Chapter 11 uses "systemic risks" for a broader family of harms from widespread deployment; the AI Act's category attaches to individual models.

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: What does 10^25 FLOP mean?**

A floating-point operation is a single arithmetic step, such as multiplying two decimal numbers. 10^25 is a 1 followed by 25 zeros. With the rule of thumb from Section 4.1 (training compute ≈ 6 × parameters × training tokens), a model with 100 billion parameters trained on 15 trillion tokens uses 6 × 10^11 × 1.5 × 10^13 = 9 × 10^24 FLOP, just under the EU threshold. Train the same model on 20 trillion tokens and it reaches 1.2 × 10^25 FLOP and is presumed to carry systemic risk. California and New York set their thresholds ten times higher, at 10^26. The guidelines' indicative floor for a general-purpose model, 10^23 FLOP, is a hundred times lower than the EU systemic-risk line. Because the thresholds are fixed while the training compute of frontier models has grown about five-fold a year since 2020 [@epochai2026trends], more models cross them every year. Epoch AI counted 33 publicly disclosed models trained with at least 10^25 FLOP by June 2025, from 12 developers [@rahman2025over].
:::

Providers of systemic-risk models must also perform model evaluations "in accordance with standardised protocols and tools reflecting the state of the art", including adversarial testing; assess and mitigate systemic risks at Union level; track and report serious incidents to the AI Office "without undue delay"; and ensure "an adequate level of cybersecurity protection" for the model and its infrastructure (Article 55) [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. Models already on the market before August 2025 have until 2 August 2027 to comply.

To show how providers could comply, the Commission published a **General-Purpose AI Code of Practice** on 10 July 2025. It has three chapters: transparency, copyright, and safety and security, the last applying only to systemic-risk models. Signing is voluntary, but under Article 53(4) providers may rely on such a code to demonstrate compliance until harmonised standards exist [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. When the Commission published its list of signatories on 1 August 2025, it included Amazon, Anthropic, Google, IBM, Microsoft, Mistral AI and OpenAI. xAI signed only the safety and security chapter, and Meta declined to sign [@europeancommission2025generalpurpose; @haeck2025commission]. Non-signatories must show compliance by other means.

### Enforcement and penalties

Enforcement is split between levels. Each member state designates market-surveillance authorities to supervise AI systems on its territory; the Commission's AI Office supervises general-purpose models directly and, since the omnibus, many systems built on them [@europeanparliament2024regulation; @europeanparliament2026regulation]. An AI Board of member-state representatives coordinates, and a scientific panel of independent experts can issue alerts about systemic-risk models. A single general-purpose model may sit inside thousands of products, so problems found nationally may have to be traced to a provider supervised in Brussels. The penalty provisions for member states have applied since 2 August 2025, and the Commission's power to fine GPAI providers since 2 August 2026. Table 13.2 sets out the ceilings.

## 13.3 The United States: executive orders, states and pre-emption

As of October 2026 the United States had no comprehensive federal AI statute. Federal policy has been set mainly by executive orders, which bind agencies but can be revoked by the next president.

Executive Order 14110, signed by President Biden on 30 October 2023, required developers of the largest models to report to the government on training and safety testing; its trigger was a model trained with "greater than 10^26 integer or floating-point operations" [@biden2023safe]. On 20 January 2025 President Trump revoked it [@trump2025initial]. Executive Order 14179, signed three days later, declared it US policy "to sustain and enhance America's global AI dominance" and ordered a review of actions taken under the revoked order [@trump2025removing]. The resulting *America's AI Action Plan*, published in July 2025, organised policy around innovation, infrastructure, and international diplomacy and security, and proposed that federal funding should not flow to states with "burdensome" AI rules [@whitehouse2025winning]. The same month, Executive Order 14319 used the government's purchasing power instead of regulation. It directed agencies to procure only large language models that follow two "Unbiased AI Principles": "truth-seeking" and "ideological neutrality", the latter meaning that developers "shall not intentionally encode partisan or ideological judgments" into outputs unless those judgments are prompted by or readily accessible to the user. Guidance from the Office of Management and Budget was to let vendors show compliance by disclosing system prompts, specifications or evaluations, avoiding disclosure of model weights where practicable [@trump2025preventing].

### The pre-emption fight

**Pre-emption** is the doctrine under which valid federal law overrides conflicting state law. In July 2025 the Senate voted 99 to 1 to strip from a budget bill a ten-year moratorium on enforcing state AI laws [@knapp2026hidden]. On 11 December 2025 Executive Order 14365 took another route [@trump2025ensuring]. It ordered the Attorney General to create an **AI Litigation Task Force** to challenge state AI laws; asked the Commerce Department to identify "onerous" state laws within 90 days; conditioned some federal broadband funds on states not enforcing such laws; and told the Federal Communications Commission and the Federal Trade Commission to consider a federal disclosure standard and to explain when state rules requiring changes to "truthful outputs" are pre-empted by federal consumer-protection law. Because an executive order cannot itself override state statutes, it also called for legislation, with child-safety rules left untouched.

The Attorney General established the task force by memorandum on 9 January 2026, with the "sole responsibility" of challenging state AI laws "on grounds that such laws unconstitutionally regulate interstate commerce, are preempted by existing Federal regulations, or are otherwise unlawful" [@usdoj2026artificial]. The legislative route followed in July 2026. The FRONTIER Act (H.R. 9925), introduced on 23 July 2026 by Representative Jay Obernolte, would bar states from imposing new substantive obligations on AI developers in three "covered subject areas" (frontier-risk transparency, third-party auditing and independent verification, and incident reporting), while preserving generally applicable laws, state regulation of deployers and users, and rules protecting minors. As of 22 October 2026 it had been referred to committee and had passed neither chamber [@uscongress2026frontier].

Federal action in 2026 continued to favour voluntary arrangements. Executive Order 14409 of 2 June 2026 asked federal agencies, including the standards body NIST, to develop a classified benchmark of models' cyber capabilities and a *voluntary* framework under which developers of "covered frontier models" could give the government access for up to 30 days before releasing a model to other trusted partners. It stated that nothing in it authorised "a mandatory governmental licensing, preclearance, or permitting requirement" [@trump2026promoting]. Executive Order 14434 of 29 September 2026 directed agencies to use "Super Intelligence" and "SI" in place of "artificial intelligence" and "AI" in non-statutory documents, without requiring changes to existing regulations [@trump2026inaugurating].

### The states

With little federal legislation, the states have written most binding US AI rules. According to the 2026 AI Index, the number of AI-related bills enacted by US states rose from fewer than 10 in 2020 to 150 in 2025; California enacted 20 that year, Texas 12 and New York 10 [@sajadieh2026ai]. California's SB 243 on companion chatbots is discussed in Chapter 10. Three kinds of law matter most for developers.

The first is frontier-model transparency. California's Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act (SB 53) was signed on 29 September 2025 and took effect on 1 January 2026 [@californiagovernor2025governor]. It defines a "frontier model" as one trained with more than 10^26 operations, counting fine-tuning and reinforcement learning on top of the original run. It targets "catastrophic risk": a foreseeable risk that a model materially contributes to the death or serious injury of more than 50 people, or more than $1 billion in damage, in a single incident, through expert help with chemical, biological, radiological or nuclear weapons, through autonomous cyber-attacks or crimes, or by evading its developer's control. All frontier developers must publish a transparency report when they deploy a new frontier model and report critical safety incidents to the state Office of Emergency Services within 15 days; an incident posing an imminent risk of death or serious injury must be disclosed within 24 hours to an appropriate authority such as a law-enforcement or public-safety agency. "Large" developers, with annual revenue above $500 million, must also publish a frontier AI framework describing how they manage catastrophic risk, and face civil penalties of up to $1 million per violation, recoverable only by the Attorney General. The law protects whistleblowers [@californialegislature2025sb53; @metr2026frontier].

New York's RAISE Act, signed on 19 December 2025 and amended by a law signed on 27 March 2026, takes effect on 1 January 2027 [@nyassembly2026a9449]. As amended, it follows SB 53's structure but is stricter in three respects. Incidents must be reported within 72 hours; a new office within the Department of Financial Services supervises developers and funds itself through assessments on them; and penalties for large developers reach $1 million for a first violation and $3 million for each subsequent one. The second kind of law bans specific harmful uses. Texas's Responsible Artificial Intelligence Governance Act (HB 149), signed on 22 June 2025 and in force since 1 January 2026, prohibits developing or deploying AI that intentionally aims to incite self-harm, harm to others or crime, AI developed with the intent to discriminate unlawfully against a protected class, and social scoring by government. A disparate impact "is not sufficient by itself to demonstrate an intent to discriminate". Only the Attorney General can enforce it, after a 60-day period to cure; penalties run from $10,000 to $12,000 for curable violations and from $80,000 to $200,000 for uncurable ones, and the Act creates a 36-month regulatory sandbox [@texaslegislature2025hb149].

The third kind regulates consequential decisions such as hiring and lending, and has proved the most contested.

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: Colorado's AI Act, from statute to stand-off**

In 2024 Colorado passed SB 24-205, the first broad US state law on "high-risk" AI systems used in consequential decisions such as hiring, lending and housing. It required both developers and deployers to "use reasonable care to protect consumers from any known or reasonably foreseeable risks of algorithmic discrimination", and deployers to carry out impact assessments. After a delay, it was due to take effect on 30 June 2026 [@usdoj2026complaint]. Executive Order 14365 singled it out, arguing that the law "may even force AI models to produce false results" [@trump2025ensuring].

On 9 April 2026 xAI sued Colorado's Attorney General in federal court [@usdccolorado2026docket]. On 24 April the Department of Justice filed to join the case as a plaintiff, arguing mainly that the law violated the constitutional guarantee of equal protection [@usdoj2026complaint]. On 27 April a federal magistrate judge approved the parties' agreement that the state would not begin enforcing SB 24-205, "or any legislation replacing or amending" it, until 14 days after the court rules on a future request for a preliminary injunction [@usdccolorado2026minute].

On 14 May 2026 the Governor signed SB 26-189, which repealed and replaced the 2024 law [@coloradogeneralassembly2026automated]. The new law regulates "automated decision-making technology" through disclosure: notices to consumers, an explanation within 30 days after an adverse decision, and rights to correct data and to request human review. Violations count as deceptive trade practices enforced by the Attorney General, with no private right of action. Most duties apply from 1 January 2027, and the Attorney General must adopt clarifying rules by then. As of October 2026 the federal case remained open.
:::

## 13.4 China and the United Kingdom

China has built its AI rules in layers, through targeted measures issued by government agencies led by the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC), rather than one horizontal law. The first layer, the Provisions on the Administration of Algorithmic Recommendations in Internet Information Services, has applied since 1 March 2022 [@cac2021provisions]. They require providers to offer users an option not targeted at their personal characteristics, or a convenient way to switch recommendation off (Article 17). Under Article 24, providers whose services have "public opinion attributes or social mobilisation capacity" must, within ten working days of launching, register in an online **algorithm filing** system their name, service form, field of application, algorithm type and a self-assessment report. Later rules reuse this registry, so regulators know who operates which algorithm before anything goes wrong. Rules on "deep synthesis" (deepfakes and other synthetic media) followed from 10 January 2023 [@cac2022provisions].

The **Interim Measures for the Administration of Generative Artificial Intelligence Services**, issued by seven agencies on 10 July 2023, took effect on 15 August 2023 [@cac2023interim]. They apply to generative AI services offered to the public in mainland China. Providers must uphold "socialist core values", prevent prohibited content, use lawfully sourced training data, protect personal information and label generated images and video. Services with public-opinion attributes or mobilisation capacity must undergo a security assessment and file their algorithms. In effect, a public chatbot with such attributes needs both a registration and a security review before launch, a form of pre-market control that no Western jurisdiction applies to general-purpose models.

China followed with rules on specific issues. The **Measures for Labelling AI-Generated and Synthetic Content**, issued by four agencies in March 2025 and in force from 1 September 2025, require explicit labels that users can see or hear (text, sound or graphics) and implicit labels in file metadata that identify the content as AI-generated and name the provider [@cac2025measures]. In October 2025 the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress amended the Cybersecurity Law to add an article on the "safe and sound development of AI", in force from 1 January 2026 [@xinhua2025china]. In April 2026 five agencies issued interim measures on "anthropomorphic" AI services that simulate human personalities for companionship or emotional support. Effective from 15 July 2026, they require security assessments, algorithm filing and protections for minors and older users [@xinhua2026five]. Internationally, China published a **Global AI Governance Action Plan** of 13 points at the World Artificial Intelligence Conference in Shanghai on 26 July 2025. It frames AI as "an international public good", calls for open-source sharing and for international cooperation on capacity-building, especially for the Global South, and ties governance to the United Nations' Global Digital Compact [@mfa2025global]. 

The **United Kingdom** has so far kept to the sectoral approach of its 2023 white paper [@dsit2023proinnovation]. Its policy since 2025 has emphasised adoption and infrastructure. The *AI Opportunities Action Plan*, written by the entrepreneur Matt Clifford and published on 13 January 2025, made 50 recommendations [@dsit2025ai]. The government agreed to 47, partially agreed to two and gave no position on the one concerning text-and-data-mining reform, pending a consultation. Among the agreed items were "AI Growth Zones" with easier access to power and planning and expanding public compute capacity "by at least 20x by 2030" [@dsit2025opportunities]. The AI Safety Institute was renamed the AI Security Institute in February 2025 and refocused on risks such as cyber-attacks, chemical and biological misuse and crime; the government said it would "not focus on bias or free speech" [@dsit2025tackling]. Labour's 2024 manifesto had pledged binding rules for the most powerful models, yet the King's Speech of 13 May 2026 contained no AI bill [@iapp2026kings]. The UK's most consequential AI decision of 2026 concerned copyright (Section 13.6).

## 13.5 International coordination

AI models cross borders easily, which makes a case for international rules. Yet states disagree about values, compete for economic and military advantage, and share few institutions with authority over AI. Figure 13.2 places the main international milestones alongside the national ones.

### Summits

A series of summits began at Bletchley Park in the UK on 1–2 November 2023, where 28 countries and the EU, including the US and China, signed the **Bletchley Declaration**. It recognised the potential for "serious, even catastrophic, harm" from the most capable frontier models [@ukgov2023bletchley]. At the follow-up summit in Seoul in May 2024, 16 companies signed the **Frontier AI Safety Commitments**, promising to publish safety frameworks with thresholds at which risks would be intolerable and, in the extreme, not to develop or deploy a model whose risks could not be kept below them; four more companies joined later [@dsit2024frontier]. Chapter 12 examines the frameworks that resulted.

The Paris summit of 10–11 February 2025 was renamed the AI Action Summit and shifted emphasis towards inclusion, sustainability and economic opportunity [@elysee2025statement]. Its closing statement listed priorities including narrowing digital divides, avoiding market concentration, and considering AI's effects on work and the environment. It was signed by 63 countries plus the EU and the African Union Commission (as listed in October 2026), but not by the United States or the United Kingdom. India hosted the AI Impact Summit in New Delhi on 16–20 February 2026, organised around "people, planet and progress", which the Indian government described as the first global AI summit held in the Global South [@pib2026india]. The titles, from "safety" to "action" to "impact", track a shift in political priorities.

None of the summits' outcomes is legally binding or monitored. Their effects have been indirect: the Seoul promise to publish safety frameworks reappears as a legal duty in SB 53 and the EU Code of Practice. They are also exposed to national politics, as the US and UK signatures at Bletchley and their absence from Paris show.

### Treaties, the United Nations and the OECD

The only binding international treaty specifically on AI is the Council of Europe's **Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law** (CETS No. 225). A **framework convention** sets general obligations and leaves details to national law. This one requires parties to ensure that activities across the lifecycle of AI systems are consistent with human rights, democracy and the rule of law. It was opened for signature on 5 September 2024, when the UK, the United States and the EU were among the first to sign [@moj2024uk; @councilofeurope2024framework]. It enters into force about three months after five signatories, including at least three Council of Europe member states, have ratified it. Signature alone does not bind a state; ratification does. According to the Council of Europe's treaty office, as of 22 October 2026 the convention had 21 signatories, including Canada, Japan and Ukraine, but only one ratification, by the EU on 15 May 2026, so it was not in force.

The United Nations has acted through the General Assembly. Resolution 78/265 of 21 March 2024 called for "safe, secure and trustworthy" AI for sustainable development [@un2024seizing]. Resolution 79/325 of 26 August 2025 established two bodies limited to the non-military domain [@un2025terms]. The first is an **Independent International Scientific Panel on AI** of 40 experts serving three-year terms, to produce evidence-based assessments. The second is a **Global Dialogue on AI Governance**, to meet in Geneva in 2026 alongside the International Telecommunication Union's AI for Good summit and in New York in 2027. The first Geneva session took place on 6–7 July 2026 with delegations from 163 countries, and the Panel presented a preliminary report to it [@un2026global]. The model is loosely that of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, with the handicaps that AI capabilities change far faster than the climate and much of the evidence is held by private companies.

The **OECD AI Principles**, adopted in 2019 and updated in May 2024, had 47 adherents in 2026. They set out values such as human rights, transparency, robustness and accountability [@oecd2024principles], and their definition of an AI system was adopted almost word for word in the EU AI Act. The G7's **Hiroshima AI Process** produced International Guiding Principles and a Code of Conduct for organisations developing advanced AI systems, endorsed by G7 leaders on 30 October 2023 [@mic2023hiroshima]. Since February 2025 the OECD has run a voluntary reporting framework against the Code; its first report drew on submissions from 20 organisations [@oecd2025hiroshima].

Finally, **technical standards** turn principles into checklists that auditors can verify. ISO/IEC 42001, published in 2023, specifies requirements for an AI management system: the policies, roles and processes an organisation uses to develop or use AI responsibly [@iso2023information]. Organisations can be certified against it, as they are against quality-management standards. Under the EU AI Act, compliance with **harmonised standards**, European standards requested by the Commission, gives a legal presumption of conformity. Their late arrival helped cause the omnibus delays, and the omnibus now requires the Commission to request them "without undue delay" [@europeanparliament2026regulation].

## 13.6 Copyright, training data and personal data

Large models are trained on vast collections of text, images and music, much of it protected by copyright and much of it containing personal data. Two copyright questions arise. The **input question** asks whether copying works to train a model infringes copyright. The **output question** asks whether a model, or what it generates, reproduces protected works. By October 2026 courts in the US, the UK and Germany had given partial and partly conflicting answers, summarised in Table 13.4.

### The United States: fair use

In the United States the central defence is **fair use**, which allows some unlicensed use of copyrighted works. Courts weigh four factors: the purpose and character of the use, including whether it is "transformative" (serves a new purpose); the nature of the work; the amount used; and the effect on the market for the original.

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: Bartz v. Anthropic**

Authors sued Anthropic, alleging that it had trained its Claude models on their books without permission. The court record describes two sources. Anthropic had downloaded at least five million copies of books from Library Genesis and at least two million from the Pirate Library Mirror, "over seven million copies of books" in all, knowing they were pirated. Later it spent "many millions of dollars" buying print books, often used, which were stripped from their bindings, scanned and discarded [@usdcnortherncal2025bartz].

On 23 June 2025 Judge William Alsup ruled that training the models was "exceedingly transformative and was a fair use". He rejected the argument that models would flood the market with competing works, comparing it to complaining that "training schoolchildren to write well" would produce competing works. Scanning purchased print books was also fair use. Building a permanent library from pirated copies was not excused, and that claim went towards a class-action trial.

The parties settled for $1.5 billion. On 20 July 2026 Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín, who had taken over the case, granted final approval [@bloomberglaw2026anthropic]. The settlement covers more than 480,000 works at about $3,100 per work and requires Anthropic to destroy the pirated datasets. According to the settlement website, claimants were being notified from September 2026, with payments to follow [@anthropicsettlement2026]. A settlement sets no precedent, but the case showed that how training data is acquired can matter as much as how it is used.
:::

Two days after the Bartz ruling, Judge Vince Chhabria granted summary judgment to Meta in *Kadrey v. Meta*, brought by thirteen authors over books Meta had downloaded from shadow libraries including LibGen and Anna's Archive [@usdcnortherncal2025kadrey]. (Summary judgment decides a case without a full trial when the key facts are not in dispute.) On outputs, even prompts designed to make the models regurgitate training data produced no more than 50 words and punctuation marks of the plaintiffs' books. Chhabria ruled for Meta only because the authors had not presented evidence on the harm he considered decisive, "market dilution": generative models could flood the market with works that compete with the originals. He warned that unlicensed training would, "in most cases", likely be illegal; the ruling bound only these thirteen authors.

On 29 September 2026 the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit decided *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence* on an interlocutory appeal from Judge Stephanos Bibas [@thirdcircuit2026thomson]. Ross, a legal-research start-up, had used 2,243 Westlaw headnotes (editorial summaries of points of law), obtained through a contractor, to train an AI search tool that it advertised as a competitor to Westlaw at prices "in line with" Westlaw's. The court held that the headnotes were copyrightable and that Ross's use was "minimally transformative, at best", since it served the same purpose as the original. On market harm it found that "the market for licensing headnotes as text to train AI is rapidly developing" and that Ross had usurped Thomson Reuters's opportunity to enter it. That reasoning, that training data has a licensing market of its own, could matter far beyond legal research. The court stressed, however, that "ROSS's AI was not a generative AI", and noted a statement filed by the US Department of Justice on 1 September 2026 in the consolidated cases against OpenAI in New York, which include the *New York Times* claims, arguing that training a large language model is transformative. Those cases remained pending in October 2026.

On outputs, US law requires a human author. In *Thaler v. Perlmutter* the Court of Appeals for the DC Circuit held on 18 March 2025 that the Copyright Act "requires all work to be authored in the first instance by a human being", upholding the refusal to register an image whose sole listed author was an AI system [@uscadc2025thaler]. The US Copyright Office published its analysis of copyrightability in January 2025 and of generative-AI training in pre-publication form on 9 May 2025; the training report had not been finalised by October 2026 [@uscopyrightoffice2025copyright].

### Europe: the model as a copy

In the UK, Getty Images sued Stability AI over the image generator Stable Diffusion [@highcourt2025getty]. Getty dropped its training claim for lack of evidence that training took place in the UK, leaving a claim that importing the model was secondary infringement because it was an "infringing copy". On 4 November 2025 the High Court rejected it. Although an "article" under UK copyright law can be intangible, Mrs Justice Joanna Smith held that "the model weights are not themselves an infringing copy and they do not store an infringing copy"; Stable Diffusion "does not store or reproduce any Copyright Works and nor has it ever done so". She found limited trade-mark infringement where outputs of older model versions showed Getty and iStock watermarks, calling these findings "historic and extremely limited in scope". She later gave Getty permission to appeal on the "infringing copy" point, a question of law "on which the minds of reasonable lawyers may differ" [@fairhurst2026permission]. No appeal decision had been reported by October 2026.

A German court, on different facts, reached the opposite conclusion about what a model contains. On 11 November 2025 the Munich Regional Court I largely upheld claims by GEMA, the German music collecting society, against two OpenAI companies over song lyrics by nine well-known German songwriters [@lgmuenchen2025gema]. According to the court's press release, the lyrics were "reproducibly contained" in the GPT-4 and GPT-4o models, a case of **memorisation**: the model's parameters had absorbed the training text in full, as shown by comparing the lyrics with ChatGPT's outputs, and the length of the texts ruled out coincidence. That fixation in parameters was a reproduction under EU law, even though it existed only as probability values. The **text and data mining exception** of EU law, in its German implementation, covered the preparatory copies made to assemble a training corpus but not copies retained in the model, because it rests on the premise that only information, not the work itself, is extracted. The outputs also infringed, and OpenAI, not its users, was responsible, since simple prompts had produced them and OpenAI controlled the training and architecture. The judgment was not final. Under the EU AI Act, GPAI providers must in any case respect machine-readable opt-outs from text and data mining and publish summaries of their training content [@europeanparliament2024regulation].

The two European rulings differ less than they seem: London found no evidence that Stable Diffusion stored Getty's images, Munich found that GPT-4 reproduced specific lyrics. Both treated memorisation as decisive, turning a legal question into a partly technical one about what a given model has stored.

Governments have hesitated to legislate. The UK's Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, which received Royal Assent on 19 June 2025, required the government to publish an economic impact assessment and a report on the use of copyright works in developing AI within nine months [@ukparliament2025data]. Its March 2026 report dropped its earlier preferred option, a broad exception allowing training unless rights holders opted out, after most consultation respondents rejected it. A broad exception with opt-out was "no longer the government's preferred way forward", and the government proposed to gather more evidence [@dsit2026report].

### Personal data

Text and images collected from the web contain **personal data**, information about an identifiable person. In the EU its use is governed by the GDPR, which requires a legal basis, such as consent or a "legitimate interest", for each use. National data-protection authorities enforce it, and they acted on AI years before the AI Act applied. On 30 March 2023 the Italian authority, the Garante, ordered OpenAI to stop processing Italian users' data through ChatGPT, citing missing information to users, no legal basis for the "massive collection and processing of personal data" used in training, and no age checks [@garante2023intelligenza]. In December 2024 it fined OpenAI €15 million, again citing the lack of a legal basis for training; in March 2026 the Tribunal of Rome upheld OpenAI's appeal against that decision [@garante2024chatgpt].

The European Data Protection Board, which brings together the EU's national authorities, set up a ChatGPT taskforce in April 2023. Its May 2024 report observed that because of "the probabilistic nature of the system" the training approach "may also produce biased or made up outputs", which users are likely to take as fact. The principle of data accuracy must nonetheless be complied with, and responsibility cannot be shifted to users through terms and conditions [@edpb2024report]. The Board's Opinion 28/2024 of December 2024 set out three points [@edpb2024opinion]. A model counts as anonymous only if it is "very unlikely" that people in the training data can be identified or their data extracted by queries. Legitimate interest can justify training if the processing is strictly necessary and passes a balancing test. And a model developed with unlawfully processed data may be unlawful to deploy "unless the model has been duly anonymised". Guidelines on web scraping for generative AI, adopted for public consultation on 7 July 2026, add practical expectations: defining precise collection criteria, filtering out sensitive categories of data, and excluding websites "which clearly oppose the scraping" [@edpb2026guidelines].

Enforcement has ranged from fines to negotiated changes. In May 2024 the Dutch authority fined Clearview AI €30.5 million for building a facial-recognition database for law enforcement, processing biometric data without a legal basis and without adequately informing the people concerned [@edpb2024dutch]. Large platforms have faced pressure rather than fines. In June 2024 Meta paused plans to train its models on public posts by adults in the EU after the Irish Data Protection Commission raised concerns, and started such training from 27 May 2025, after changing its notices and making it easier to object [@dpc2025statement].

## 13.7 Liability, compute thresholds and the cost of regulation

### Liability

When an AI system gives dangerous advice or makes a discriminatory decision, it may be unclear whether the developer, the deploying company or the user is responsible. The EU's answer rests on product liability. The revised Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853 applies to products placed on the market from 9 December 2026 and covers "all types of software", including AI systems. Manufacturers can be liable for defects that arise after release through updates or machine learning [@europeancommission2024liability]. A second instrument, the AI Liability Directive proposed in September 2022 to ease fault-based claims, was withdrawn by the Commission on 6 October 2025 [@europeanparliament2025procedure]. EU victims of AI harm therefore rely on the strict-liability product regime plus national tort law.

The US has no equivalent statute. Liability is developing through state laws, such as SB 53's civil penalties, Texas's attorney-general enforcement and Colorado's treatment of violations as deceptive trade practices, and through individual lawsuits against developers, several of which concern chatbots and minors (Chapter 10).

### Compute thresholds and their critics

Regulators need a way to decide which models deserve extra scrutiny before anyone knows exactly what those models can do. The most common answer has been a **compute threshold**, a legal trigger based on the computation used in training. The EU uses 10^25 FLOP as a presumption of systemic risk; California and New York use 10^26, as did the revoked Executive Order 14110 (Table 13.3).

The case for thresholds is practical. Compute is easier to observe, count and restrict than data or algorithms, as Section 7.7 explains [@sastry2024computing]. In a preprint, Heim and Koessler argue that training compute is the best available metric for *identifying* models that warrant oversight: it correlates with capabilities, can be measured before deployment and can be checked by outsiders [@heim2024training]. They stress that it is an imperfect proxy for risk and should serve only as an initial filter that triggers evaluations. Critics question even that role. Hooker, in another preprint, argues that the relationship between compute and risk is "highly uncertain and rapidly changing" [@hooker2024limitations]. Smaller models trained with better data and methods can match larger ones, so a fixed threshold may both catch harmless models and miss dangerous ones.

Lawmakers have hedged. The EU lets the Commission designate models on other grounds and amend the threshold; SB 53 counts fine-tuning and reinforcement learning towards its threshold. The US government's 2026 cyber framework abandoned compute altogether, defining "covered frontier models" by results on a classified capability benchmark [@trump2026promoting]. The rules triggered by these thresholds remain mostly about **transparency and process**: publishing safety frameworks, testing models, reporting incidents and protecting whistleblowers. Only China requires a security assessment before certain public services launch, and the 2026 US order explicitly rules out licensing. Whether disclosure duties will suffice for the most capable systems remains a central open question, linking to the safety methods of Chapter 12 and the scenarios of Chapter 14.

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Does AI regulation hold back innovation?**

The 2024 report on European competitiveness prepared by Mario Draghi for the European Commission counted "around 100 tech-focused laws and over 270 regulators active in digital networks" in the EU and cited the AI Act's extra duties for general-purpose models above a compute threshold as an example of its precautionary approach; the report concluded that the overall regulatory burden leaves only larger companies, "which are often non-EU based", able to bear the costs of compliance [@draghi2024future]. The best evidence comes from the GDPR, an earlier EU digital law with a clear start date (May 2018). A peer-reviewed study found that EU technology ventures afterwards raised less money, in fewer and smaller deals, than comparable US ventures [@jia2021short]. A working paper by Janßen and colleagues found that the GDPR induced the exit of "about a third of available apps", while new app entry fell by half [@janssen2022gdpr]. A study by Demirer and colleagues, circulated as a working paper in 2024 and published in the *Journal of Political Economy* in 2026, found in its working-paper version that EU firms cut data storage by 25 per cent and data processing by 15 per cent relative to comparable US firms, consistent with a 21 per cent rise in the cost of data [@demirer2024data].

The case against starts from the size of those effects and their causes. The same Demirer study finds that the higher cost of data translates into "only a 0.1–0.6% rise in overall production costs", because data plays a small role in production compared with computation [@demirer2024data]. The legal scholar Anu Bradford argues that the US–EU gap in technology firms is better explained by fragmented capital markets, bankruptcy law, the incomplete single market and immigration policy than by digital regulation, and calls the trade-off a "false choice" [@bradford2024false]. The AI investment gap is real: in 2025 private AI investment reached $285.9 billion in the United States against $5.9 billion in the United Kingdom, Europe's largest recipient (Section 7.7) [@sajadieh2026ai]. But it opened before the AI Act's main duties applied, so it cannot be their effect.

Neither side has decisive evidence for AI. The GDPR studies concern a data-protection law and mostly its short-run effects, and the AI Act's high-risk duties will not apply until December 2027. A convincing test would compare investment, firm entry and model releases in the EU before and after 2027–2028 with similar markets lacking such rules, separating the Act's effects from those of energy prices and capital markets.
:::
