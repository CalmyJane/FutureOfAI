# Chapter 13: Governing AI: Law, Policy and International Coordination

::: {.objectives}
**After working through this chapter you should be able to:**

- explain the main tools of AI governance (binding law, voluntary commitments, technical standards and liability) and the idea of risk-based regulation;
- compare the approaches of the European Union, the United States, China and the United Kingdom as they stood in October 2026;
- evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of training-compute thresholds as a trigger for regulating frontier models;
- summarise the main court rulings on copyright and AI training, and identify which questions remain open;
- assess what international bodies, summits and treaties can and cannot achieve in coordinating AI policy.

The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act was written around a date. Its main rules for "high-risk" AI systems, such as software used to screen job applicants or to decide access to public benefits, were to apply from 2 August 2026 [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. When that date arrived, they did not. A few days earlier, on 27 July 2026, an amending law known as the Digital Omnibus on AI had entered into force. It moved the deadline to 2 December 2027 for most of these systems and to 2 August 2028 for AI built into products such as machinery or medical devices [@europeanparliament2026regulation]. The legislators gave a practical reason: the technical standards and national authorities needed to apply the rules were not ready [@councileu2026proposal].

In the same months, the US state of Colorado repealed its own broad AI law before it took effect and replaced it with a narrower one. A federal court had already recorded the state's agreement not to enforce the original law while a lawsuit by Elon Musk's company xAI, joined by the US government, was pending [@usdccolorado2026minute; @coloradogeneralassembly2026automated]. Both episodes show something students of AI governance need to accept early: the law in this area is moving. This chapter therefore describes the landscape as it stood in October 2026, gives dates for every rule it mentions, and marks clearly what was still pending.

## 13.1 The governance toolkit

**Governance** means the full set of rules, institutions and practices that shape how a technology is built and used. Law made by parliaments and enforced by courts is only one part. Governments also issue executive orders and guidance, companies sign voluntary commitments, standards bodies write technical specifications, and courts apply older law (on copyright, negligence or discrimination) to new cases. A useful first question about any AI rule is therefore: what kind of instrument is it, and who can enforce it?

Four families of instrument recur in this chapter. **Hard law** is binding and enforceable, such as an EU regulation or a US state statute, usually backed by fines. **Soft law** covers declarations, principles and voluntary codes; it relies on reputation, peer pressure and the hope that it will later be written into hard law. **Technical standards** describe how to do something, for example how to run a risk-management process, and often become the practical route to complying with hard law. **Liability** rules decide who pays when harm occurs, and they shape behaviour after the fact rather than before it.

A second distinction is between horizontal and sectoral regulation. A **horizontal** law applies across the whole economy to anything that meets its definition of AI; the EU AI Act is the leading example. A **sectoral** approach leaves AI to the existing regulators of finance, health, transport and so on. The UK's 2023 white paper chose the sectoral path: it set five principles (safety, transparency, fairness, accountability, and contestability and redress) and asked existing regulators to apply them, without a new AI statute or AI regulator [@dsit2023proinnovation]. An analogy helps here. A horizontal law resembles a national building code that applies to every structure, whereas a sectoral approach resembles separate rules for hospitals, schools and bridges. The analogy breaks down because AI, unlike concrete, can be repurposed after release: a general-purpose model written for one sector can be used in all of them.

::: {.box .concept}
**Key concept: Risk-based regulation**

Risk-based regulation scales obligations to the harm a system could cause in a given use. Rather than regulating "AI" as such, it asks what the system is used for and who could be hurt. A spam filter faces no new duties; a system that ranks job candidates must meet documentation, testing and human-oversight requirements; a few uses are banned outright. The approach concentrates effort where harm is likeliest, but it depends on drawing the categories well, and general-purpose models that can serve any use fit it awkwardly.
:::

Each instrument has typical weaknesses. Hard law is slow to make and slow to change, which matters when the underlying technology changes within months. Soft law is fast but has no penalty for breach. Standards take years to agree and are written largely by industry experts. Liability operates only after harm and depends on courts finding a defendant, a defect and a causal link. Most jurisdictions therefore mix instruments, and the mix they choose reveals their priorities.

## 13.2 The European Union's AI Act

The AI Act is Regulation (EU) 2024/1689. It entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in stages [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. As a regulation, rather than a directive, it applies directly in all 27 member states without national transposition. Figure 13.1 shows its structure: four tiers of risk for AI systems, plus a separate set of rules for the general-purpose models on which many systems are built.

![Figure 13.1: The risk tiers of the EU AI Act. Obligations rise towards the top of the pyramid; general-purpose AI models are regulated in a separate track that cuts across the tiers, with extra duties for models presumed to pose "systemic risk". Source: @europeanparliament2024regulation; @europeanparliament2026regulation.](figures/fig-13-01.svg){#fig:13-01}

At the top are **prohibited practices**, listed in Article 5 and banned since 2 February 2025. They include AI that uses manipulative or deceptive techniques to distort behaviour and cause significant harm. Also banned are social scoring that leads to unjustified unfavourable treatment; predicting a person's risk of committing a crime solely from profiling or personality traits; building facial-recognition databases by untargeted scraping of images from the internet or CCTV; inferring emotions in workplaces and schools (except for medical or safety reasons); and, with narrow exceptions, real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. The 2026 omnibus added two further bans from 2 December 2026: AI that generates non-consensual intimate images of identifiable people, and AI that generates child sexual abuse material [@europeanparliament2026regulation].

The second tier is **high-risk** AI. It comes in two kinds. The first is AI that serves as a safety component of a product already covered by EU product law (Annex I). The second is AI used in eight sensitive areas listed in Annex III: biometrics; critical infrastructure; education and vocational training; employment and worker management; access to essential private and public services and benefits; law enforcement; migration, asylum and border control; and the administration of justice and democratic processes [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. Providers of such systems must operate a risk-management system, use appropriate data governance, keep technical documentation and logs, enable human oversight, and meet standards of accuracy and robustness before placing the system on the market.

The third tier concerns **transparency**. Under Article 50, people must be told when they are interacting with an AI system unless it is obvious. Providers of systems that generate synthetic audio, images, video or text must mark outputs in a machine-readable way so that they can be detected as artificial. Deployers of "deepfakes" must disclose that the content has been generated or manipulated [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. These duties applied from 2 August 2026, although the omnibus gave generative systems already on the market before that date until 2 December 2026 to add machine-readable marking [@europeanparliament2026regulation]. The technical side of marking and provenance is covered in Chapter 9. Everything else falls into the fourth tier, **minimal risk**, with no new obligations.

General-purpose AI (GPAI) models, such as the large language models described in Chapter 3, are handled separately. Since 2 August 2025, all GPAI providers must keep technical documentation, give information to companies that build on their models, adopt a policy to comply with EU copyright law, and publish a summary of the content used for training (Article 53). Models whose training used more than 10^25 floating-point operations are presumed to pose **systemic risk**, a term for risks that could have large-scale effects across society. Their providers must also evaluate and adversarially test the model, assess and mitigate systemic risks, report serious incidents and ensure cybersecurity (Article 55) [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. The Commission's power to fine GPAI providers applied from 2 August 2026, and models already on the market before August 2025 have until 2 August 2027 to comply. Fines for the most serious breaches of the Act can reach €35 million or 7 per cent of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher [@europeanparliament2024regulation].

Enforcement is split between levels. Each member state must designate national authorities to supervise AI systems on its market, while the Commission's AI Office supervises general-purpose models directly [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. This split resembles the way the EU already polices product safety, with national inspectors for most goods and a central body for a few. It differs in one respect: a single general-purpose model may sit inside thousands of products across all member states, so problems found at the national level often have to be traced back to a provider supervised centrally.

To show how providers could comply, the Commission published a **General-Purpose AI Code of Practice** on 10 July 2025. It has three chapters: transparency, copyright, and safety and security; the last applies only to systemic-risk models [@europeancommission2025generalpurpose]. Signing is voluntary; under Article 53(4) of the Act, providers may rely on such a code to demonstrate compliance until harmonised standards exist [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. According to the Commission's list, signatories included Amazon, Anthropic, Google, IBM, Microsoft, Mistral AI and OpenAI; xAI signed only the safety and security chapter, and Meta declined to sign [@europeancommission2025generalpurpose].

The Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, was proposed by the Commission in November 2025 as part of a wider drive to simplify EU digital rules. The European Parliament approved the agreed text on 16 June 2026 [@councileu2026proposal]. Beyond the delays and new bans already mentioned, it softened the AI-literacy duty on companies into a duty to take measures to support literacy. It also extended some relief for smaller firms to "small mid-cap enterprises", and gave the Commission's AI Office exclusive competence to supervise AI systems built on a GPAI model by the same provider [@europeanparliament2026regulation]. ::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Does AI regulation hold back innovation?**

One position holds that the EU's regulatory approach is a drag on its technology sector. The 2024 report on European competitiveness led by Mario Draghi noted that the EU had "around 100 tech-focused laws" and over 270 digital regulators, and cited the AI Act's extra requirements for models above a compute threshold as an example of a precautionary approach that larger, often non-EU firms can absorb more easily than start-ups [@draghi2024future]. The omnibus delays can be read as partial acceptance of this argument.

The opposing position, argued by the legal scholar Anu Bradford, is that the US–EU gap in technology firms is better explained by other factors, such as capital markets, bankruptcy law, the fragmented single market and immigration policy, than by digital regulation itself; she calls the trade-off a "false choice" [@bradford2024false]. Supporters of regulation add that clear rules can increase trust and adoption.

Neither side yet has decisive evidence for AI specifically, because the AI Act's main obligations have only just begun to apply. Comparative data on investment, firm entry and product launches in the EU before and after 2026–2028, set against similar markets without such rules, would help settle the question.
:::

## 13.3 The United States: executive action, states and pre-emption

As of October 2026, the United States had no comprehensive federal AI statute. Federal policy has been set mainly by presidential executive orders, which bind federal agencies but can be revoked by the next president. The contrast between two administrations shows how quickly this can happen.

Executive Order 14110, signed by President Biden on 30 October 2023, required developers of the largest models to report to the government on training and safety testing. It set the reporting trigger at models trained with more than 10^26 operations [@biden2023safe]. On 20 January 2025, President Trump revoked it [@trump2025initial]. Executive Order 14179, signed three days later, declared it US policy "to sustain and enhance America's global AI dominance" and ordered a review of all actions taken under the revoked order [@trump2025removing]. The resulting *America's AI Action Plan*, published in July 2025, organised policy around three pillars (innovation, infrastructure, and international diplomacy and security) and proposed that federal funding should not flow to states with "burdensome" AI rules [@whitehouse2025winning].

The question of state laws became central. In July 2025 the Senate voted 99 to 1 to remove from a budget bill a proposed ten-year moratorium on the enforcement of state AI laws [@knapp2026hidden]. On 11 December 2025, Executive Order 14365 took a different route. It directed the Attorney General to set up an **AI Litigation Task Force** to challenge state AI laws in court. It also asked the Commerce Department to identify "onerous" state laws, tied some federal broadband funds to states not enforcing such laws, and called for a legislative proposal for a national framework that would pre-empt conflicting state laws while leaving child-safety rules untouched [@trump2025ensuring]. **Pre-emption** is the legal doctrine under which valid federal law overrides conflicting state law. The same order told the Federal Communications Commission to consider a federal reporting and disclosure standard for AI models that would override conflicting state rules, and asked the Federal Trade Commission to explain when state laws requiring changes to "truthful outputs" of AI models are pre-empted by federal consumer-protection law [@trump2025ensuring]. Because an executive order does not by itself override state statutes, the order relied on litigation and funding conditions in the meantime and called on Congress to legislate [@trump2025ensuring]. A federal bill along these lines, the FRONTIER Act, was introduced in July 2026; as of early October 2026 it had not been enacted [@knapp2026hidden].

Federal action in 2026 continued to favour voluntary arrangements. Executive Order 14409 of 2 June 2026 asked security agencies to develop a classified benchmark of AI models' cyber capabilities. It also asked them to design a *voluntary* framework under which developers could give the government access to such "covered frontier models" for up to 30 days before release. The order stated explicitly that it did not create any licensing or pre-clearance requirement [@trump2026promoting]. An order of 29 September 2026 directed federal agencies to use the term "Super Intelligence" in place of "artificial intelligence" in non-statutory documents; it changed no legal obligations [@trump2026inaugurating].

With little federal legislation, the states have been the main source of binding AI rules. California's Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act (SB 53) was signed on 29 September 2025 and took effect on 1 January 2026 [@californiagovernor2025governor]. It applies to developers that have trained a model with more than 10^26 operations, with heavier duties for "large" developers with annual revenue above $500 million. These must publish a frontier AI safety framework and transparency reports, report critical safety incidents to the state's Office of Emergency Services (generally within 15 days), and protect whistleblowers; civil penalties reach $1 million per violation [@metr2026frontier]. New York's RAISE Act, amended in March 2026 to align more closely with SB 53, is due to take effect on 1 January 2027, according to a summary by the research organisation METR [@metr2026frontier].

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: Colorado's AI Act, from statute to stand-off**

In 2024 Colorado passed SB 24-205, the first broad US state law on "high-risk" AI systems used in consequential decisions such as hiring, lending and housing. After a delay, it was due to take effect on 30 June 2026 [@usdoj2026complaint]. Executive Order 14365 singled it out, arguing that its ban on "algorithmic discrimination" might force models "to produce false results" [@trump2025ensuring].

In April 2026, xAI sued Colorado's Attorney General, and on 24 April the US Department of Justice joined the case as a plaintiff, arguing among other things that the law violated the constitutional guarantee of equal protection [@usdoj2026complaint]. On 27 April 2026, a federal magistrate judge approved the parties' agreement: the state would not begin enforcement of SB 24-205, "or any legislation replacing or amending" it, until 14 days after the court rules on a future request for a preliminary injunction [@usdccolorado2026minute]. On 14 May 2026, the Governor signed SB 26-189, which repealed and replaced the 2024 law. The new law regulates "automated decision-making technology" through disclosure: notices to consumers, explanations after adverse decisions, and rights to correct data and request human review. Most duties apply from 1 January 2027, subject to rules from the Attorney General [@coloradogeneralassembly2026automated]. As of October 2026 the court case remained pending.

The case shows how US AI policy is now made: through state legislation, federal litigation and negotiated pauses, rather than a single federal statute.
:::

## 13.4 China and the United Kingdom

China has taken a different route again: rather than one horizontal law, a series of targeted rules issued by government agencies, led by the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC). The **Interim Measures for the Administration of Generative Artificial Intelligence Services**, issued by seven agencies on 10 July 2023, took effect on 15 August 2023. They apply to generative AI services offered to the public in mainland China. Providers must uphold "socialist core values" and prevent prohibited content, use lawfully sourced training data, protect personal information, and label generated images and video. Services with "public opinion attributes or social mobilisation capacity" must undergo a security assessment and file their algorithms with the regulator [@cac2023interim].

China followed with rules on specific issues. The **Measures for Labelling AI-Generated and Synthetic Content**, issued by four agencies in March 2025, took effect on 1 September 2025. They require both explicit labels that users can see or hear (text, sound or graphics) and implicit labels embedded in file metadata identifying the content as AI-generated and naming the provider [@cac2025measures]. In October 2025, the national legislature amended the Cybersecurity Law to add an article on the "safe and sound development of AI", in force from 1 January 2026 [@xinhua2025china]. In April 2026, five agencies issued interim measures on "anthropomorphic" AI services, which simulate human personalities for companionship or emotional support, effective from 15 July 2026; they require security assessments, algorithm filing and protections for minors and older users [@xinhua2026five]. This step-by-step approach lets regulators act quickly on a specific concern, while keeping close state control over the content that AI services produce.

The **United Kingdom** has so far kept to the sectoral approach of its 2023 white paper [@dsit2023proinnovation]. Its main AI-specific body, the AI Safety Institute, was renamed the AI Security Institute in February 2025 and refocused on risks with security implications, such as cyber-attacks, chemical and biological misuse, and crime, while stating that it would not focus on bias or free speech [@dsit2025tackling]. Although the governing Labour Party had pledged binding rules for the most powerful models, the King's Speech of 13 May 2026, which sets out the government's legislative programme, contained no AI bill [@iapp2026kings]. The UK's main AI decision of 2026 concerned copyright and is discussed in Section 13.6.

These four jurisdictions are not the only ones, but they illustrate the main options: a comprehensive risk-based statute (EU), executive orders plus state legislation (US), targeted agency rules with strong content control (China), and principles applied by existing regulators (UK). The work of AI safety institutes is covered in Chapter 12, and export controls on chips in Chapter 7.

## 13.5 International coordination

AI models cross borders easily, which creates a case for international rules. Yet states disagree about values, compete for economic and military advantage, and have few institutions with authority over AI. Figure 13.2 summarises the main international and national milestones from 2023 to 2026.

![Figure 13.2: Selected milestones in AI governance, 2023–2026, by jurisdiction. National and regional laws (upper lanes) developed alongside non-binding international declarations and one binding treaty (lower lanes). Source: @ukgov2023bletchley; @dsit2024frontier; @elysee2025statement; @pib2026india; @councilofeurope2024framework; @un2025terms; @europeanparliament2024regulation; @europeanparliament2026regulation; @biden2023safe; @trump2025ensuring; @cac2025measures; @californiagovernor2025governor.](figures/fig-13-02.svg){#fig:13-02}

A series of summits began at Bletchley Park in the UK on 1–2 November 2023. Twenty-eight countries and the EU, including the US and China, signed the **Bletchley Declaration**, which recognised the potential for "serious, even catastrophic, harm" from the most capable frontier models [@ukgov2023bletchley]. At the follow-up summit in Seoul in May 2024, 16 companies signed the **Frontier AI Safety Commitments**. They promised to publish safety frameworks setting thresholds at which risks would be "intolerable" and, in the extreme, not to develop or deploy a model if risks could not be kept below them; four more companies joined later [@dsit2024frontier]. The summit in Paris on 10–11 February 2025 was renamed the AI Action Summit and shifted emphasis towards inclusion, sustainability and economic opportunity. Its closing statement was signed by 63 countries plus the EU and the African Union Commission (as listed in October 2026), but not by the United States or the United Kingdom [@elysee2025statement]. India hosted the AI Impact Summit in New Delhi on 16–20 February 2026, the first in the series held in the Global South, organised around "people, planet and progress" [@pib2026india]. The Paris statement's priorities included narrowing digital divides, avoiding market concentration, considering AI's effects on work and its environmental footprint, and strengthening international cooperation [@elysee2025statement]. The shift in titles, from "safety" to "action" to "impact", tracks a shift in political priorities.

What did the summits achieve? None of their outcomes is legally binding, and none creates a body that can check compliance. Their main effects have been indirect. They put frontier-model risk on the agenda of heads of government. They produced company commitments resembling duties later written into binding rules (the Seoul commitments to publish safety frameworks anticipate similar requirements in SB 53 and the EU Code of Practice), and they created regular occasions for governments that otherwise rarely cooperate to meet. The same features leave them exposed to changes in national politics: the United States and the United Kingdom, both signatories at Bletchley, did not sign the Paris statement [@ukgov2023bletchley; @elysee2025statement].

The only binding international treaty specifically on AI is the Council of Europe's **Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law**. It was opened for signature on 5 September 2024, when the UK signed [@moj2024uk]; its signatories also include the United States and the EU [@councilofeurope2024framework]. It requires parties to ensure that activities across the lifecycle of AI systems are consistent with human rights, democracy and the rule of law. It enters into force three months after five states, including at least three Council of Europe members, have ratified it [@councilofeurope2024framework]. Signature alone does not bind a state to apply a treaty; ratification does. Whether enough ratifications had been deposited by October 2026 could not be confirmed for this chapter, and readers should check the Council of Europe treaty office.

The United Nations has acted through the General Assembly. Resolution 78/265 of 21 March 2024 called for "safe, secure and trustworthy" AI for sustainable development [@un2024seizing]. Resolution 79/325 of 26 August 2025 established two bodies. The first is an **Independent International Scientific Panel on AI** of 40 experts serving three-year terms, to produce evidence-based assessments. The second is a **Global Dialogue on AI Governance**, meeting first in Geneva in 2026 alongside the International Telecommunication Union's AI for Good summit, and then in New York in 2027. Both are limited to the non-military domain [@un2025terms]. The model is loosely that of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change: shared scientific assessment as a basis for later political agreement. The analogy is imperfect because AI capabilities change far faster than the climate system, and much of the relevant evidence is held by private companies.

Two further forums matter. The **OECD AI Principles**, adopted in 2019 and updated in May 2024, have 47 adherents and set out values such as human rights, transparency, robustness and accountability [@oecd2024principles]. The G7's **Hiroshima AI Process** produced International Guiding Principles and a Code of Conduct for organisations developing advanced AI systems, endorsed by G7 leaders on 30 October 2023 [@mic2023hiroshima]. Since February 2025 the OECD has run a voluntary reporting framework against the Code; a first report was based on 20 organisations' submissions [@oecd2025hiroshima].

Finally, **technical standards** turn principles into checklists that auditors can verify. ISO/IEC 42001, published in 2023, specifies requirements for an AI management system: the policies, roles and processes an organisation uses to develop or use AI responsibly [@iso2023information]. Organisations can be certified against it, much as they are against quality-management or information-security standards. Under the EU AI Act, compliance with "harmonised" European standards will give a presumption of conformity, but their late arrival was one of the reasons given for the omnibus delays [@councileu2026proposal].

## 13.6 Copyright, training data and liability

Large models are trained on vast collections of text, images and music, much of it protected by copyright. Two legal questions arise. The **input question** asks whether copying works to train a model infringes copyright. The **output question** asks whether a model, or what it generates, reproduces protected works. By October 2026, courts in the US, the UK and Germany had given partial and sometimes conflicting answers.

In the United States, the key defence is **fair use**, which allows some unlicensed use of copyrighted works. Courts weigh four factors: the purpose and character of the use (including whether it is "transformative", meaning it adds a new purpose), the nature of the work, the amount used, and the effect on the market for the original. Three rulings set out the current range of views.

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: Bartz v. Anthropic**

Authors sued Anthropic, alleging that it had trained its Claude models on their books without permission. On 23 June 2025, Judge William Alsup ruled that using books to train the models was "exceedingly transformative" and a fair use, as was scanning print books that the company had bought. He did not excuse the company's downloading of millions of books from pirate websites to build a permanent library, which went towards trial [@usdcnortherncal2025bartz]. The parties then settled for $1.5 billion. On 20 July 2026, Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín, who had taken over the case, granted final approval: the settlement covered more than 480,000 works, at about $3,100 per work, and required Anthropic to destroy the pirated datasets [@bloomberglaw2026anthropic]. According to the settlement website, the first payments were expected by 15 November 2026 [@anthropicsettlement2026]. The settlement sets no legal precedent, but it shows that how training data is acquired can matter as much as how it is used.
:::

Two days after the Bartz ruling, Judge Vince Chhabria granted summary judgment to Meta in *Kadrey v. Meta*, but only because the authors had not presented evidence that Meta's models harmed the market for their books. He warned that copying protected works to train generative models without permission would, "in most cases", likely be illegal, because such models could flood the market with competing works, a harm he called "market dilution" [@usdcnortherncal2025kadrey]. On 29 September 2026, the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit held in *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence* that a legal-research start-up's use of Westlaw's case summaries to train its AI was not fair use, because it served the same purpose as the original and aimed to compete with it. The court stressed that Ross's system was not generative and distinguished the generative-AI cases [@thirdcircuit2026thomson]. It also noted that the US Department of Justice had filed a statement on 1 September 2026 in the consolidated cases against OpenAI in New York, which include the *New York Times* claims, arguing that training a large language model is transformative; those cases remained pending [@thirdcircuit2026thomson]. The US Copyright Office's analysis of generative AI training, released in pre-publication form in May 2025, had not been finalised by October 2026 [@uscopyrightoffice2025copyright].

In the UK, Getty Images sued Stability AI over the image generator Stable Diffusion. Getty dropped its main training claim, since the training had taken place outside the UK. On 4 November 2025 the High Court rejected its remaining copyright claim, holding that the model was not an "infringing copy" of Getty's photographs, while finding limited trade-mark infringement involving Getty watermarks in some outputs [@highcourt2025getty]. The judge later gave Getty permission to appeal on the "infringing copy" point, calling it a question of law "on which the minds of reasonable lawyers may differ" [@fairhurst2026permission]; no appeal decision had been reported by October 2026. In March 2026, the UK government reported to Parliament that its earlier preferred option, a broad exception allowing training unless rights holders opted out, was "no longer the government's preferred way forward", after most consultation respondents rejected it; it proposed to gather more evidence [@dsit2026report].

In Germany, the Munich Regional Court ruled on 11 November 2025 in a case brought by GEMA, the music-rights society, that OpenAI had infringed copyright because its models memorised song lyrics and reproduced them in response to simple prompts. According to GEMA's account of the judgment, the court held that OpenAI needed licences both to train and to operate ChatGPT with these lyrics, and that the EU's text-and-data-mining exception did not justify storing or outputting them, since "OpenAI is not a privileged research organisation" [@gema2025landmark]. Unlike the US rulings, this decision turned on memorisation: the court treated the model as containing copies of the works, a point on which the UK High Court, applying different law to different facts, reached a different conclusion for Stable Diffusion. This was a first-instance judgment and is open to appeal. Under the EU AI Act, GPAI providers must in any case respect rights holders' machine-readable opt-outs and publish summaries of their training content [@europeanparliament2024regulation].

**Liability** for harm caused by AI raises related questions. When an AI system gives dangerous advice or makes a discriminatory decision, it may be unclear whether the developer, the deploying company or the user is responsible. The EU's revised Product Liability Directive, which member states must apply to products placed on the market from 9 December 2026, explicitly covers all software, including AI systems, and can hold manufacturers liable for defects that arise through updates or machine learning after release [@europeancommission2024liability]. In the US, liability is developing through state statutes (SB 53's civil penalties; Colorado's treatment of violations as deceptive trade practices) and through individual lawsuits.

## 13.7 Compute thresholds and frontier models

Regulators need a way to decide which models deserve extra scrutiny before anyone knows exactly what those models can do. The most common answer has been a **compute threshold**: a legal trigger based on the amount of computation used in training, measured in floating-point operations (FLOP). The EU uses 10^25 FLOP as the presumption of systemic risk; California's SB 53 and New York's RAISE Act use 10^26; the revoked US Executive Order 14110 also used 10^26 [@europeanparliament2024regulation; @metr2026frontier; @biden2023safe]. Chapter 4 explains why training compute has grown so quickly.

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: What does 10^25 FLOP mean?**

10^25 is a 1 followed by 25 zeros, or ten million billion billion operations. A rough estimate of training compute is 6 × (number of model parameters) × (number of training tokens), because each token requires about six arithmetic operations per parameter across the forward and backward passes (Chapter 2). A model with 100 billion parameters (10^11) trained on 15 trillion tokens (1.5 × 10^13) would use about 6 × 10^11 × 1.5 × 10^13 ≈ 9 × 10^24 FLOP, just under the EU threshold. Because the threshold is fixed while training budgets grow, more models cross it each year. Epoch AI counted 33 publicly disclosed models trained with at least 10^25 FLOP by June 2025, from 12 developers [@rahman2025over].
:::

The case for compute thresholds is practical. Sastry and colleagues argue that compute is an unusually good lever for governance because it is "detectable, excludable, and quantifiable", and because the chips it relies on come from a highly concentrated supply chain [@sastry2024computing]. Heim and Koessler argue that training compute is the best available metric for *identifying* models that warrant oversight: it correlates with capabilities, can be measured early, and can be verified by outsiders. They also stress that it is an imperfect proxy for risk and should serve only as an initial filter that triggers evaluations, not as a measure of danger in itself [@heim2024training].

Critics question even that role. Hooker argues that the relationship between compute and risk is "highly uncertain and rapidly changing": smaller models trained with better data and methods can match larger ones, so a fixed threshold may both catch harmless models and miss dangerous ones [@hooker2024limitations]. The EU addressed this by letting the Commission designate models as systemic-risk on other grounds, such as capabilities or number of users [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. The US government's 2026 cyber-capability framework took a different approach, defining "covered frontier models" by results on a classified capability benchmark rather than by compute [@trump2026promoting].

The resulting rules on each side of the Atlantic are more alike than their political framing suggests. Both the EU's systemic-risk duties and California's SB 53 require the largest developers to assess catastrophic or systemic risks, document their safety measures, and report serious incidents to a public authority [@europeanparliament2024regulation; @metr2026frontier]. The main differences lie in enforcement (an EU-wide authority with turnover-based fines against a state attorney general with fixed penalties) and in reach (any provider placing a model on the EU market against developers subject to California law).

The rules triggered by these thresholds are, so far, mostly about **transparency and process**: publishing safety frameworks, testing models, reporting incidents and protecting whistleblowers. None of the jurisdictions examined here requires a licence to train or release a frontier model, and the 2026 US order explicitly rules one out [@trump2026promoting]. Whether disclosure duties of this kind are enough, or whether pre-approval will eventually be needed for the most capable systems, is one of the central open questions of AI governance, and it links directly to the safety methods discussed in Chapter 12 and the scenarios in Chapter 14.

## Summary
- AI governance combines hard law, soft law, technical standards and liability; jurisdictions differ mainly in how they mix these instruments.
- The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is a horizontal, risk-based law: prohibitions since February 2025, general-purpose AI duties since August 2025, and high-risk obligations deferred by the July 2026 Digital Omnibus to December 2027 (Annex III) and August 2028 (Annex I).
- The US has no comprehensive federal AI statute. Executive orders since 2025 favour deregulation and voluntary arrangements, while a December 2025 order targets state laws through litigation and funding conditions; federal pre-emption legislation was pending in October 2026.
- US states, notably California (SB 53, in force since January 2026) and New York (RAISE Act, due January 2027), regulate frontier developers mainly through transparency and incident reporting; Colorado replaced its broad AI Act with a narrower disclosure law amid federal litigation.
- China regulates through targeted agency rules on generative AI, labelling and anthropomorphic services, with strong content controls; the UK relies on existing regulators and had no AI bill as of October 2026.
- International coordination rests mainly on non-binding declarations, UN scientific and dialogue bodies, OECD and G7 principles, and one Council of Europe treaty.
- Courts are split on copyright and training: two US district courts found training on books to be fair use in 2025, the Third Circuit rejected fair use for a non-generative competitor in 2026, and German and UK courts have reached their own, partly contrary, conclusions; several key cases remain pending.
- Compute thresholds (10^25 or 10^26 FLOP) are a practical but imperfect trigger for frontier-model rules, and are increasingly supplemented by capability-based tests.
