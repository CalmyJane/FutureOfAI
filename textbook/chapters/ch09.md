# Chapter 9: Information, Media and Democracy

On 21 January 2024, two days before the New Hampshire presidential primary in the United States, thousands of voters received a telephone call in a voice that sounded like President Joe Biden. The voice had been cloned with generative AI, and the message urged recipients to "save your vote for the November election" instead of voting in the primary. The US Federal Communications Commission (FCC) traced the calls to a political consultant, Steve Kramer, and proposed a $6 million fine against him for unlawfully spoofing caller identification [@fcc2024proposes]. It confirmed the fine in a forfeiture order adopted on 26 September 2024 [@fcc2024issues]. In a separate state criminal trial, a New Hampshire jury acquitted him in June 2025 of all voter-suppression and impersonation charges [@associatedpress2025new].

The episode compresses the main questions about AI and the information ecosystem into one case. The fake was cheap: researchers who cloned voices for a 2025 study used a commercial service that offered "instant voice cloning for as little as $5 a month" [@barrington2025people]. Listeners had no reliable way to tell it from the real voice. Its effect on turnout is unknown, because nobody can count the people who stayed at home. And the institutional response came from a telecoms regulator applying the Truth in Caller ID Act [@fcc2024issues], not from any rule about synthetic media.

## 9.1 How synthetic media is made, and what cheapness changes

**Synthetic media** is any image, audio, video or text generated or substantially altered by AI. A **deepfake** is synthetic media that realistically depicts a real person, place or event in a way that could be mistaken for authentic. The EU's AI Act defines a deep fake as AI-generated or manipulated image, audio or video content that resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities or events and "would falsely appear to a person to be authentic or truthful" [@europeanparliament2024regulation, Article 3(60)]. A **cheap fake** is a misleading edit made with ordinary tools, such as slowing down a video or cutting a quotation out of context. Researchers also distinguish **misinformation** (false or misleading content shared without intent to deceive) from **disinformation** (false content spread deliberately), and both from **influence operations**, coordinated and often covert efforts, frequently by states, to shape opinion through networks of fake accounts.

### Images and video: diffusion models

Many current image generators are **diffusion models**. The method, set out by Ho, Jain and Abbeel in 2020, has two halves [@ho2020denoising]. In training, a real image is gradually destroyed by adding small amounts of random (Gaussian) noise over many steps; in their model there were *T* = 1,000 steps, with the variance of the noise added at each step rising linearly from 0.0001 to 0.02. After the last step nothing of the original image remains. A neural network is trained on millions of such corrupted images to solve one narrow task: given a noisy image and the step number, predict the noise that was added. Generation runs the process backwards. The model starts from pure noise and, step by step, subtracts its own estimate of the noise, until an image emerges that looks as if it came from the training distribution.

Two further ideas made text-to-image generation practical. Rombach and colleagues ran the diffusion process not on the pixels themselves but on a compressed "latent" representation produced by a separate autoencoder, which cut the computing cost of training and generation substantially. They also added cross-attention layers (the mechanism described in Chapter 3) so that a text prompt could steer each denoising step [@rombach2022high]. This **latent diffusion** design made high-resolution text-to-image generation affordable outside the largest laboratories.

### Voices: speech as a language-modelling problem

Voice cloning followed a parallel path. Microsoft's VALL-E, described in a 2023 preprint, converts speech into sequences of discrete codes using a neural audio codec, then trains a language model to predict those codes much as a text model predicts words. Trained on 60,000 hours of English speech, it could imitate an unseen speaker from "only a 3-second enrolled recording" used as a prompt, and it preserved the speaker's emotional tone and acoustic surroundings [@wang2023neural]. Commercial services had turned such methods into consumer products by 2024. In one study, Barrington, Cooper and Farid built clones of more than 200 speakers with the ElevenLabs "Instant Voice Cloning" service, using audio from only the first two recorded prompts of each speaker [@barrington2025people].

### What cheapness changes, and what it does not

Generative AI therefore mainly changes one part of the information system: the cost of producing plausible content. In a 2023 preprint, Goldstein and colleagues argued that language models could make influence operations cheaper, larger and harder to attribute. They located four points where intervention is possible: how models are built, who can access them, how content is disseminated and how audiences form beliefs [@goldstein2023generative]. The evidence on the third and fourth links tempers the concern about the first two. Meta reported in December 2024 that the covert influence networks it had taken down made "only incremental productivity and content-generation gains" from generative AI, according to the company [@clegg2024what]. The 2026 International AI Safety Report, written by an independent expert panel, found that AI-generated content can be as persuasive as human-written content in experiments, but that real-world use for political manipulation "is documented but not yet widespread" [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.1.1].

The reason, according to a strand of research on misinformation, is that production was never the binding constraint. In an essay for the Knight First Amendment Institute, Kapoor and Narayanan (2024) argue that AI lowers the cost of creating false content but not the cost of distributing it, and that misinformation spreads mainly because of demand: people seek out claims that fit what they already believe [@kapoor2024looked]. Altay, Berriche and Acerbi (2023) reach a similar conclusion from the research literature. People do not believe everything they see online, and misinformation largely "preaches to the choir", reaching those who already agree [@altay2023misinformation]. Two developments complicate this picture. AI can now hold a conversation, adapting to each person's objections, which static content cannot (Section 9.5). And generative models are being built into the distribution channels themselves, from search pages to social feeds (Section 9.7).

## 9.2 Detection: why people and machines struggle

### Human judgement

The laboratory evidence on human detection is consistent. In a pre-registered experiment with 210 participants, Köbis, Doležalová and Soraperra (2021) found that people could not reliably tell deepfake videos from real ones; neither raising awareness nor paying for correct answers improved accuracy, and participants overestimated their own ability [@kobis2021fooled]. Nightingale and Farid (2022) showed participants a mix of real photographs of faces and faces generated by StyleGAN2, an image generator released in 2019. In the first experiment (315 participants), accuracy was 48.2%, no better than a coin toss. Training and trial-by-trial feedback raised it only to 59.0% in a second experiment. In a third, synthetic faces were rated 7.7% more trustworthy than real ones [@nightingale2022ai].

Voices are no easier. Barrington and colleagues ran perceptual studies with 604 listeners. Participants judged a cloned voice to belong to the same person as the real recording "approximately 80% of the time", and correctly identified a voice as AI-generated "only about 60% of the time" [@barrington2025people]. For video, Groh and colleagues (2022) asked 15,016 participants in two online studies to sort authentic videos from deepfakes. Ordinary viewers were about as accurate as the leading computer-vision detector, although they made different kinds of mistakes. Showing participants the model's prediction improved average accuracy, but when the model was wrong it often led people to change a correct answer to an incorrect one [@groh2022deepfake].

### Automated detectors

Automated detectors are classifiers trained on examples of real and generated media to pick up statistical traces that generators leave behind. On academic benchmarks they often score well. Performance is usually reported as the **AUC** (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve): the probability that the detector gives a randomly chosen fake a higher "fake" score than a randomly chosen genuine item. An AUC of 1.0 is perfect; 0.5 is guessing.

The problem is **distribution shift**: a detector learns the fingerprints of the generators in its training data, and new generators leave different ones. A 2025 preprint by Chandra and colleagues at the non-profit TrueMedia.org tested this directly. They assembled Deepfake-Eval-2024 from deepfakes circulating in 2024, collected from social media and from users of TrueMedia.org's free detection service, among them journalists: 45 hours of video, 56.5 hours of audio and 1,975 images from 88 websites in 52 languages. Compared with their scores on older academic benchmarks, the AUC of open-source detectors fell by 50% for video, 48% for audio and 45% for images. The best open-source model in any modality reached an AUC of 0.58, and many were close to 0.5. Commercial detectors from seven vendors did better, but none reached 90% accuracy, and none matched trained human forensic analysts [@chandra2025deepfake].

Two further obstacles are structural rather than a matter of better engineering. First, detection is an adversarial game. Saberi and colleagues proved, for classifier-based deepfake detectors, a trade-off between robustness (resisting small deliberate perturbations) and reliability (few errors on unperturbed content), and demonstrated it experimentally [@saberi2024robustness]. A generator's developer, or anyone with access to a detector, can test outputs against it and adjust them until they pass. Second, base rates are unforgiving. Consider an illustrative detector that flags 99% of fakes and wrongly flags only 1% of genuine items. Applied to a platform's stream of one million genuine videos and one thousand fakes, it would catch 990 fakes but also flag 10,000 genuine videos, so that more than nine in ten alarms would be false. Detection of AI-written *text* faces the same problems in sharper form: a test of 14 tools found them "neither accurate nor reliable" [@weberwulff2023testing], and Chapter 10 describes their bias against non-native English writers.

## 9.3 Watermarks and provenance: labelling at the source

If synthetic content cannot reliably be recognised after the fact, an alternative is to mark it when it is made. **Watermarking** hides a signal in AI-generated content at the moment of creation, so that software holding the right key can later recognise it. **Content provenance** attaches a cryptographically signed record of where content came from and how it was edited. The two answer different questions, and they fail in different ways (Table 9.1).

### Text watermarks: the green-list scheme and SynthID

The best-studied text watermark was proposed by Kirchenbauer and colleagues in 2023 [@kirchenbauer2023watermark]. Its logic is statistical, and the Deeper box below works through it with the paper's own numbers.

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: How a text watermark is embedded and detected**

A language model writes by repeatedly choosing the next token (a word or word fragment). In the scheme of Kirchenbauer and colleagues, before each choice a secret key and the preceding token are fed into a hash function, which splits the vocabulary pseudo-randomly into a "green list" containing a fraction γ (gamma) of all tokens and a "red list" containing the rest. The model then adds a bonus δ (delta) to the scores of green tokens before sampling. Where many words would fit, this tilts the choice towards green ones; where one word is almost certain, such as the second half of a name, the bonus barely matters and quality is preserved.

A detector that knows the key recomputes the green list at every position and counts green tokens. Unwatermarked text should contain about γ*T* green tokens in a passage of *T* tokens, with a standard deviation of √(*T*γ(1 − γ)). The test statistic *z* is the excess count divided by that standard deviation. With γ = 0.5 and *T* = 200, chance predicts 100 green tokens with a standard deviation of about 7.1. The authors flag text as watermarked when *z* exceeds 4, which happens at about 128 green tokens and corresponds to a false-positive probability of 3 in 100,000. In their tests with δ = 2, watermarked 200-token passages contained 159.5 green tokens on average, far above the threshold, and 98.4% of them were detected [@kirchenbauer2023watermark]. The failures were mostly low-entropy passages, such as memorised text, where the model had little freedom to choose green words.
:::

Google DeepMind's SynthID-Text, published in *Nature* in 2024, uses a different embedding method called tournament sampling. The model proposes many candidate tokens; these compete in a knockout tournament of up to 30 rounds, in each of which a pseudo-random function keyed to the four preceding tokens scores the candidates, and the winner is emitted. Detection again needs the key but not the model [@dathathri2024scalable]. Google tested it in a live experiment on nearly 20 million Gemini chatbot responses: the rates at which users gave watermarked and unwatermarked answers a thumbs-up or thumbs-down differed by 0.01 and 0.02 percentage points, within statistical noise. The authors released the code openly and listed the limits themselves: watermarks are weakened by paraphrasing, they work less well in low-entropy text, and they require "coordination between actors running the LLM text generation services" [@dathathri2024scalable]. Google also applies SynthID to images and video; a 2025 preprint by its researchers states that SynthID-Image had marked "over ten billion images and video frames", with the verification service available only to "trusted testers" [@gowal2025synthidimage].

### How robust are watermarks?

Robustness is the central technical weakness. Kirchenbauer and colleagues themselves tested an attack in which a second, smaller language model (T5-Large) replaced randomly chosen words in watermarked 200-token passages. Replacing 10% of tokens left detection almost intact; replacing 30% or more degraded it markedly, but also degraded the text, as Figure 9.1 shows [@kirchenbauer2023watermark]. Watermarks are therefore hard to remove by light editing, and an attacker who rewrites heavily pays in quality, unless the attacker has another strong model at hand, in which case the watermarked one was unnecessary.

Later work found more economical attacks. Zhang and colleagues proved that, under natural assumptions, no watermark can be "strong", meaning impossible to remove without degrading quality. An attacker with a way to judge quality and a way to make small random changes can always walk the text away from the watermark while keeping it good [@zhang2024watermarks]. Jovanović, Staab and Vechev showed that an attacker can partly reverse-engineer a green-list-style watermark by querying the model's public interface and studying its outputs. With that knowledge the attacker can both strip the watermark from text ("scrubbing") and stamp it onto human-written text ("spoofing"). For "under $50", they reached average success rates above 80% against schemes previously considered safe [@jovanovic2024watermark]. Image watermarks face analogous limits. Saberi and colleagues showed that "diffusion purification", adding noise to a watermarked image and letting a diffusion model clean it up, removes watermarks that make only subtle changes to an image, while stronger watermarks can be defeated by an attack on a substitute model. Spoofing works here too: a watermarked noise pattern can be added to real photographs so that they are wrongly flagged as AI-generated [@saberi2024robustness].

Two further limits are not technical. A watermark exists only if the generator adds it, and open-weight models (Chapter 7) can be run without one. And the detector key is a security asset: if it is widely shared, attackers can test their evasion against it; if it is held tightly, as with SynthID-Image, outside verification depends on the company.

### Content Credentials: how C2PA provenance works

Provenance starts from the opposite assumption: instead of trying to prove that content is fake, it lets honest sources prove where their content came from. The main standard is maintained by the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA), founded in February 2021 by Adobe, Arm, the BBC, Intel, Microsoft and Truepic [@microsoft2021technology], and marketed to the public as "Content Credentials".

The technical design, as of specification version 2.2 (2025), works as follows [@c2pa2025c2pa]. Each file carries a **manifest store**, usually embedded in the file in a container format called JUMBF, or held externally and referenced. A **manifest** holds three things. The first is a set of **assertions**: labelled statements about the content. The mandatory `c2pa.actions` assertion records what was done to the file, starting with a "created" action. For AI output, its `digitalSourceType` field takes the value `trainedAlgorithmicMedia`, a term from the news industry's IPTC vocabulary. Other assertions list "ingredients", the earlier files from which this one was composed, whose own manifests are copied in. The second is the **claim**, a data structure that references every assertion by its cryptographic hash. The third is the **claim signature**: the claim is signed with the private key behind an X.509 certificate, the same public-key technology that secures web browsing, in a standard format called COSE. A trusted time-stamp from an external authority (RFC 3161) lets a signature remain verifiable after the signing certificate expires. Each editing step adds a new manifest; the most recent one, the "active manifest", is the one validated against the file.

The manifest is tied to the content by a **hard binding**: one or more cryptographic hashes of the file's bytes, so that changing a single pixel breaks the match. Because metadata is easily stripped, for instance when a platform re-encodes an upload, the standard also allows **soft bindings**: a watermark or perceptual fingerprint that survives re-encoding and can be used to look up a copy of the manifest in a cloud repository. Soft bindings, the specification insists, "shall not be used as a hard binding". Signers are identified by their certificate, normally an organisation or a device's software, not a person: version 2.0 removed identified humans from the assertion metadata, a choice relevant to photographers and witnesses whose safety depends on anonymity. And the coalition states plainly that provenance "cannot tell you whether the digital content is true, accurate or factual" [@c2pa2025c2pa]. Figure 9.2 shows the pipeline.

![Figure 9.2: A simplified content provenance pipeline. Adoption has moved from software to hardware. In September 2025 Google announced that the Pixel 10 phones attach Content Credentials to every JPEG photograph taken with the built-in camera app, not only to AI-edited images, and that the app had achieved "Assurance Level 2", the highest security rating then defined by the C2PA's conformance programme, using keys protected by the phone's security chip. The company gave an explicit reason for signing everything. If only synthetic content is labelled, users come to believe that unlabelled content is authentic, which Google described, citing research, as "the implied truth effect" [@lynch2025how]. That effect is well documented for fact-check labels. In an experiment with 5,271 participants, Pennycook and colleagues found that attaching warnings to some false headlines made untagged false headlines seem more accurate. Marking some true headlines as verified removed the effect [@pennycook2020implied].

Provenance has its own limits. It depends on cameras, editing tools, platforms and browsers all reading and displaying the credentials. A signed record that a photograph came from a particular phone does not show that the scene was not staged. Most authentic content in circulation carries no credentials at all, so absence proves nothing. Provenance is therefore strongest for sources that choose to sign, such as news agencies and official bodies, and weakest at exposing fakes from those that do not.

## 9.4 Deepfakes and the 2024 election year

### What monitoring found

In 2024 voters went to the polls in the United States, India, Indonesia, Mexico, the United Kingdom, the European Union and many other places [@clegg2024what]. Many commentators predicted that generative AI would flood these campaigns with deceptive content. The evidence gathered since points towards a limited measurable impact on results, with important caveats about what can be measured.

The most systematic monitoring came from the Alan Turing Institute's Centre for Emerging Technology and Security (CETaS). Its September 2024 briefing paper on the UK general election, the French legislative elections and the European Parliament elections identified "just 16 confirmed viral cases" of AI-enabled disinformation or deepfakes during the UK election and "only 11" across the EU and French elections combined. It found "no evidence" that they "meaningfully impacted" any result [@stockwell2024threat]. The institute's November 2024 report on the US election likewise reported a "lack of evidence" of a measurable impact on the result. It attributed this "primarily" to "insufficient data" on real-world voter behaviour. It recorded "far higher – albeit still relatively moderate – volumes" of viral AI-enabled disinformation in the US campaign than in the earlier elections. It also warned that users' uncertainty about what was authentic had "damaged trust in the integrity of online sources" [@stockwell2024ai].

Platform data point the same way, with an obvious limitation. Meta, which owns Facebook and Instagram, reported that ratings of AI content about elections, politics and social topics made up "less than 1%" of all misinformation fact-checked by its partners during the major elections of 2024, according to the company [@clegg2024what]. That figure covers only content that fact-checkers chose to examine.

### Cheap fakes outnumbered deepfakes

Kapoor and Narayanan (2024) analysed 78 election-related uses of AI collected by WIRED's AI Elections Project. Half of them (39) showed no deceptive intent: they included parody and satire, and campaigns using AI openly, for example to translate a speech, which was the most common non-deceptive use. For each of the 39 deceptive cases, the authors estimated that similar content could have been produced without AI for no more than a few hundred dollars. They also cited a count by the News Literacy Project in which cheap fakes were used about seven times as often as AI-generated content in the US election [@kapoor2024looked]. The bottleneck for a would-be deceiver in 2024 was reach and credibility, not the technical ability to fabricate.

### The liar's dividend

A subtler effect works through authentic content. When the public knows that convincing fakes exist, a person caught on a genuine recording can claim that it was faked. Chesney and Citron (2019) called this the **liar's dividend** and noted, perversely, that it grows as public education about deepfakes succeeds, because a sceptical public is more willing to doubt real evidence [@chesney2019deep].

Schiff, Schiff and Bueno (2025) tested the idea in five survey experiments with more than 15,000 American adults. Participants read about real scandals involving politicians, followed by a hypothetical response in which the politician claimed the story was fake. The authors tested two mechanisms: "informational uncertainty", making people unsure what to believe, and "oppositional rallying", encouraging the politician's supporters to close ranks against the accusers. Both raised support for the politician across partisan groups, and both worked better than staying silent or apologising. The claims were effective against text-based reports but "largely ineffective against video evidence", and they did not reduce general trust in the media [@schiff2025liars]. The result confirms the existence of a liar's dividend while suggesting that, when the experiments were run, video still carried more evidential weight than text. As video generation improves, that is the margin most likely to erode. CETaS already reported, for the 2024 US campaign, that confusion over what was real, including genuine content wrongly dismissed as AI-generated, was damaging trust in online information [@stockwell2024ai].

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Were fears about AI and the 2024 elections overblown?**

*Largely, yes.* Simon, Altay and Mercier (2023) argued before the election year that supply of false content was never the main constraint on misinformation; attention and belief are [@simon2023misinformation]. The record of 2024 fits their view. The CETaS studies found a few dozen viral AI cases across four major elections and no measurable effect on outcomes [@stockwell2024threat; @stockwell2024ai]. AI content was under 1% of fact-checked misinformation on Meta's platforms [@clegg2024what]. Most deceptive uses could have been produced cheaply without AI, and cheap fakes outnumbered AI content by about seven to one [@kapoor2024looked].

*Not proven.* Every one of these sources measures visible content, not effects on behaviour. CETaS itself said the US finding reflected "insufficient data", and monitoring covered mainly the UK, France, the EU and the US. The cheapest harm, eroding confidence that any evidence is real, is the hardest to count, yet it is the one that survey experiments on the liar's dividend show can benefit politicians [@schiff2025liars]. Private conversations with chatbots, which experiments show can shift attitudes (Section 9.5), are invisible to monitoring of public posts.

*What evidence would decide it:* panel data linking individuals' exposure to AI-generated content, through platform data access, with later changes in their knowledge, trust and turnout, collected across several elections as tools improve.
:::

The most consequential interference of the period did not depend mainly on generative AI. The annulment of Romania's 2024 presidential election, discussed in Section 9.8, turned on the alleged coordinated amplification of content by platform accounts and undisclosed paid promotion.

## 9.5 AI as a persuader

### The baseline: persuasion is hard

Political scientists have long found that changing voters' minds during a general election is difficult. Kalla and Broockman (2018) combined a meta-analysis of 40 field experiments with nine new experiments of their own and concluded that the best estimate of the effect of campaign contact and advertising on Americans' candidate choices in general elections is zero [@kalla2018minimal]. Any claim that AI is unusually persuasive has to be read against that baseline.

### Static messages: as good as humans, little more

The first generation of studies examined static messages: a fixed text that every participant reads. Goldstein and colleagues (2024) gave 8,221 US respondents either nothing, a real article from a foreign covert propaganda campaign, or an article on the same thesis written by GPT-3. Among respondents who read no article, 24.4% agreed with the propaganda thesis. Reading the original propaganda raised agreement to 47.4%, and reading the GPT-3 version raised it to 43.5%. With an English speaker editing the prompts or selecting the outputs, GPT-3 articles were under some conditions as persuasive as the originals [@goldstein2024persuasive].

Two later findings undercut common fears about static messages. Hackenburg and Margetts (2024) built an application that fed each participant's self-reported demographic and political data into GPT-4 prompts in real time, producing individually tailored messages on four political issues. In a pre-registered experiment with 8,587 participants, these **microtargeted** messages moved opinion by 4.83 percentage points against 6.20 points for generic GPT-4 messages, a difference that was not statistically significant [@hackenburg2024evaluating]. Hackenburg and colleagues (2025) then generated 720 messages on ten US policy issues with 24 language models of very different sizes and tested them on 25,982 participants. Persuasiveness showed "sharply diminishing returns" to model size. Once the authors adjusted for basic task completion (staying coherent and on topic), the association between size and persuasiveness was no longer statistically significant [@hackenburg2025scaling]. For static text, frontier models appeared to be close to a ceiling.

### Conversation is different

The picture changed when AI was allowed to talk back. Costello, Pennycook and Rand (2024) asked 2,190 Americans who believed a conspiracy theory to describe it in their own words, together with the evidence they found convincing. Participants then had three rounds of conversation with GPT-4 Turbo, which was instructed to reduce their belief using evidence tailored to their stated reasons. Belief in the chosen conspiracy fell by about 20% on average, and the effect persisted undiminished for at least two months. A professional fact-checker rated 99.2% of a sample of 128 AI claims as true. The AI did not reduce belief in conspiracies that were in fact true, and the effect spilled over to reduce belief in unrelated conspiracy theories [@costello2024durably]. According to the authors' university, about one in four participants disavowed the conspiracy after the conversation, and the intervention was less successful among those for whom the conspiracy was central to their worldview [@cornell2024ai].

The same capacity can be pointed in any political direction. Hackenburg and colleagues (2025) ran three experiments with 76,977 UK adults, 19 language models and 707 political issues, and checked the accuracy of 466,769 claims the models made [@hackenburg2025levers]. Conversations were 41% (GPT-4o) to 52% (GPT-4.5) more persuasive than a static message from the same model, whose effects were 6.1 and 6.9 points on a 0–100 agreement scale. Between 36% and 42% of the immediate effect remained measurable a month later. Post-training a model specifically for persuasion raised persuasiveness by up to 51%, and prompting strategies by up to 27%; personalisation added on average 0.43 points and never more than one. The dominant mechanism was information density. Each additional fact-checkable claim was associated with about 0.3 points more persuasion, and the number of claims accounted for roughly half of the explainable variation. The most persuasive configurations were also the least accurate: in the estimated "maximal persuasion" condition, the effect was 15.9 points (26.1 among participants who initially disagreed) against a 9.4-point average, and 30.0% of fact-checkable claims were rated inaccurate against 16.0% on average [@hackenburg2025levers].

Lin and colleagues (2025) moved from policy issues to real elections. In pre-registered experiments before the 2024 US presidential election, the 2025 Canadian federal election and the 2025 Polish presidential election, participants were randomly assigned to converse with an AI that advocated for one of the two leading candidates. In the US (2,306 participants), the gap in candidate preference between the pro-Harris and pro-Trump conditions was 2.88 points on a 0–100 scale (95% confidence interval 2.02 to 3.74). In Canada (1,530 participants) it was 10.05 points and in Poland (2,118 participants) 9.94 points, effects the authors note are larger than those typically found for video advertisements. For a Massachusetts ballot measure on psychedelics, the gap among participants without extreme prior views was 22.73 points. Slightly more than a third of the US effect persisted after more than a month. In Canada and Poland, instructing the AI not to use facts and evidence cut its effect by more than half. Models advocating for right-leaning candidates made more inaccurate claims in all three countries, as rated by an AI system validated against professional fact-checkers and politically balanced lay panels [@lin2025persuading].

Salvi and colleagues (2025) compared AI directly with people. In short multi-round debates with 900 participants, GPT-4 given basic demographic information about its opponent was more persuasive than a human opponent in 64.4% of the pairs where the two differed, an 81.2% increase in the odds of higher post-debate agreement (95% CI 26.0% to 160.7%) [@salvi2025conversational]. ### External validity

Several features of these experiments limit what they imply for real elections. Participants were recruited online, paid and told they were talking to an AI. In the experiments of Hackenburg and colleagues, conversations lasted nine minutes on average [@hackenburg2025levers], and few voters choose to spend that long discussing politics with a chatbot. Outside experiments such use is still a minority habit: in the Reuters Institute's 2026 survey, 10% of respondents used AI chatbots for news each week [@egan2026digital]. Effects decayed, with roughly a third to two-fifths remaining after a month in the two largest studies, and an election campaign exposes voters to many competing messages. Lin and colleagues debriefed participants before the elections and randomised the direction of persuasion, so that any lasting effects would cancel out. They suggest that AI dialogue may matter more in state and local contests, where voters have weaker prior views, than in national ones [@lin2025persuading].

Against these limits stand two findings that make the experiments more, not less, concerning. The effects came mainly from presenting information, not from psychological tricks or personal data, which means they do not depend on any capability that providers could easily withhold. And persuasiveness and accuracy pulled in opposite directions in both large studies. A campaign optimising for votes would therefore be pushed towards the less accurate configurations, unless model developers or regulators constrain it.

## 9.6 Synthetic respondents: AI as a stand-in for the public

A different use of language models is to replace survey respondents. Argyle and colleagues (2023) conditioned GPT-3 on thousands of first-person "backstories" built from the demographic and political characteristics of real participants in the American National Election Studies (ANES) of 2012, 2016 and 2020 [@argyle2023out]. In the preprint version of the study, the model's predicted Republican vote share was 0.391 against 0.404 among ANES respondents in 2012, 0.432 against 0.477 in 2016 and 0.472 against 0.412 in 2020. The tetrachoric correlation between individual simulated and reported votes, a measure of agreement for yes/no variables, was 0.90, 0.92 and 0.94. The authors called this property **algorithmic fidelity**: with the right conditioning, a model reproduces the response patterns of many subgroups. They named the resulting synthetic respondents **silicon samples**; using them in place of human respondents has come to be called **silicon sampling**. The authors proposed such samples as a new tool for social-science research.

The later evidence found the limits. Bisbee and colleagues (2024) asked ChatGPT, adopting different personas, to rate 11 social and political groups. Average scores closely matched the ANES, but the synthetic answers varied far less than real ones, and the statistical relationships between variables often differed from those in the human data. Results changed with small changes to prompt wording, and the same prompt gave significantly different results over three months, a serious problem for reproducibility [@bisbee2024synthetic].

The most demanding test so far came from the Pew Research Center in September 2026 [@pew2026well]. Pew built a "digital twin" for each member of its American Trends Panel who had completed its 2025 political typology survey, giving the model the person's demographics and earlier answers. It then had the twins answer three 2026 survey waves, question by question, under the same instructions as the human panellists. The best-performing model, Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.6, missed the human results by 12.4 percentage points on average: 11.3 points in January, 14.7 in March and 11.2 in April. Errors were 16.1 points for Republicans and Republican leaners and 13.6 for Democrats and Democratic leaners; no demographic group had an average error below 12 points. The deeper problem was homogeneity. The synthetic respondents were "far less politically diverse" than the population and turned majority views within a party into near-unanimity. 86% of synthetic Democrats and Democratic leaners said that having people with fortunes of a billion dollars or more is a bad thing for the country, against 45% of real ones. More than 90% of synthetic Republicans, against 69% of real ones, said police should be allowed to stop anyone who fits a suspect's description. Pew stated that it has no current or future plans to use AI models to generate survey results [@pew2026well].

The pattern across the three studies is consistent. Models capture the average direction of opinion in large, well-studied groups. They miss the variance within groups and the views of people whose opinions cut across party lines, and their answers shift with prompt wording and model version. Variance, cross-cutting views and change over time are exactly what surveys exist to measure. Silicon samples can still help design questionnaires or explore hypotheses before fieldwork, but the evidence does not support using them in place of asking people.

## 9.7 Recommender systems, AI search and journalism

### Recommender systems and polarisation

A **recommender system** is software that selects and ranks content for each user, such as the order of posts in a social media feed, usually by predicting what the user will engage with. Recommender systems shaped public information long before chatbots, and a common fear is that they create "echo chambers" and increase **polarisation**, the growing distance or hostility between political groups.

Some of the strongest evidence comes from a collaboration between Meta and independent academics during the 2020 US election. Guess and colleagues (2023) switched a sample of consenting Facebook and Instagram users from the algorithmic feed to a reverse-chronological one. Time on the platforms fell substantially, and users saw more political and more untrustworthy content; over three months, there was no significant change in polarisation, political knowledge or other key attitudes [@guess2023social]. Nyhan and colleagues (2023) reduced exposure to content from like-minded sources by about a third for 23,377 users and found no measurable effect on eight pre-registered attitude measures, including polarisation and belief in false claims [@nyhan2023like]. González-Bailón and colleagues (2023) used aggregated data on 208 million US Facebook users. Ideological segregation in political news was high and increased at each stage, from what users could have seen, to what the feed showed them, to what they engaged with. It was also asymmetric: a substantial corner of the news ecosystem was consumed exclusively by conservatives, and most content rated false by Meta's fact-checkers sat in that corner, with no equivalent on the liberal side [@gonzalezbailon2023asymmetric].

Other studies found effects from more targeted changes. A long-running randomised experiment on Twitter compared users of the algorithmic feed with a control group of nearly 2 million daily active accounts on a chronological feed. The mainstream political right enjoyed higher algorithmic amplification than the mainstream left in six of seven countries studied, with no evidence that far-left or far-right groups were amplified more than moderate ones [@huszar2022algorithmic]. Piccardi and colleagues (2025) used a language model to rerank, in real time, the X feeds of 1,256 participants during the 2024 US campaign, demoting or promoting posts expressing antidemocratic attitudes and partisan animosity. Over ten days, this shifted feelings towards the opposing party by more than 2 points on a 100-point "feeling thermometer", in both directions and with no detectable difference between Democrats and Republicans [@piccardi2025reranking]. The two sets of findings are compatible. Removing the algorithm wholesale changed little over three months, while targeted changes to what it promotes had measurable short-term effects. The Piccardi study also shows that independent researchers can now test ranking interventions without a platform's cooperation, using a language model to do the reranking.

### AI search and the click

Search engines now often place an AI-written summary above the list of links. Pew Research Center tracked the browsing of 900 US adults in March 2025 and analysed 68,879 Google searches, 18% of which produced an AI summary. When a summary appeared, users clicked a traditional result link in 8% of visits, against 15% when none appeared. They clicked a link inside the summary in only 1% of visits, and ended their browsing session on the results page in 26% of visits against 16% [@chapekis2025google]. Summaries appeared on only 8% of one- or two-word searches but on 53% of searches of ten words or more, and on 60% of searches phrased as questions. The median summary ran to 67 words. Wikipedia, YouTube and Reddit together accounted for 15% of the sources the summaries cited; news sites accounted for 5%, the same share as in ordinary results [@chapekis2025google]. Figure 9.4 shows the click data. The study observed one month of behaviour in one country.

For publishers, fewer clicks mean less advertising and subscription revenue. The Reuters Institute's *Digital News Report 2026*, based on surveys of about 2,000 people in each of 48 markets (97,520 in total), reports Chartbeat data showing that Google search traffic to more than 2,500 news sites fell by a third (33%) globally between November 2024 and November 2025, and by 38% in the United States. Publishers expect a further fall of 43% over three years. The report notes that the role of AI summaries in this decline is widely assumed rather than proven, and that hard-news queries are mostly excluded from them [@egan2026digital].

The same report tracks how audiences use AI for news. Weekly use of AI chatbots for news rose from 7% of respondents in 2025 to 10% in 2026, and to 16% among under-35s, though use did not grow in the United States, the United Kingdom, France or Germany. Only 20% of respondents said they trusted news answers from chatbots, against 37% for news in general; in the UK the figure was 6%. Among chatbot news users, 42% said they always or often clicked through to the original sources, compared with 44% from search and 36% from social media [@egan2026digital]. Whether AI answers attribute and link to journalism, and whether they reproduce it accurately, thus matters for both the business and the public value of news.

Accuracy remains a problem. In a study coordinated by the European Broadcasting Union (EBU) and the BBC, journalists from 22 public service media organisations in 18 countries and 14 languages rated more than 3,000 news answers from ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini and Perplexity. 45% of answers had at least one significant issue; 31% had serious sourcing problems and 20% major accuracy problems, such as invented details or outdated information [@ebu2025ais]. These figures describe one set of products in 2025 and may change quickly. Whether news content may lawfully be used to train AI systems belongs to the wider copyright disputes covered in Section 13.6.

## 9.8 Regulating platforms and synthetic content

The rules on what platforms must do about harmful information differ sharply between jurisdictions. In the United States, Section 230 of the Communications Act provides that no online service shall be treated as the publisher of information provided by its users [@uscongress1996protection], and new federal rules have targeted specific harms. The European Union regulates broadly, through the Digital Services Act for platforms and the AI Act for the makers and users of generative AI. Table 9.3 compares the main instruments.

### The Digital Services Act

The **Digital Services Act (DSA)** has applied in full since 17 February 2024 [@europeanparliament2022regulation, Article 93] and scales duties to platform size. Its strictest rules apply to "very large online platforms" and search engines, those with at least 45 million average monthly active users in the EU [@europeanparliament2022regulation, Article 33(1)]. Each year these must assess "systemic risks" arising from their design and use, including how their recommender systems contribute. The listed risks explicitly include "any actual or foreseeable negative effects on civic discourse and electoral processes" [@europeanparliament2022regulation, Article 34]. Platforms must then take reasonable mitigation measures. These may include testing and adapting recommender systems, and ensuring that generated or manipulated images, audio or video that falsely appear authentic are distinguishable "through prominent markings" [@europeanparliament2022regulation, Article 35(1)(d) and (k)]. They must offer at least one recommender option not based on profiling the user [@europeanparliament2022regulation, Article 38] and give vetted researchers access to data. The European Commission can fine them up to 6% of worldwide annual turnover [@europeanparliament2022regulation, Article 74].

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: Romania's annulled election and the DSA**

On 6 December 2024, Romania's Constitutional Court annulled the presidential election after its first round, two days before a scheduled run-off involving the first-round leader, Călin Georgescu. The decision followed the declassification of intelligence reports alleging a coordinated campaign on TikTok and Telegram; one report said about 25,000 pro-Georgescu TikTok accounts became highly active two weeks before the vote. Much of this evidence consisted of allegations, Georgescu called the decision a coup, and the attribution to Russia rested on similarities with earlier operations [@solea2024romanias]. On 17 December 2024 the European Commission opened formal DSA proceedings against TikTok, focused on the management of election risks linked to its recommender systems and on its rules for political advertising; opening proceedings does not prejudge the outcome [@europeancommission2024commission]. The case shows that the most consequential threats to an election may come from coordinated amplification and undisclosed paid promotion, with or without generative AI, and that annulling an election is itself contested.
:::

The DSA's first non-compliance decision came on 5 December 2025, when the Commission fined X €120 million. It found that X's paid "blue checkmark" was deceptive design, that its advertising repository lacked required information, and that its terms prevented researchers from accessing public data [@europeancommission2025commission]. The research-access finding matters for the evidence base itself: much of what is known about recommender systems comes from studies that depended on a platform's voluntary cooperation [@guess2023social], and the DSA attempts to make such access a legal right.

The DSA is contested, and the two sides rely on different kinds of evidence. Critics argue that duties framed around "systemic risks" push platforms to remove lawful speech to avoid large fines. In July 2025 the Republican majority on the US House Judiciary Committee published an interim staff report arguing that the DSA "compels global censorship". Its central claim is that platforms apply one set of terms worldwide, and that Commission officials, according to documents the committee obtained under subpoena, expected platforms to change those global terms [@housejudiciary2025foreign]. The report is a partisan document in a transatlantic dispute and rests on the committee's own selection of material. The regulation's defenders point to its text, which requires platforms to weigh negative effects on fundamental rights, including freedom of expression, alongside risks to civic discourse [@europeanparliament2022regulation, Article 34(1)(b)]. Independent empirical work gives a mixed verdict on the DSA's transparency tools. Kaushal and colleagues (2024) analysed a representative sample of the 131 million "statements of reasons" for moderation decisions that platforms submitted to the DSA Transparency Database in November 2023. They found some transparency gains but concluded that compliance "remains problematic" because the database allows platforms "a lot of discretion" in what they report [@kaushal2024automated].

### The AI Act's transparency duties

Article 50 of the AI Act divides the labelling task between two groups. **Providers** of AI systems that generate synthetic audio, images, video or text must ensure that outputs "are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated". Their technical solutions must be effective, interoperable, robust and reliable "as far as this is technically feasible". The duty does not apply to tools that only assist with standard editing or do not substantially alter the input [@europeanparliament2024regulation, Article 50(2)]. **Deployers**, those who use such systems, must disclose that deepfake image, audio or video content is artificial. For evidently artistic, creative, satirical or fictional works, the duty shrinks to disclosing the existence of such content in a way that does not hamper enjoyment of the work. Deployers who publish AI-generated text to inform the public on matters of public interest must also disclose it, unless a human has reviewed it and someone holds editorial responsibility [@europeanparliament2024regulation, Article 50(4)]. Breaches can be fined up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide turnover [@europeanparliament2024regulation, Article 99(4)].

These duties began to apply on 2 August 2026 [@europeanparliament2024regulation, Article 113]. The Digital Omnibus on AI, in force since 27 July 2026, gave generative systems already on the market before that date until 2 December 2026 to add machine-readable marking. It also added two prohibitions from the same date, on AI systems that generate non-consensual intimate images of identifiable people and on those that generate child sexual abuse material [@europeanparliament2026regulation]. To make Article 50 workable, the Commission published a voluntary Code of Practice on marking and labelling AI-generated content on 10 June 2026. According to a summary by the law firm Jones Day, signatories commit to "at least two layers of machine-readable marking where necessary", such as metadata combined with a watermark. They must also provide detection tools that deployers, researchers and authorities can use, and label deepfakes with a common EU icon or equivalent [@jonesday2026european]. The layered approach reflects the evidence in Section 9.3: no single marking technique is robust on its own. Chapter 13 sets out the AI Act's overall timetable.

### Non-consensual intimate imagery

The harm from deepfakes that is best documented, and most clearly growing, is not political but sexual. Chesney and Citron (2019) predicted that the technology would be used for non-consensual intimate imagery (NCII), fraud and harassment as well as political deception [@chesney2019deep].

In the United States, the TAKE IT DOWN Act, signed on 19 May 2025, made it a federal crime knowingly to publish non-consensual intimate images of identifiable people, including AI-generated "digital forgeries". The penalty is up to two years' imprisonment where the victim is an adult and three years where the victim is a minor. Covered platforms must set up a notice-and-removal process within one year, that is by 19 May 2026. On receiving a valid request they must remove the image "as soon as possible, but not later than 48 hours", and make reasonable efforts to remove known identical copies. Failure to comply is treated as an unfair or deceptive practice enforceable by the Federal Trade Commission, and platforms are shielded from liability for good-faith removals [@uscongress2025tools]. The Electronic Frontier Foundation, a digital-rights group, opposed the law. It argued that the takedown provision covers a broader category of content than the criminal offence and "lacks critical safeguards against frivolous or bad-faith takedown requests". In its view, the 48-hour deadline will push smaller services to remove lawful material rather than verify claims [@kelley2025congress].

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: Grok and the "undressing" images of January 2026**

Around the turn of 2026, X rolled out an image-editing feature powered by its Grok chatbot that let users alter photographs of real people with text prompts such as "remove her clothes". The Center for Countering Digital Hate, a campaigning non-profit, estimated from a random sample of images that Grok generated about three million sexualised images over 11 days, including about 23,000 that appeared to depict children; the estimate did not establish how many were made without consent [@afp2026musks]. Regulators responded under platform law rather than AI law. On 12 January 2026 the UK regulator Ofcom opened an investigation under the Online Safety Act into whether X had assessed and mitigated the risk of illegal content; the Act allows fines of up to £18 million or 10% of qualifying worldwide revenue [@jones2026ofcom]. On 26 January the European Commission opened formal DSA proceedings into whether X had assessed and mitigated the risks of deploying Grok's functions, including "manipulated sexually explicit images", stating that "these risks seem to have materialised" [@europeancommission2026commission]. The case illustrates the gap that the AI Act's new prohibition of non-consensual intimate image generation, applicable from 2 December 2026, is intended to close: the platform rules applied only after the images had spread.

Each of these regimes addresses one link of the chain. The AI Act targets production, the DSA and the Online Safety Act target distribution, and the TAKE IT DOWN Act targets individual publication and removal. None of them yet answers the measurement problem on which much of the evidence on AI and elections founders: whether independent researchers will get the data needed to trace exposure to synthetic content and link it to what people believe and do.
