# Chapter 9: Information, Media and Democracy

On 21 January 2024, two days before the New Hampshire presidential primary in the United States, thousands of voters received a telephone call in a voice that sounded like President Joe Biden. The voice had been cloned with generative AI, and the message urged recipients to "save your vote for the November election" instead of voting in the primary [@fcc2024proposes; @associatedpress2025new]. The US Federal Communications Commission (FCC) traced the calls to a political consultant, Steve Kramer, and confirmed a $6 million fine against him in September 2024 for unlawfully spoofing caller identification [@fcc2024proposes; @fcc2024issues]. In a separate state criminal trial, a New Hampshire jury acquitted him in June 2025 of all voter-suppression and impersonation charges [@associatedpress2025new].

The case contains most of the themes of this chapter. Synthetic audio was cheap to make and hard for listeners to identify. The harm was real but its size is uncertain: nobody knows how many people, if any, stayed at home because of the call. Existing institutions (a telecoms regulator, a state prosecutor, a jury) responded, with mixed results. This chapter asks what the evidence, as of October 2026, says about AI and the information ecosystem. It covers synthetic media, elections, persuasion, simulated public opinion, provenance technology, recommender systems, news and search, and platform regulation. Throughout, it reports both the findings that give cause for concern and those that suggest earlier fears were overstated.

## 9.1 What changes when content becomes cheap

The **information ecosystem** is the whole system through which a society produces, distributes and consumes information. It includes journalists, platforms, search engines, governments, advertisers and ordinary users. Researchers usually distinguish **misinformation** (false or misleading content shared without intent to deceive) from **disinformation** (false content spread deliberately). A third category, **influence operations**, describes coordinated efforts, often by states, to shape opinion covertly, for example through networks of fake accounts.

Generative AI mainly changes one part of this system: the cost of producing content. A large language model can write thousands of plausible posts, comments or articles in minutes, in many languages and styles. Image, audio and video generators can do the same for pictures and voices. An early assessment by Goldstein and colleagues (2023, a preprint) argued that these tools could make influence operations cheaper, larger and harder to detect. They described a pipeline with four points of intervention: how models are built, who can access them, how content is distributed and how audiences form beliefs [@goldstein2023generative]. Early evidence from platforms suggests that the gains so far have been modest. Meta reported in December 2024 that the covert influence networks it took down had made "only incremental productivity and content-generation gains" from generative AI, according to the company [@clegg2024what].

Production cost is only one link in that chain, however. A useful analogy is printing: cheap printing made it easy to produce leaflets, but a leaflet nobody reads changes nothing. Content must also be distributed, noticed and believed. Kapoor and Narayanan (2024) argue that generative AI lowers the cost of creating false content but not the cost of distributing it, and that misinformation spreads mainly because of demand, since people seek out claims that fit what they already believe [@kapoor2024looked]. Altay, Berriche and Acerbi (2023) make a related argument from the research literature: people do not believe everything they see online, and misinformation often "preaches to the choir", reaching those who already agree [@altay2023misinformation].

The analogy has limits. Unlike leaflets, AI can also take part in conversations, answer questions and adapt to each reader, which is why the persuasion research in Section 9.4 matters. Generative tools are also being built into the distribution channels themselves, such as search engines and social media feeds, which Section 9.6 examines. The rest of the chapter therefore looks at each link of the chain in turn: making content, persuading people, verifying content, and distributing it.

## 9.2 Deepfakes, synthetic media and the liar's dividend

**Synthetic media** is any image, audio, video or text generated or substantially altered by AI. A **deepfake** is synthetic media that realistically depicts a real person or event in a way that could be mistaken for authentic. The European Union's AI Act defines a deep fake as AI-generated or manipulated image, audio or video content that resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities or events and "would falsely appear to a person to be authentic or truthful" [@europeanparliament2024regulation, Article 3(60)]. A **cheap fake**, by contrast, is a misleading edit made with ordinary tools, such as slowing down a video or cutting a quotation out of context.

How good are people at spotting synthetic content? Laboratory evidence suggests not very good. In a pre-registered experiment with 210 participants, Köbis, Doležalová and Soraperra (2021) found that people could not reliably detect deepfake videos. Neither raising awareness nor offering money for correct answers improved accuracy, and participants overestimated their own ability [@kobis2021fooled]. Nightingale and Farid (2022) showed participants a mix of real faces and faces generated by AI. In their first experiment (315 participants), accuracy was 48.2%, close to chance; training and feedback raised it only to 59.0% in a second experiment. In a third experiment, synthetic faces were rated 7.7% more trustworthy than real ones [@nightingale2022ai]. These studies used still images or short clips, and generators have improved since, so detection by eye is unlikely to have become easier.

Deepfakes can also harm individuals directly. Chesney and Citron (2019) warned early that the technology would be used for non-consensual intimate imagery, fraud and harassment as well as political deception [@chesney2019deep]. Legislators have responded. In the United States, the TAKE IT DOWN Act, signed on 19 May 2025, made it a federal crime to knowingly publish non-consensual intimate images, including "digital forgeries" created with AI. It also requires covered platforms to remove such images within 48 hours of a valid request, with platforms given one year to set up the removal process [@uscongress2025tools]. The EU AI Act requires anyone who deploys an AI system to create a deepfake to disclose that the content is artificial, with lighter duties for evidently artistic or satirical work [@europeanparliament2024regulation, Article 50(4)].

A subtler effect of deepfakes concerns authentic content. If people know that convincing fakes exist, a person caught on a genuine recording can claim that it was faked. Chesney and Citron called this the **liar's dividend**.

::: {.box .concept}
**Key concept: The liar's dividend**

The liar's dividend is the benefit that people caught in genuine scandals can gain by falsely claiming that the evidence is fake. Chesney and Citron (2019) observed, perversely, that the dividend grows as public education about deepfakes succeeds, because a sceptical public is more willing to doubt real evidence [@chesney2019deep]. A courtroom analogy helps: once everyone knows that documents can be forged, a defendant can cast doubt on a genuine letter just by alleging forgery. The analogy breaks down because courts have formal rules for authenticating evidence, while public opinion has none.
:::

Is the liar's dividend real? Schiff, Schiff and Bueno (2025) tested it in five survey experiments with more than 15,000 American adults. Participants read about real scandals involving politicians, followed by a hypothetical response from the politician. False claims that a story was "fake news" raised support for the politician across partisan groups, more than staying silent or apologising did. However, the strategy worked against text-based reports and was largely ineffective against video evidence, and it did not reduce general trust in the media [@schiff2025liars]. The study therefore supports the existence of a liar's dividend while suggesting that, at least when it was conducted, video still carried more weight than text. Whether that remains true as convincing video fakes become common is an open question.

## 9.3 The 2024 election year: what the evidence shows

In 2024 voters went to the polls in the United States, India, Indonesia, Mexico, the United Kingdom, the European Union and many other places [@clegg2024what], and many commentators predicted that generative AI would flood these campaigns with deceptive content. Two years later, several bodies of evidence allow a first assessment. Most of them point towards limited measurable impact on results, with important caveats about what can be measured.

Researchers at the Alan Turing Institute's Centre for Emerging Technology and Security (CETaS) examined the UK general election, the French legislative elections and the European Parliament elections of mid-2024. They identified 16 confirmed viral cases of AI-enabled disinformation or deepfakes during the UK election and 11 across the EU and French elections combined, and found no evidence that any of them meaningfully affected a result [@stockwell2024threat]. Their later report on the US election likewise found no evidence of a measurable impact on the result, while noting that this was largely because data on real-world voter behaviour were lacking. It also recorded far more viral AI-enabled disinformation than in the earlier elections and warned about damage to trust [@stockwell2024ai]. Meta, the company that owns Facebook and Instagram, reported that during the major elections of 2024, its fact-checking partners' ratings of AI content on elections, politics and social topics made up less than 1% of all fact-checked misinformation, according to the company [@clegg2024what]. This figure covers only content that fact-checkers examined, and does not show how much AI content circulated overall.

Kapoor and Narayanan (2024) analysed 78 election-related uses of AI collected by the magazine WIRED's AI Elections Project. Half of them (39) showed no deceptive intent. For each of the 39 deceptive cases, the authors estimated that similar content could have been produced without AI for no more than a few hundred dollars. They also cited a finding by the News Literacy Project that cheap fakes were used about seven times as often as AI content in the US election [@kapoor2024looked].

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Were fears about AI and the 2024 elections overblown?**

*Yes, largely.* Simon, Altay and Mercier (2023) argued before the election year that concerns about AI-driven misinformation were overblown, because the supply of false content was never the main constraint; attention and belief are [@simon2023misinformation]. The record of 2024 appears to support them: monitoring studies found few viral AI cases and no measurable effect on outcomes [@stockwell2024threat; @stockwell2024ai], AI content was a small share of fact-checked misinformation on Meta's platforms [@clegg2024what], and most deceptive cases could have been made cheaply without AI [@kapoor2024looked].

*Not so fast.* Absence of evidence of an effect on outcomes is not evidence of no effect. Elections are decided by small margins, effects on turnout or trust are hard to measure, and monitoring catches only what becomes visible. CETaS itself warned that confusion over what is real, including genuine content wrongly dismissed as AI-generated, was damaging trust in online information [@stockwell2024threat; @stockwell2024ai]. Experimental research, discussed in Section 9.4, also shows that conversational AI can shift political attitudes in ways that static content does not, and monitoring of public posts cannot see private conversations with chatbots.

*What would settle it:* access to platform data sufficient to trace exposure to AI content and link it to behaviour, and repeated assessment as tools change. The EU's data-access rules for researchers (Section 9.7) are partly designed to make such studies possible.
:::

The more serious documented interference of the period did not rely mainly on generative AI. The best-known case, the annulment of Romania's 2024 presidential election, is discussed as a case study in Section 9.7, because it concerns the amplification of content by platforms more than its creation.

## 9.4 AI as a persuader, and AI as a stand-in for the public

### Static messages and conversations

Political scientists have long found that persuading voters during a general election is hard. Drawing on a meta-analysis of 40 field experiments and nine new experiments of their own, Kalla and Broockman (2018) concluded that the best estimate of the effect of campaign contact and advertising on Americans' candidate choices in general elections is zero [@kalla2018minimal]. Studies of AI-written static messages (a fixed text that everyone reads) found that AI could match human writers, with little sign of exceeding them. Goldstein and colleagues (2024) found that propaganda articles written by GPT-3 were highly persuasive and, when a fluent English speaker edited the prompts or curated the output, under some conditions as persuasive as real foreign propaganda [@goldstein2024persuasive]. Yet Hackenburg and Margetts (2024) found that tailoring GPT-4 messages to each person's characteristics, known as **microtargeting**, did not make them more persuasive than generic messages [@hackenburg2024evaluating]. A further study found sharply diminishing returns to model size for single-message persuasion: larger models were somewhat more persuasive, but further scaling was not expected to add much [@hackenburg2025scaling].

Conversation appears to be different. In a widely cited experiment, Costello, Pennycook and Rand (2024) asked 2,190 Americans who believed a conspiracy theory to describe it, along with their evidence, and then to discuss it with GPT-4 Turbo over three rounds. The AI was instructed to reduce belief using evidence. Belief in the chosen conspiracy fell by about 20% on average, and the effect persisted undiminished for at least two months. A professional fact-checker rated 99.2% of a sample of 128 AI claims as true. The AI did not reduce belief in conspiracies that were true, and the effect spilled over to reduce belief in unrelated conspiracy theories [@costello2024durably]. The result suggests that some beliefs thought to be immune to evidence can change when the evidence is detailed and tailored to the person's own arguments.

The same capacity can be pointed in any direction. Hackenburg and colleagues (2025) ran three experiments with 76,977 UK adults, 19 language models and 707 political issues. Conversations with AI were 41% to 52% more persuasive than a static AI-written message, and between 36% and 42% of the immediate effect was still measurable a month later. Two further findings stand out. First, the main sources of persuasive power were post-training and prompting, not model size or personalisation. Methods that encouraged models to pack conversations with facts and evidence worked best: each additional fact-checkable claim was associated with about 0.3 points more persuasion, and roughly half of the explainable variation in persuasion was attributable to the number of claims made. Personalisation, by contrast, added less than one point on average. Second, the most persuasive set-ups were less accurate. In the most persuasive conditions, the authors estimated a persuasive effect of 15.9 points on a 0–100 agreement scale (against a 9.4-point average), with 30.0% of fact-checkable claims rated inaccurate, compared with 16.0% on average [@hackenburg2025levers].

Lin and colleagues (2025) moved from policy issues to real elections. In pre-registered experiments before the 2024 US presidential election, the 2025 Canadian federal election and the 2025 Polish presidential election, participants were randomly assigned to talk with an AI that advocated for one of the two leading candidates. The effects on candidate preference were larger than typically found for video advertisements. In the US experiment (2,306 participants), the difference between the pro-Harris and pro-Trump conditions was 2.88 points on a 0–100 scale; in Canada and Poland it was about 10 points. Slightly more than a third of the US effect persisted after more than a month. In the Canadian and Polish experiments, when the AI was told not to use facts and evidence, its persuasive effect fell by more than half. The authors also report that models advocating for candidates on the political right made more inaccurate claims in all three countries, a finding based on accuracy ratings produced by an AI system and checked against ratings by professional fact-checkers and by politically balanced groups of lay people [@lin2025persuading]. Figure 9.1 brings these results together.

These results need careful interpretation. Participants were recruited online, paid and told they were talking to an AI; in the experiments of Hackenburg and colleagues, conversations lasted nine minutes on average [@hackenburg2025levers], and real voters may never choose to spend that long discussing politics with a chatbot. Lin and colleagues note that they debriefed participants before the elections and randomised the direction of persuasion so that any lasting effects would cancel out [@lin2025persuading]. Effects in the US presidential race were also small compared with those for the less familiar Massachusetts ballot measure in the same study, where the gap between pro and anti conditions among people without strong views was 22.7 points; the authors suggest that AI dialogues may have a bigger impact on state and local contests than on national ones [@lin2025persuading]. Finally, Salvi and colleagues (2025) found that GPT-4 given basic demographic information about its opponent was more persuasive than a human debater in 64.4% of the debates in which the two were not equally persuasive (900 participants) [@salvi2025conversational]. Taken together, the evidence shows that AI dialogue can change minds, mainly by marshalling information, and that accuracy and persuasiveness can pull in opposite directions.

### Silicon sampling

A different use of language models is to stand in for survey respondents. Argyle and colleagues (2023) conditioned GPT-3 on thousands of demographic "backstories" taken from real US survey participants and compared the model's answers with the real ones. They found that, with suitable conditioning, the model reproduced the response patterns of many subgroups, a property they called **algorithmic fidelity**, and they named the resulting synthetic respondents **silicon samples** [@argyle2023out].

::: {.box .concept}
**Key concept: Silicon sampling**

Silicon sampling means using a language model, prompted with the characteristics of real people, to generate synthetic answers to survey questions. The analogy is a flight simulator: it can be useful for practice and for testing ideas cheaply, but no airline would certify a new aircraft on simulator data alone. The analogy breaks down because a flight simulator is built from known physics, whereas a language model's picture of public opinion comes from text that may be outdated, unrepresentative or shaped by the model's own training.
:::

Later studies found the limits of this approach. Bisbee and colleagues (2024) asked ChatGPT, adopting different personas, to rate 11 social and political groups. Average scores closely matched the American National Election Study, but the synthetic answers varied less than real ones, statistical relationships often differed, results changed with small changes to prompt wording, and the same prompt gave significantly different results over three months [@bisbee2024synthetic]. In September 2026 the Pew Research Center tested "digital twins" built with Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.6, the best performer among several models it tried, using real panel members' demographics and earlier answers. Across three 2026 survey waves, synthetic results differed from human results by about 12 percentage points on average, with the largest error (16.1 points) for Republicans. The synthetic samples were "far less politically diverse" than the real population: 86% of synthetic Democrats and Democratic leaners said billionaires' fortunes were bad for the country, against 45% of real Democrats. Pew stated that it has no plans to use AI models to generate survey results [@pew2026well]. Silicon samples may help researchers design questions and generate hypotheses, but the evidence so far does not support using them in place of asking people.

## 9.5 Provenance, watermarking and detection

If people cannot reliably spot synthetic content by eye, can technology help? Three approaches exist, and they answer different questions. **Detection** tries to infer from the content alone whether it was made by AI. **Watermarking** hides a signal in AI-generated content at the moment of creation so that it can be recognised later. **Content provenance** attaches a signed record of where content came from and how it was edited.

Detection after the fact has a poor record for text. Weber-Wulff and colleagues (2023) tested 14 detection tools, including two commercial systems widely used in education, and concluded that they were "neither accurate nor reliable", with a bias towards classifying AI text as human-written; disguising the text, for example by machine translation or paraphrasing, made them worse [@weberwulff2023testing]. Liang and colleagues (2023) found that detectors frequently misclassified writing by non-native English speakers as AI-generated, which raises fairness concerns when such tools are used to judge students [@liang2023gpt].

Content provenance takes the opposite approach: instead of guessing whether something is fake, it records where authentic or labelled content came from. The main standard is maintained by the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA), founded in February 2021 by Adobe, Arm, the BBC, Intel, Microsoft and Truepic [@microsoft2021technology]. A C2PA **manifest**, marketed as "Content Credentials", is a bundle of statements about a file (for example, which camera or AI tool created it and what edits were made) that is digitally signed so that tampering can be detected. Cryptographic hashes, a kind of digital fingerprint of the exact file contents, bind the manifest to the file, so any change to the content invalidates them. Because manifests can be stripped from files, the standard also allows "soft bindings", such as invisible watermarks, that help locate a stored copy of the manifest. The C2PA states plainly that provenance information "cannot tell you whether the digital content is true, accurate or factual" [@c2pa2025c2pa]. Figure 9.2 shows the pipeline.

Watermarking for AI-generated content is now deployed at scale by some developers. Google DeepMind's SynthID-Text, described in *Nature* by Dathathri and colleagues (2024), changes only how the model chooses each next word, so that a statistical signal can later be detected without access to the model. In a live experiment covering nearly 20 million responses from Google's Gemini chatbot, user feedback indicated no loss of text quality [@dathathri2024scalable]. Google said in May 2025 that more than 10 billion pieces of content had been watermarked with SynthID, according to the company [@perez2025google].

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: How a text watermark works**

A language model writes by repeatedly choosing the next token (a word or word fragment) from a probability distribution. In the scheme of Kirchenbauer and colleagues (2023), before each choice a secret key and the preceding token are used to split the vocabulary pseudo-randomly into a "green list" and a "red list", and the model is nudged towards green tokens [@kirchenbauer2023watermark]. Human writers, who do not know the key, use green tokens about as often as chance predicts. A detector with the key counts green tokens: if a 200-token passage contains far more than expected, it is very unlikely to be unwatermarked. In words, the test asks "how surprising is this many green tokens if the text were not watermarked?", much as one might suspect a loaded die after seeing too many sixes. SynthID-Text uses a different sampling method, called tournament sampling, but rests on the same idea of a hidden statistical bias [@dathathri2024scalable]. Paraphrasing replaces tokens and weakens the signal, which is why short or heavily edited texts are hard to classify.
:::

Every one of these tools has limits. Watermarks work only if the generator adds them, and open-weight models that anyone can run (Chapter 7) need not. Zhang and colleagues (2024) proved that, under natural assumptions, no watermark can be "strong" in the sense of being impossible to remove without degrading quality, because an attacker can make small random edits while preserving quality [@zhang2024watermarks]. Provenance depends on cameras, editing software and platforms all adopting the standard and displaying it in ways users understand. A signed record that a photograph came from a particular camera does not show that the scene was not staged, and the absence of a record proves nothing, since most authentic content carries none. Provenance is therefore most useful for verifying content from sources that choose to sign it, such as news organisations, and less useful for exposing fakes from those that do not. Regulators are nonetheless building on these tools. The EU AI Act requires providers of systems that generate synthetic audio, images, video or text to ensure that outputs "are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated", where technically feasible [@europeanparliament2024regulation, Article 50(2)]. These transparency duties began to apply on 2 August 2026 [@europeanparliament2024regulation, Article 113], although the 2026 Digital Omnibus on AI gave generative systems already on the market before that date until 2 December 2026 to add machine-readable marking [@europeanparliament2026regulation]; Chapter 13 discusses the timetable and the 2026 amendments.

## 9.6 Recommender systems, search and journalism

### Recommender systems

A **recommender system** is software that selects and ranks content for each user, such as the order of posts in a social media feed, usually by predicting what the user will engage with. Recommender systems were among the first forms of AI to shape public information, long before chatbots. A common fear is that they create "echo chambers" by showing people only views they already hold, and so increase **polarisation**, the growing distance or hostility between political groups.

Some of the strongest evidence comes from a collaboration between Meta and independent academics during the 2020 US election. Guess and colleagues (2023) switched a sample of consenting Facebook and Instagram users from the algorithmic feed to a reverse-chronological one. Time on the platforms fell substantially, and users saw more political and more untrustworthy content, but over three months there was no significant change in polarisation, political knowledge or other key attitudes [@guess2023social]. Nyhan and colleagues (2023) reduced exposure to content from like-minded sources by about a third for 23,377 users and found no measurable effect on eight pre-registered attitude measures, including polarisation and belief in false claims [@nyhan2023like]. Using aggregated data on 208 million US Facebook users, González-Bailón and colleagues (2023) found that ideological segregation in political news was high and increased from the news users could have seen, to what the feed showed them, to what they engaged with. They also found an asymmetry: a substantial corner of the news ecosystem was consumed exclusively by conservatives, and most content rated false by Meta's fact-checking partners sat in that corner, with no equivalent on the liberal side [@gonzalezbailon2023asymmetric].

Other studies point the other way. A long-running randomised experiment on Twitter, comparing a control group of nearly 2 million daily active accounts on a chronological feed with users on the algorithmic one, found that the mainstream political right enjoyed higher algorithmic amplification than the mainstream left in six of seven countries studied; it found no evidence that the algorithm amplified far-left or far-right groups more than moderate ones [@huszar2022algorithmic]. Piccardi and colleagues (2025) used a language model to rerank the X feeds of 1,256 participants during the 2024 US campaign, reducing or increasing posts that expressed partisan animosity or antidemocratic attitudes. Over ten days, this shifted animosity towards the opposing party by more than two points on a 100-point "feeling thermometer", in both directions and with no detectable difference between Democrats and Republicans [@piccardi2025reranking]. The two sets of findings can both hold: removing the algorithm entirely changed little over three months, while targeted changes to what the algorithm promotes had measurable short-term effects. The Piccardi study also illustrates that language models can be used to make feeds less divisive as well as more.

### AI search and the news business

Generative AI is also changing how people reach news. Search engines now often place an AI-written summary above the list of links. Pew Research Center tracked the browsing of 900 US adults in March 2025. In visits to search pages that showed an AI summary, users clicked a traditional result link 8% of the time, against 15% when no summary appeared; they clicked a link inside the summary in only 1% of visits, and were more likely to end their browsing session [@chapekis2025google]. Figure 9.3 shows these results. The study observed one month of behaviour in one country and cannot show how habits change over time.

For publishers, fewer clicks mean less advertising and subscription revenue. The Reuters Institute's *Digital News Report 2026*, based on surveys in 48 markets, reports Chartbeat data showing that Google search traffic to more than 2,500 news sites fell by a third globally between November 2024 and November 2025, and that publishers expect a further fall of more than 40% over three years. The report notes that the effect of AI summaries is widely assumed rather than proven, and that hard-news queries are mostly excluded from them. Weekly use of AI chatbots for news rose from 7% to 10% of respondents in a year (16% among under-35s), while only 20% said they trusted news answers from chatbots, compared with 37% for news in general. Among those who used chatbots for news, 42% said they always or often clicked through to the original sources [@egan2026digital]. For news organisations, this makes it important whether AI answers attribute and link to their reporting, and whether those answers are accurate.

Whether these answers are reliable is itself a research question. In a study coordinated by the European Broadcasting Union (EBU) and the BBC, journalists from 22 public service media organisations in 18 countries rated more than 3,000 news answers from ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini and Perplexity. Some 45% of answers had at least one significant issue; 31% had serious sourcing problems and 20% had major accuracy problems, such as invented details or outdated information [@ebu2025ais]. These figures describe one set of products at one time (2025) and may change quickly, but they suggest that AI assistants do not yet reliably reproduce what the news organisations they draw on actually reported. Chapter 13 discusses the related legal disputes over the use of news content to train AI systems.

## 9.7 Platform governance

The rules that decide what platforms must do about harmful information differ sharply between jurisdictions. In the United States, Section 230 of the Communications Act provides that no online service shall be treated as the publisher of information provided by its users [@uscongress1996protection], and new regulation has focused on specific harms, such as the intimate-image law described in Section 9.2. The European Union has taken a broader approach through the **Digital Services Act (DSA)**, which has applied in full since 17 February 2024 [@europeanparliament2022regulation, Article 93] and sets duties for online platforms in proportion to their size.

The DSA's strictest rules apply to "very large online platforms" and search engines. Each year these must assess "systemic risks" arising from their design and use, explicitly including "any actual or foreseeable negative effects on civic discourse and electoral processes", and must consider how their recommender systems contribute [@europeanparliament2022regulation, Article 34]. They must then take reasonable mitigation measures, which may include testing and adapting their recommender systems and ensuring that generated or manipulated images, audio or video that falsely appear authentic are distinguishable "through prominent markings" [@europeanparliament2022regulation, Article 35(1)(d) and (k)]. They must offer at least one recommender option not based on profiling the user [@europeanparliament2022regulation, Article 38], and give vetted researchers access to data. The European Commission can fine them up to 6% of their worldwide annual turnover [@europeanparliament2022regulation, Article 74].

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: Romania's annulled election and the DSA**

On 6 December 2024, Romania's Constitutional Court annulled the presidential election after its first round, two days before a scheduled run-off involving the first-round leader, Călin Georgescu. The decision followed the declassification of intelligence reports alleging a coordinated campaign on TikTok and Telegram; one report said about 25,000 pro-Georgescu TikTok accounts became highly active two weeks before the vote. Much of this evidence consisted of allegations, Georgescu called the decision a coup, and the attribution to Russia rested on similarities with earlier operations [@solea2024romanias]. On 17 December 2024 the European Commission opened formal DSA proceedings against TikTok, focusing on the management of election risks linked to its recommender systems and on its rules for political advertising; opening proceedings does not prejudge the outcome [@europeancommission2024commission]. The case shows that the most consequential threats to an election may come from coordinated amplification and undisclosed paid promotion, with or without generative AI, and that annulling an election is itself contested.
:::

The DSA's first non-compliance decision came on 5 December 2025, when the Commission fined X €120 million. The decision found that X's paid "blue checkmark" was deceptive design, that its advertising repository lacked required information, and that its terms prevented researchers from accessing public data [@europeancommission2025commission]. The research-access provisions matter for this chapter's evidence: much of what is known about recommender systems comes from studies that depended on a platform's voluntary cooperation [@guess2023social], and the DSA attempts to make such access a legal right.

Platform governance increasingly overlaps with AI governance. The EU AI Act's marking and disclosure duties (Section 9.5) apply to those who make and deploy generative AI, while the DSA's duties apply to the platforms that distribute content. The United States' TAKE IT DOWN Act targets one specific harm across all covered platforms [@uscongress2025tools]. Chapter 13 compares these approaches with those of China, the United Kingdom and international bodies. The evidence reviewed in this chapter suggests several questions that any of these regimes must answer: how to make distribution, not only production, more accountable; how to keep provenance signals intact as content moves between services; and how to give independent researchers the data needed to measure effects that the monitoring studies of 2024 could not detect.

## Summary

- Generative AI lowers the cost of producing persuasive and deceptive content, but distribution, attention and belief remain important constraints on its impact.
- People detect deepfakes poorly, and experimental evidence supports a "liar's dividend" for politicians who falsely dismiss text-based reports as fake, though less so for video.
- Monitoring studies and platform reports from the 2024 election year found few viral AI cases and no evidence that AI changed results; these findings measure visible content and outcomes, not subtler effects on trust or turnout.
- Conversations with AI changed political attitudes and conspiracy beliefs more than static messages, with effects that partly persisted for one to two months; persuasion came mainly from presenting information, and the most persuasive set-ups were less accurate.
- Silicon samples reproduce some average patterns of public opinion but understate its diversity and are not a substitute for surveying people.
- Provenance standards (C2PA) and watermarks (such as SynthID) can label content at creation, but they depend on adoption, can be stripped or weakened, and cannot establish truth; detectors that judge content after the fact are unreliable.
- Field experiments found that removing feed algorithms changed polarisation little over three months, while targeted reranking had small measurable effects; AI search summaries are associated with fewer clicks to news sites.
- The EU's Digital Services Act requires the largest platforms to assess and mitigate risks to electoral processes and has produced its first fine; approaches elsewhere are narrower.

## Discussion questions

1. Kapoor and Narayanan argue that political misinformation is "not an AI problem" because AI lowers the cost of producing content but not of distributing it. Using evidence from Sections 9.3 and 9.4, evaluate how well this argument holds for static content and for conversational AI.
2. Schiff, Schiff and Bueno found that claiming "fake news" helped politicians against text reports but not against video. What would you expect to happen to this result as realistic video generation becomes widespread, and how could you test your prediction?
3. A city council proposes to replace its annual resident survey with a silicon sample built from last year's respondents. Using the Pew (2026) and Bisbee et al. (2024) findings, write a short recommendation on what the synthetic sample could and could not be used for.
4. Lin et al. found that AI persuasion effects were about three times larger in Canada and Poland than in the US presidential race, and much larger for a state ballot measure. What explanations can you suggest, and what do they imply for which elections are most exposed to AI persuasion?
5. A news photograph carries valid Content Credentials showing that it came from a professional camera and was only cropped. Does this establish that the photograph is an accurate record of the event? Explain using the distinction between provenance and truth.
6. The Meta-academic studies of 2020 found that switching off feed algorithms did not reduce polarisation, while Piccardi et al. (2025) found that reranking posts did change attitudes. Are these findings contradictory? What features of each design might explain the difference?
