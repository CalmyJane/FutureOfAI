In the second week of October 2024, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded two of its Nobel Prizes for work on artificial intelligence. On 8 October the physics prize went to John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton "for foundational discoveries and inventions that enable machine learning with artificial neural networks" [@royal2024nobelphysics]. The next day, half of the chemistry prize went to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper of Google DeepMind "for protein structure prediction", with the other half going to David Baker for computational protein design [@royal2024nobelchemistry]. The physics prize honoured the ideas behind the networks described in Chapter 2. The chemistry prize honoured something different: a use of those networks that had changed how a whole field works.

These awards are a convenient marker, but they do not settle what AI does for science. A model can be accurate without telling us why it is accurate. A system can produce thousands of candidate results that nobody has the time to check. And a headline about AI "discovering" something can rest on an evaluation that a later reanalysis overturns. This chapter looks at four areas where the evidence is unusually good (weather forecasting, materials science, mathematics and computing) and then at systems that try to automate the whole research process. It ends with the critiques: reproducibility, the risk of believing we understand more than we do, and the idea of "intellectual debt". Biology and medicine appear only briefly, at the level of the protein-structure example; Chapter 10 covers health.

## 6.1 Where AI enters the scientific cycle

Science is often described as a cycle. Researchers read what is already known, form a hypothesis, design an experiment or a simulation, collect and analyse data, and then publish results that others check and build on. Machine learning can enter at every stage, but it does different jobs at different stages. It helps to separate four of them.

The first job is **prediction**: learning a mapping from inputs to outputs from many solved examples, so that new cases can be answered without doing the slow work each time. The second is **emulation**: learning to imitate an expensive simulation, such as a weather model, so that it can be run far more cheaply. The third is **search**: proposing many candidate solutions (crystal structures, proofs, algorithms) and keeping the ones that pass a test. The fourth is **automation** of the research workflow itself: reading papers, writing code, running analyses and drafting manuscripts. Figure 6.1 places these jobs on the scientific cycle.

The four jobs differ in how easily their results can be checked, and this turns out to matter more than the sophistication of the model. A prediction can be compared with a later measurement. An emulator can be compared with the simulation it imitates and with what actually happens. A candidate found by search may be checkable instantly (a proof, a faster algorithm) or only after months of laboratory work (a new compound). Automated research produces claims of every kind at once. The sections that follow keep returning to this question of verification.

Protein structure prediction is the clearest example of the first job. A protein is a chain of building blocks (amino acids) that folds into a three-dimensional shape, and the shape largely determines what the protein does. Working out a single shape by experiment has typically taken months to years, and only around 100,000 unique structures had been determined, a small fraction of the known sequences [@jumper2021highly]. AlphaFold, a neural network trained on the public archive of experimentally determined structures, the Protein Data Bank, predicts the 3D structure from the sequence alone. In the CASP14 assessment, a blind competition in which predictions are scored against unpublished experimental structures, it achieved accuracy "competitive with experimental structures in a majority of cases" [@jumper2021highly]. According to the Academy's press release, the team has since predicted the structure of "virtually all the 200 million proteins" researchers have identified, and more than two million people in 190 countries have used the tool [@royal2024nobelchemistry]. AlphaFold is the case that other AI-for-science projects are measured against. It also shows the pattern that recurs through this chapter: a large archive of carefully checked human data, a well-defined target, and an independent test.

::: {.box .concept}
**Key concept: Emulator (surrogate model)**

An emulator is a machine-learning model trained to reproduce the outputs of a slower, more detailed model or measurement. Once trained, it gives approximate answers in seconds that would otherwise take hours of supercomputer time. A flight simulator is a rough analogy: it imitates how an aircraft responds without building one. The analogy breaks down because a flight simulator is written from the equations of flight, whereas an emulator learns only from examples, so it can be confidently wrong in situations its training data never covered.
:::

Scientists are adopting these tools quickly, and with mixed feelings. A 2023 *Nature* survey of more than 1,600 researchers found them both excited and concerned, with many expecting AI tools to become central to research soon [@vannoorden2023ai]. Lisa Messeri and M. J. Crockett offer a useful way to think about those concerns. Reviewing how scientists describe their hopes for AI, they identify four imagined roles: AI as *Oracle* (searching and summarising the literature), as *Surrogate* (standing in for data that are hard to collect), as *Quant* (analysing data too large for a human) and as *Arbiter* (judging which studies are sound) [@messeri2024artificial]. Each role promises more productivity and more objectivity. Section 6.6 returns to their warning about what those promises can hide.

## 6.2 Weather forecasting: learning the atmosphere

Weather forecasting is the area where machine learning has moved fastest from research paper to daily operational use, so it repays a closer look. Conventional forecasting is called **numerical weather prediction** (NWP). It starts from the best estimate of the current state of the atmosphere and steps forward in time by solving the equations of fluid dynamics and thermodynamics on a supercomputer. The European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) ran the leading global system; its high-resolution forecast produced a ten-day global forecast in around an hour [@lam2023learning].

Machine-learning weather models take another route. They are trained on **reanalysis** data, a consistent reconstruction of past weather that combines decades of observations with a physics model. ECMWF's ERA5 archive, which begins in 1979, is the standard training set. The model learns how the atmosphere tends to change over a few hours, then applies that learned step repeatedly to produce a forecast many days ahead.

GraphCast, published by Google DeepMind researchers in *Science* in 2023, was reported to beat the leading deterministic NWP system on most measures. Trained on 39 years of ERA5 data (1979–2017), it predicts hundreds of variables ten days ahead on a global grid of 0.25 degrees (roughly 28 kilometres at the equator) in under one minute on a single specialised chip [@lam2023learning]. It outperformed ECMWF's high-resolution forecast on 90% of 1,380 verification targets and gave better predictions of tropical cyclone tracks and extreme temperatures [@lam2023learning]. Training it took about four weeks on 32 chips [@lam2023learning], a cost paid once, after which each forecast is cheap.

A single forecast, however good, does not tell a decision-maker how confident to be. Operational centres therefore run **ensembles**: many forecasts from slightly different starting points, whose spread indicates uncertainty. GenCast, published in *Nature*, is a machine-learning ensemble model built on the same kind of generative method (a diffusion model) used for image generation. It produces 15-day forecasts and, in the authors' evaluation, had greater skill than ECMWF's operational ensemble on 97.2% of 1,320 targets, with better predictions of extreme weather, cyclone tracks and wind-power output [@price2025probabilistic]. Each 15-day forecast took about eight minutes on one chip, and the 50 ensemble members can be run in parallel.

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: Rolling a forecast forward**

GraphCast takes two snapshots of the global atmosphere, six hours apart, and predicts the snapshot six hours later. To forecast ten days ahead, it feeds its own prediction back in as input and repeats the step 40 times [@lam2023learning]. This is called an *autoregressive rollout*, the same idea a language model uses when it adds one token at a time (Chapter 3). Small errors can compound over many steps, so GraphCast was trained to minimise its error over up to 12 consecutive steps, not just one. A known side-effect is blurring: when the model is uncertain, the safest guess under its training objective is an average of possible outcomes, which looks smooth. GenCast instead samples individual sharp forecasts and lets the spread of 50 samples carry the uncertainty [@price2025probabilistic]. Its skill is scored with the continuous ranked probability score, which rewards forecasts whose range of predicted outcomes is centred close to what actually happened and is neither too narrow nor too wide.
:::

The step from research to operations came quickly. On 25 February 2025, ECMWF put its own Artificial Intelligence Forecasting System (AIFS) into operational service alongside its physics-based model. According to ECMWF, the AIFS outperforms leading physics-based models on many measures, including tropical cyclone tracks, with gains of up to 20%, and uses roughly 1,000 times less energy per forecast [@ecmwf2025ecmwfs]. ECMWF also points to practical users, such as renewable-energy operators who need forecasts of sunshine at the surface and wind speeds at turbine height [@ecmwf2025ecmwfs]. In December 2025 the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) launched a suite of three operational AI-based global forecasting systems; NOAA says it used GraphCast as the initial foundation and fine-tuned it on its own analyses. NOAA reports that its deterministic AI model uses up to 99.7% less computing than the traditional one, and that a 31-member AI ensemble needs only 9% of the computing of its physics-based ensemble [@noaa2025noaa]. A third, hybrid system combines the two ensembles and, according to NOAA, outperforms both across most major verification measures. Combining physics-based and learned forecasts in this way is one sign that the two approaches are being treated as complements.

Two deeper limits deserve attention. The first is dependence. These models start from an estimate of the current atmosphere produced by a physics-based system's **data assimilation**, the process that merges fresh observations into a model state; ECMWF notes that the AIFS uses the same initial conditions as its physics model [@ecmwf2025ecmwfs]. The AI models also learned from reanalysis data that a physics model produced. So far, they run alongside the older system.

The second limit concerns rare extremes. Sun and colleagues asked whether an AI weather model can forecast storms stronger than any it has seen [@sun2025can]. They trained versions of the FourCastNet model on 1979–2015 data, some with all tropical cyclones of Category 3 to 5 removed, and tested them on Category 5 storms from 2018–2023. All versions were similarly accurate for ordinary global weather. The version that had never seen strong storms, however, "cannot accurately forecast Category 5" storms, which suggests that such models do not extrapolate from weaker events [@sun2025can]. Removing strong storms from one ocean basin only did less harm, because the model could learn from strong storms elsewhere. The authors expect similar results for other models with similar training methods, though that remains to be tested. Weather forecasting therefore illustrates both sides of emulation: large gains in speed and average skill, with the greatest uncertainty precisely where forecasts matter most.

## 6.3 Materials and physical science: discovery claims under scrutiny

New materials underpin batteries, solar cells and microchips. Finding them has traditionally been slow: a chemist proposes a compound, a computer calculates whether it is likely to be stable, and a laboratory tries to make it. Machine learning promises to speed up the first two steps by searching enormous numbers of candidates.

In November 2023 Google DeepMind researchers published GNoME (Graph Networks for Materials Exploration) in *Nature*. The system uses graph neural networks, which represent a crystal as atoms (nodes) connected by bonds (edges), to predict whether a proposed crystal structure is stable. Building on about 48,000 stable crystals already known, the authors reported "the discovery of 2.2 million structures below the current convex hull", that is, structures predicted to be more stable than known alternatives, and described this as "an order-of-magnitude expansion in stable materials known to humanity" [@merchant2023scaling]. They also reported that 736 of their stable structures had already been made independently by experimental groups [@merchant2023scaling].

The claim drew a careful response from two solid-state chemists, Anthony Cheetham and Ram Seshadri. They examined the first 250 of 2,047 entries in one GNoME compilation and a random sample from the larger database of 384,870 predicted stable structures [@cheetham2024artificial]. They found "scant evidence for compounds that fulfill the trifecta of novelty, credibility, and utility". A recurring problem was ordering. The calculations, carried out in effect at absolute zero, place different atoms on separate, neat positions in the crystal; at the temperatures where real synthesis happens, similar atoms often mix on shared sites. Many "new" predictions were therefore ordered versions of compounds that were already known. Cheetham and Seshadri also argued that a compound only becomes a *material* when it shows some useful property, and that the work "does not report any new materials but reports a list of proposed compounds" [@cheetham2024artificial]. They nonetheless called the method promising and asked for more domain expertise in the next version.

A companion paper tested the next step: making predicted compounds with a robotic laboratory. Its targets were chosen using stability data from the Materials Project, a public database of computed materials properties, and from Google DeepMind [@szymanski2023autonomous]. The case study below follows what happened to it.

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: The A-Lab and its correction**

In the same issue of *Nature* as GNoME, a team at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and the University of California, Berkeley described the A-Lab, an autonomous laboratory that combines computation, recipes learned from the literature, machine learning and robotics. Over 17 days of continuous operation, they reported, it "realized 41 novel compounds from a set of 58 targets" [@szymanski2023autonomous].

In early 2024 a group of solid-state chemists published a reanalysis in *PRX Energy* [@leeman2024challenges]. They argued that the automated analysis of the X-ray diffraction patterns, the measurements used to identify what had been made, was "not yet reliable", and that the predictions neglected the atomic disorder described above. Their conclusion was that about two-thirds of the claimed successes were "likely to be known compositionally disordered versions of the predicted ordered compounds", and that no new materials had been discovered.

In January 2026 the original authors published a correction [@szymanski2026author]. It clarifies that "novel" had meant new to the prediction platform, "not necessarily new to science". After a manual reanalysis of the diffraction data, peer-reviewed after publication, the authors confirmed the platform's conclusion in 36 of the 40 reported successes and judged 4 inconclusive, so the count of compounds successfully made was reduced to 36. A further compound was removed from the discussion because it had mistakenly been included in the training data. The correction does not claim that the compounds are new to science. The episode shows the scientific process working, since outside experts checked a claim and the record was corrected. It also shows how long that took (more than two years) and how much expert labour it needed.
:::

Machine learning has also entered experimental physics directly. In 2022 researchers from DeepMind and the Swiss Plasma Center at EPFL in Lausanne used **reinforcement learning**, in which a system learns by trial and error against a reward signal, to control the magnetic coils of a fusion reactor of the tokamak type [@degrave2022magnetic]. The controller learned by interacting with a simulator of the reactor and was then used, without further tuning, on the real Tokamak à Configuration Variable. It held a range of plasma shapes, including two separate plasmas in the vessel at once. Here the test of success is direct: the plasma either holds its shape or it does not. That clarity is one reason why control problems in physics have been a productive area for AI.

The materials examples show where the difficulty lies. Generating candidates has become cheap; confirming them has not. A prediction of stability is a statement about a computer model at zero temperature. A claim of discovery is a statement about matter in a laboratory, and a claim of a useful material is a further statement about performance. Each step needs a different kind of evidence, and the AI system speeds up only the first.

## 6.4 Mathematics and computing: where answers can be checked

Mathematics occupies a special place in AI for science because some of its answers can be checked by machine. A **proof assistant** such as Lean is a programming language in which mathematical statements and proofs are written so precisely that a computer can verify every step. If a proof compiles, it is correct relative to the definitions used, whoever (or whatever) wrote it. This makes mathematics an unusually clean test of AI reasoning, although translating a problem into Lean is itself hard and can introduce errors.

Progress on competition mathematics has been rapid. In January 2024 Trinh and colleagues described AlphaGeometry, which combines a language model with a symbolic reasoning engine. Trained on synthetic theorems rather than human proofs, it solved 25 of 30 recent olympiad geometry problems, against 10 for the previous best method [@trinh2024solving]. At the International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO) in July 2024, AlphaProof and an improved AlphaGeometry 2 scored 28 out of 42 points, equivalent to a silver medal, one point below the gold threshold [@google2024ai]. The result came with caveats: humans first translated the problems into formal language, and some solutions took up to three days of computation, against the 4.5 hours students are given for each paper [@google2024ai]. The peer-reviewed account appeared online in *Nature* in November 2025 [@hubert2025olympiad]. It describes AlphaProof as an agent modelled on AlphaZero, the system that taught itself chess, shogi and Go [@google2024ai], that learns to find proofs in Lean by reinforcement learning. Its training set consisted of millions of problems automatically translated into Lean. For the hardest problems it generated and learned from many variants of the problem while it was working on it, which the authors call test-time reinforcement learning [@hubert2025olympiad]. The design relies on the proof checker: every proof the system finds is verified, so it can learn from its own successes without a human marking them.

A year later the conditions had changed. At the 2025 IMO, an advanced version of Google's Gemini model with its "Deep Think" reasoning mode solved five of the six problems for 35 points, a gold-medal score. It worked in natural language from the official problem statements within the time limit, and its solutions were graded and certified by IMO coordinators [@google2025advanced]. Two days earlier, OpenAI researchers had announced that an experimental OpenAI model reached the same score on the same five problems under exam conditions [@wei2025openai; @willison2025advanced]. That is a company claim: according to OpenAI, each proof was graded by three former IMO medallists, and the result was not certified by the IMO's coordinators. In twelve months, the best AI result had moved from formal-language solving with days of computation to natural-language proofs written under exam conditions. Olympiad problems are hard, but they have known solutions. Research mathematics asks questions nobody has answered. Here the most productive approach so far has been a search loop with an automatic checker. FunSearch, published in *Nature*, paired a language model that writes short programs with an evaluator that scores what the programs produce. Applied to the cap set problem, a question in combinatorics about how large a set of points can be without three in a line, it found constructions larger than any previously known [@romeraparedes2024mathematical]. Because it searches for programs rather than raw answers, its outputs could be read and understood by mathematicians.

AlphaEvolve, announced by Google DeepMind with a white paper in May 2025, extended this idea into a general coding agent that evolves whole algorithms [@novikov2025alphaevolve]. Its best-known result concerns multiplying two 4×4 matrices of complex numbers: it found a method using 48 multiplications, the first improvement in this setting since Volker Strassen's algorithm of 1969. On more than 50 open mathematical problems it matched the best known constructions in about 75% of cases and improved on them in about 20%, including a new lower bound for the "kissing number" in 11 dimensions (593 spheres touching a central sphere). In engineering, a scheduling rule it found for Google's data centres was reported to recover on average 0.7% of the company's fleet-wide computing resources [@novikov2025alphaevolve]. These are company results in a preprint, but the mathematical constructions can be checked independently. A follow-up paper with the mathematician Terence Tao, released as a preprint in November 2025 and published in *PNAS* in 2026, applied the system to 67 problems; it "rediscovered the best known solutions in most cases and improved on them in several" [@georgiev2025mathematical].

Computing itself has also been a target. In 2023 DeepMind's AlphaDev used reinforcement learning to discover short sorting routines that outperformed the human-written ones; they were added to the LLVM standard C++ sort library, a widely used open-source component [@mankowitz2023faster]. The pattern is the same as in FunSearch: a precisely defined goal, an automatic way to score attempts, and a search far larger than any human could run.

Claims about open problems need particular care. In October 2025, OpenAI staff posted that GPT-5 had found solutions to ten "open" problems from a website that collects problems posed by the mathematician Paul Erdős. The site's maintainer, Thomas Bloom, called this "a dramatic misinterpretation": "open" on his site meant only that he did not know of a solution, and the model had located existing papers [@bastian2025leading]. Most of the posts were deleted. The episode showed a real strength (searching a scattered literature) presented as a different one (solving problems).

In January 2026, by contrast, Erdős problem #728 was described as "the first Erdős problem ... regarded as fully resolved autonomously by an AI system". The proof came from a combination of OpenAI's GPT-5.2 Pro and Harmonic's Aristotle system, operated by Kevin Barreto, and was checked in Lean [@sothanaphan2026resolution]. Even here the details matter. The problem as Erdős and his co-authors stated it was ambiguous and had trivial solutions, and the site's maintainer judged that the AI proof answered a tightened version, "the question in the spirit it was intended" [@bloom2026erdos]; closely related earlier work by Pomerance was identified afterwards [@tao2026ai]. Tao, commenting on the result, warned against over-reading it: as reported, he noted that Erdős problems vary in difficulty "by several orders of magnitude", that many were never seriously studied, and that failures are rarely reported, so success rates are unknown [@bastian2026terence; @tao2026ai]. The lesson is that formal verification settles whether a proof is correct. It does not settle whether the problem was important or how often the method fails.

## 6.5 Automating research: "AI scientist" systems and AI-written code

The systems so far each do one job inside a human-led project. A newer line of work tries to automate the whole loop. The best documented is The AI Scientist, developed by the Tokyo company Sakana AI with researchers at the University of British Columbia and the University of Oxford. Its first version, released as a preprint in August 2024, generated research ideas in machine learning, wrote and ran the code, produced figures, wrote a full paper and reviewed it, at a cost of less than 15 US dollars per paper [@lu2024ai].

In early 2025 the team ran an experiment with the agreement of organisers of a workshop at ICLR, a major machine-learning conference. They submitted three papers generated end to end by the second version of the system, without telling reviewers which papers were AI-written. Two were not accepted. One received scores of 6, 7 and 6, above the workshop's average acceptance threshold, and was withdrawn before publication as agreed in advance [@sakana2025ai]. The company noted that workshop papers face a lower bar than main conference papers and that its own researchers judged none of the three good enough for the main conference [@sakana2025ai].

The peer-reviewed account appeared in *Nature* in March 2026 [@lu2026towards]. It reports that the generated manuscript "passed the first round of peer review" for a workshop with an acceptance rate of 70%, and that an automated reviewer built by the team agreed with human accept/reject decisions with a balanced accuracy of 69% [@lu2026towards; @sakana2026ai]. According to the authors, the system still produces naive ideas, struggles with methodological rigour, and makes errors such as inaccurate citations. It is also limited to computational experiments [@sakana2026ai]. The abstract itself names a risk: such systems could end up "taxing overwhelmed review systems and adding noise to the scientific literature" [@lu2026towards]. The developers say they watermark the papers the system generates so that they can be identified as AI-written, and they call on the scientific community to set norms for how such work should be treated [@sakana2026ai]. Whether journals and conferences will accept AI-generated papers, and with what disclosure, remains an open question.

Kosmos, from the company Edison Scientific, takes a different design. Given a research objective and a dataset, it runs for up to 12 hours, alternating between a data-analysis agent and a literature-search agent that share a structured record of what has been found. In an average run it executes about 42,000 lines of code and reads about 1,500 papers [@mitchener2025kosmos]. Independent scientists judged 79.4% of the statements in its reports to be accurate. The breakdown is revealing: 85.5% for data-analysis statements and 82.1% for literature statements, but only 57.9% for statements that synthesise and interpret findings [@mitchener2025kosmos]. One of its reported findings, in materials science, reproduced a human result, available at the time only in a preprint that Kosmos had not accessed, that humidity during a manufacturing step strongly affects the performance of perovskite solar cells. These results are from a preprint and have not yet been peer reviewed.

The Kosmos authors also tried to estimate how much work a run replaces. Counting papers read and analyses run, and assuming fixed times per task, they estimated about 4.1 months of expert work per run. Collaborating academic groups, asked directly, estimated that the findings of a 20-cycle run would have taken them about 6 months on average, from a sample of seven [@mitchener2025kosmos]. Such estimates are informative but soft. They come from the developers and their collaborators, and the time needed to check the output is not subtracted.

The Kosmos numbers point to a general feature of AI-assisted research. The parts that can be checked mechanically (did the code run, does the cited paper say this) are the parts where the system is most reliable. Interpretation, the step where a scientist decides what a result means, is where it is least reliable, and it is also the step that readers most need to trust.

Code deserves particular attention. Much of modern science is computation, and coding assistants (Chapter 5) are now used to write research software. Kosmos writes tens of thousands of lines per run, and AlphaEvolve is itself a coding agent. The software engineer Addy Osmani uses the term **comprehension debt** for "the growing gap between how much code exists in your system and how much of it any human being genuinely understands" [@osmani2026comprehension]. In a commercial product, unread code is a maintenance problem. In research, the code *is* part of the method. If nobody has read the analysis script, nobody can say with confidence what was measured.

## 6.6 Reproducibility, understanding and intellectual debt

Machine learning brought its own reproducibility problems to science before generative AI arrived. Kapoor and Narayanan surveyed fields that had adopted machine-learning methods and found **data leakage**, in which information from the test data seeps into training so that performance looks better than it is, in 17 fields, affecting 294 papers [@kapoor2023leakage]. In their own reanalysis of political-science studies predicting the onset of civil wars, complex models had appeared to beat traditional statistics by a wide margin; once the errors were corrected, they did "not perform substantively better than decades-old" logistic regression models [@kapoor2023leakage]. They proposed "model info sheets", a checklist on which authors document how they guarded against each type of leakage.

The cases in this chapter suggest a general rule: AI-assisted claims are only as strong as the check that follows them, and checks differ in strength. A Lean proof is checked completely by a machine. A weather forecast is checked against reality within days, thousands of times a year. A predicted crystal is checked only when a skilled chemist makes and examines it, which happens for a small fraction of candidates and can take years, as the A-Lab case showed. An AI-written paper is checked by peer reviewers who are already overloaded. The inner ring of Figure 6.1 marks these checks. Where verification is cheap, AI accelerates science; where it is expensive, AI mainly accelerates the production of claims.

Several practical responses follow from this. One is to make checking part of the method: Kapoor and Narayanan's model info sheets [@kapoor2023leakage], proofs submitted with a Lean certificate [@sothanaphan2026resolution], or labelling AI-generated papers so that reviewers know what they are reading [@sakana2026ai]. Another is to value independent reanalysis. The critiques of GNoME and the A-Lab came from researchers outside both projects, and they changed what the published record says [@cheetham2024artificial; @leeman2024challenges; @szymanski2026author]. A third is to report the denominator. A system that is tried on 67 problems and improves 'several' tells us more than a single announced success, which is why the Tao collaboration's report on all 67 problems it attempted is useful [@georgiev2025mathematical].

Messeri and Crockett argue that the danger goes beyond individual errors [@messeri2024artificial]. AI tools, they write, can exploit human cognitive limits and create **illusions of understanding**, in which "we believe we understand more about the world than we actually do". They describe three such illusions. An *illusion of explanatory depth* arises when researchers who use an AI model of a phenomenon believe they understand the phenomenon more deeply than they do. An *illusion of exploratory breadth* arises when they believe they are testing all hypotheses but are testing only those that AI tools can handle. An *illusion of objectivity* arises when they treat an AI system as having no point of view, when it reflects the standpoints of its training data and its developers. The cumulative risk, they argue, is a **scientific monoculture** in which some methods and questions crowd out others, so that science may "produce more but understand less" [@messeri2024artificial].

The legal scholar Jonathan Zittrain gave a related idea a name in 2019. He observed that in some fields useful techniques have been used for decades before anyone could explain why they work. This approach, "answers first, explanations later", accrues what he calls **intellectual debt** [@zittrain2019hidden]. Debt is not always bad; borrowing lets us use what works before we know why. But debt compounds: sometimes it is paid off quickly, and sometimes we end up relying for decades on knowledge that is not fully understood. Zittrain warned that machine learning greatly expands our capacity to borrow, because it produces accurate predictions without the theories that would tell us when to stop trusting them [@zittrain2019hidden]. The weather results in Section 6.2 are a case in point: a model that forecasts well on average can fail on the unprecedented storm, and without a theory of what the model has learned, it is hard to know in advance where that boundary lies.

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Will AI make science faster but shallower?**

*Faster and deeper.* Supporters point to cases where AI output led to new understanding. AlphaFold's structures are used to form and test hypotheses, and its developers received a Nobel Prize [@royal2024nobelchemistry]. FunSearch and AlphaEvolve return programs that mathematicians can read and generalise [@romeraparedes2024mathematical; @georgiev2025mathematical]. Lean-checked proofs leave little room for error in the reasoning, provided the formal statement says what was intended [@sothanaphan2026resolution]. On this view, freeing scientists from routine work gives them more time to think, and Tao has described AI as most valuable, for now, as a time-saving assistant [@bastian2025leading].

*Faster but shallower.* Critics point to the asymmetry between generating and checking. GNoME produced hundreds of thousands of candidates, and the expert scrutiny needed to assess them took years [@cheetham2024artificial; @szymanski2026author]. Kosmos is least accurate exactly when it interprets [@mitchener2025kosmos]. Messeri and Crockett warn that the most AI-friendly questions may crowd out others [@messeri2024artificial], and Zittrain that unexplained answers accumulate as debt [@zittrain2019hidden].

*What would settle it.* Few studies measure understanding directly. Useful evidence would include: the share of AI-generated findings in a field that are later independently replicated; whether fields that adopt AI heavily narrow the range of questions and methods they study; and whether researchers using AI tools can explain their results as well as those who do not. None of these has yet been measured at scale.
:::

The cases in this chapter share a pattern. In weather forecasting, mathematics and parts of computing, AI systems have produced results that are checkable and useful. The common thread in the successful cases is a strong external check: blind competitions for protein structures, daily verification against the weather, formal proof for mathematics, and benchmarks for algorithms. Where such a check was missing, as in the materials and Erdős cases, early claims needed later correction.

## Summary
- Machine learning does four distinct jobs in science: prediction, emulation of expensive simulations, search over candidate solutions, and automation of research workflows. How easily each kind of output can be checked matters as much as the model itself.
- AlphaFold, which predicts a protein's 3D structure from its sequence, is the reference case: a large archive of checked data, a clear target and blind testing. Its developers shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
- Machine-learning weather models such as GraphCast, GenCast, ECMWF's AIFS and NOAA's AI models match or beat physics-based forecasts on most measures reported by their developers at a fraction of the computing cost. They still depend on physics-based data assimilation and can fail on extremes absent from their training data.
- In materials science, GNoME proposed millions of candidate crystals, but independent experts found little evidence of novel, credible and useful materials, and the A-Lab's synthesis claims were corrected in 2026 after reanalysis.
- Mathematics offers machine-checkable proof. AI systems went from silver-medal olympiad performance with days of computation (2024) to certified gold-medal performance in natural language (2025), and search systems such as FunSearch and AlphaEvolve have improved some best-known constructions.
- "AI scientist" systems now produce complete papers and long analyses. Published evaluations show they are most reliable on checkable steps (code, citations) and least reliable on interpretation.
- Data leakage, unread AI-written code and slow expert verification threaten reproducibility. Messeri and Crockett's "illusions of understanding" and Zittrain's "intellectual debt" describe the risk that science produces more while understanding less.

## Further reading
