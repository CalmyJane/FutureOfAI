# Chapter 1: What We Mean by "AI": Definitions and a Short History

The field of artificial intelligence began with a funding request. On 31 August 1955, John McCarthy of Dartmouth College, Marvin Minsky of Harvard, Nathaniel Rochester of IBM and Claude Shannon of Bell Telephone Laboratories asked the Rockefeller Foundation to support "a 2 month, 10 man study of artificial intelligence" the following summer, at a cost of $13,500. The proposal's central claim was far larger than its budget: that "every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it" [@mccarthy2006proposal].

Seventy years later the term is attached to products used by a large share of humanity. In late September 2026 OpenAI said that its chatbot ChatGPT, released in November 2022, had more than 1.2 billion weekly users, a company figure reported by the press and not independently verified [@bastian2026openai].

Between those two points lie at least three different ideas of how intelligent machines should be built, two collapses in funding and confidence, and a running argument about what the term should cover. That argument now has legal force. Since February 2025 the definition of an "AI system" in the EU's AI Act has decided which software the Act can reach, and false claims to use AI have drawn fines from US regulators [@europeanparliament2024regulation, art. 113; @sec2024sec]. The history that follows concentrates on the United States and Britain, where most of the founding work and funding was concentrated; Soviet, Japanese and continental European research traditions are left aside.

## 1.1 From Turing's question to the Dartmouth proposal

### Turing's behavioural test

Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing machinery and intelligence" opens with the sentence "I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'". Turing judged the words "machine" and "think" too vague to define usefully, so he replaced the question with a test he called the imitation game. A human interrogator exchanges typed messages with a hidden person and a hidden machine and tries to tell which is which. Turing predicted that in about fifty years computers with a storage capacity of about 10^9^ would play the game well. An average interrogator would then have no more than a 70% chance of making the right identification after five minutes of questioning [@turing1950computing].

Turing defined intelligence by what a system does, judged from outside, not by what goes on inside it. The weakness of that approach was visible from the start. A behavioural test says nothing about whether the machine reaches its answers in a way that resembles human thought. Modern legal definitions keep Turing's focus on outputs but add a condition about mechanism: the system must *infer* how to produce them. As Section 1.2 shows, that condition, not behaviour, decides the hard cases. The question of what goes on inside remains open for today's systems. A 2022 survey of researchers in natural language processing (NLP) found the field split almost evenly on whether a model trained only on text could understand language "in some nontrivial sense" [@michael2023what; @mitchell2023debate]; Section 3.7 sets out that dispute.

### What the Dartmouth proposal claimed

The 1955 proposal is more specific than its famous opening sentence suggests. It listed seven "aspects of the artificial intelligence problem": automatic computers, programming a computer to use a language, "neuron nets", a theory of the size of a calculation, self-improvement, abstractions, and randomness and creativity [@mccarthy2006proposal]. On computing power the authors were confident that hardware was not the bottleneck. "The major obstacle is not lack of machine capacity, but our inability to write programs taking full advantage of what we have." On language they speculated that "a large part of human thought consists of manipulating words according to rules of reasoning and rules of conjecture", close to a programme statement for the symbolic AI of Section 1.4. Their fourth topic anticipated the main obstacle of the next two decades. Trying all possible answers in order is "inefficient", so a theory of the efficiency of calculation was needed to avoid it.

The proposal's stated goal was modest: "a significant advance can be made in one or more of these problems if a carefully selected group of scientists work on it together for a summer" [@mccarthy2006proposal]. The workshop ran from 18 June to 17 August 1956, according to an account by Grace Solomonoff based on the papers of her husband, the participant Ray Solomonoff, which describes "a few dozen" thinkers who came and went [@solomonoff2023meeting]. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy counts ten attendees, Allen Newell and Herbert Simon among them [@bringsjord2018artificial]. Early in the summer Newell and Simon presented their Logic Theorist, a program that proved elementary theorems in propositional logic and that the attendees regarded as a remarkable achievement [@solomonoff2023meeting; @bringsjord2018artificial]. Its designers stressed that, unlike the systematic algorithms then normal in computing, it "relies heavily on heuristic methods similar to those that have been observed in human problem solving" [@newell1956logic]. That combination of symbols, rules and heuristic search defined the field for its first thirty years.

### Researchers' definitions

Definitions written by researchers have stayed loose. The AI pioneer Nils Nilsson described AI as "that activity devoted to making machines intelligent", and intelligence as the quality that enables an entity "to function appropriately and with foresight in its environment". The One Hundred Year Study on AI, a long-term project based at Stanford University, adopted this formulation in its 2016 report [@stone2016artificial]. The difficulty is that "intelligence" has no agreed definition either. In 2007 Shane Legg and Marcus Hutter compiled "70-odd" informal definitions of intelligence from psychologists, AI researchers and dictionaries, and distilled them into one: "Intelligence measures an agent's ability to achieve goals in a wide range of environments" [@legg2007collection]. Their summary points to the property that most AI systems of the past seventy years lacked, namely breadth. A chess program, a medical expert system or an image classifier achieves goals in one environment only.

### Narrow and general AI

Breadth is what separates **narrow AI**, a system that performs one task or a small family of tasks, from **artificial general intelligence (AGI)**, a hypothetical system that matches or exceeds people across most intellectual tasks. Every system in this chapter's history before 2018 was narrow. The term AGI has no agreed definition, and the definitions in circulation set very different bars. Legg and Hutter's breadth criterion describes a scale on which systems can rank higher or lower, not a threshold. OpenAI's charter of 2018 defines AGI economically, as "highly autonomous systems that outperform humans at most economically valuable work" [@openai2018charter]. In a 2024 conference paper, researchers at Google DeepMind, Legg among them, proposed levels instead. A "Competent AGI" would perform at least as well as the median skilled adult on most cognitive tasks. In September 2023 they rated frontier language models "Emerging AGI": competent at some tasks, such as short essays and simple coding, but not at most [@morris2024position]. Because the definitions differ, claims that AGI is near or far often disagree about the target, not the evidence. No law defines AGI. The EU AI Act comes closest with two terms: the **general-purpose AI model** (Section 1.3), and "systemic risk", the risk posed by models whose capabilities "match or exceed" those of the most advanced models, a status presumed once training has used more than 10^25^ operations [@europeanparliament2024regulation, arts. 3(64)–3(65), 51].

## 1.2 Official definitions and where they draw the line

Definitions written for governments cannot be loose, because they decide which systems a law covers, and the boundary they draw cuts across the field's own history. Under the European Commission's 2025 guidelines to the EU AI Act, a rule-based medical adviser of the 1970s would normally qualify as an AI system, while a chess program built on search plus a hand-tuned evaluation, the technique behind the most famous AI event of the 1990s, may not [@europeancommission2025guidelines, paras 39, 48]. The definitions in widest use are the OECD's, the EU AI Act's and the US definition in the National AI Initiative Act of 2020.

### The OECD definition and its revision

The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), an intergovernmental organisation of mostly high-income countries, first defined an AI system in its 2019 AI Principles, building on the textbook view of AI in Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig's *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach*. That definition covered systems that, "for a given set of human-defined objectives", make "predictions, recommendations, or decisions influencing real or virtual environments" [@oecd2024explanatory, p. 4]. Member countries approved a revision in November 2023. An explanatory memorandum published in March 2024 sets out the final text [@oecd2024explanatory, p. 4]:

> An AI system is a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments. Different AI systems vary in their levels of autonomy and adaptiveness after deployment.

Replacing "human-defined objectives" with "explicit or implicit objectives" brings in systems whose goals are never written down, such as a language model rewarded for plausible responses or a recommender system that gradually narrows down a model of a user's preferences [@oecd2024explanatory, p. 7]. "Content" joined the outputs because generative systems producing text, images, audio and video "have gained significant momentum" [@oecd2024explanatory, p. 9]. The word "infers" became the core of the definition; according to the memorandum it covers both the moment a system produces an output and the earlier "build phase" in which a model is derived from data [@oecd2024explanatory, p. 9].

### The EU AI Act and the Commission's guidelines

The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, defines an AI system in Article 3(1) in almost the same words as the revised OECD text [@europeanparliament2024regulation, art. 3(1)]. The match is deliberate, since the Act wants its definition to be "closely aligned with the work of international organisations working on AI" [@europeanparliament2024regulation, recital 12]. Recital 12 also says what is excluded: "simpler traditional software systems" and systems based on rules defined solely by people to execute operations automatically. Logic- and knowledge-based approaches that infer from encoded knowledge are included (Chapter 13 discusses the Act's obligations).

Because the boundary matters legally, the European Commission issued guidelines on the definition, published in February 2025 and formally adopted on 29 July 2025 [@europeancommission2025publishes; @europeancommission2025guidelines]. They split it into seven elements, from being machine-based to influencing physical or virtual environments [@europeancommission2025guidelines, para 9]. The element that does the work is inference. A spam filter trained on examples of junk mail derives from data how to respond and fits the definition; a fixed tax formula in a spreadsheet only executes rules a person wrote down and does not. The guidelines then name four kinds of system that "may" fall outside the definition despite some capacity to infer [@europeancommission2025guidelines, para 41]. Two matter for this chapter. Methods that improve established mathematical optimisation, "such as linear or logistic regression methods", do not "transcend 'basic data processing'", and one indication of this is that a method "has been used in consolidated manner for many years" [@europeancommission2025guidelines, para 42]. Systems based on "classical heuristics" are the second case. The guidelines' example is "a chess program using a minimax algorithm with heuristic evaluation functions", which applies predefined rules "without requiring prior learning from data" [@europeancommission2025guidelines, para 48]. The other two kinds are basic data processing, such as database queries, and simple prediction systems that a basic statistical rule could match [@europeancommission2025guidelines, paras 46, 49–50].

At the same time the guidelines count "early generation expert systems intended for medical diagnosis" and "search and optimisation methods" among the logic- and knowledge-based approaches that are inside the definition [@europeancommission2025guidelines, para 39]; the line between included search methods and excluded "classical heuristics" is what places an early medical expert system inside and a minimax chess program possibly outside. The guidelines admit the limits of the exercise. They are "not binding", only the Court of Justice of the EU can give an authoritative interpretation, and "no automatic determination or exhaustive lists" of systems inside or outside the definition are possible. They also stress that "the vast majority" of systems that do qualify face no obligations under the Act [@europeancommission2025guidelines, paras 7, 62, 63].

### The US definition

US federal law uses an older formula. The National Artificial Intelligence Initiative Act of 2020, passed as part of a defence bill and enacted on 1 January 2021, defines AI in the OECD's 2019 words, almost verbatim, and adds that such systems "use model inference to formulate options for information or action" [@uscongress2021national]. The US statutory text therefore predates both generative "content" and the shift from human-defined to implicit objectives.

## 1.3 A family of terms

Public discussion often uses "AI" as if it named a single technology. It is an umbrella term for a family of methods whose names overlap. Figure 1.1 shows the most common arrangement as nested regions.

![Figure 1.1: The usual nesting of AI terms. Machine learning is one approach within AI; deep learning is one kind of machine learning; large language models and other generative foundation models are, today, mostly built with deep learning. Agents are drawn across the boundaries because the term describes how a model is used. Schematic, not to scale. Diagram by the author.](figures/fig-01-01.svg){#fig:01-01}

**Machine learning** is the approach in which a system improves at a task by learning from data or experience instead of being given explicit rules. Arthur Samuel's 1959 study of checkers (draughts) is a classic early example. Samuel showed that "a computer can be programmed so that it will learn to play a better game of checkers than can be played by the person who wrote the program", and that it could do so in 8 to 10 hours of playing time [@samuel1959studies].

**Deep learning** is machine learning with artificial neural networks that have many layers. A neural network is a computing structure loosely inspired by the brain, made of simple connected units whose connection strengths are adjusted during training. In the words of a 2015 review, deep learning "allows computational models that are composed of multiple processing layers to learn representations of data with multiple levels of abstraction" [@lecun2015deep]. Chapter 2 explains how such networks learn.

**Generative AI** refers to systems whose main output is new content: text, images, audio, video or computer code. A system that only sorts emails into "spam" and "not spam" is not generative, because it produces a label instead of new content. Section 1.7 traces the techniques that made such output convincing.

A **foundation model** is a large model trained on broad data, usually with self-supervision at scale, that can be adapted to a wide range of downstream tasks, the specific applications built on top of it. The term was introduced in 2021 in a report by more than 100 researchers, posted as a preprint, who chose the word to stress the models' "critically central yet incomplete character" [@bommasani2021opportunities]. Self-supervision means that the training signal comes from the data itself, so that no person has to label each example. A model can be asked to predict the next word of a text from the words before it, the method behind the GPT models and most chatbots, or to fill in a word hidden in the middle of a sentence, the method behind BERT [@brown2020language; @devlin2019bert]. The EU AI Act uses a related legal term, the **general-purpose AI model**: "an AI model, including where such an AI model is trained with a large amount of data using self-supervision at scale, that displays significant generality and is capable of competently performing a wide range of distinct tasks" [@europeanparliament2024regulation, art. 3(63)].

A **large language model (LLM)** is a foundation model trained mainly on text to predict the next token, a word or piece of a word, in a sequence (Chapter 3 explains the mechanism). An **AI agent** is a system that pursues a goal over several steps by taking actions, such as searching the web, running code or filling in forms, and observing the results. The idea is old. A 1995 survey already described the agent concept as having "become important in both artificial intelligence and mainstream computer science" [@wooldridge1995intelligent]. What is new since 2024 is that agents are commonly built by placing an LLM inside a loop with tools and memory (Chapter 5).

The nesting in Figure 1.1 is a simplification. Not every generative model is a deep neural network, and not every foundation model is generative. BERT, one of the original examples, was mainly used to classify and analyse text [@bommasani2021opportunities]. "Agent" describes how a model is used, not a technique, so it cuts across the nested regions. And the outermost region contains far more than machine learning, including the symbolic methods described next, which dominated the field for its first three decades.

## 1.4 Symbolic AI: search, logic and rules

### Symbols and search

In **symbolic AI**, knowledge is written down explicitly as symbols and rules, and the computer reasons by manipulating them. Most of the work was done by logical inference, which derives new statements from old ones, and by search, which explores a space of possible states (board positions, partial proofs, plans) for one that meets a goal. The difficulty with search was quantified before the field had a name. In 1950 Shannon estimated that a typical chess position offers "of the order of 30 legal moves", so that one move by each side gives about 10^3^ possibilities, and a game of about 40 moves yields some 10^120^ variations from the initial position. A machine checking one variation per microsecond "would require over 10^90^ years to calculate the first move" [@shannon1950programming], a deliberately cautious figure, since 10^120^ microseconds is about 3 × 10^106^ years. Every symbolic system therefore needed heuristics, rules of thumb such as an evaluation of material and position, that prune the search to a manageable size. The Logic Theorist's reliance on "heuristic methods" was the same answer applied to proofs [@newell1956logic].

Predictions in this period were bold. As quoted by Melanie Mitchell, in an essay posted as a preprint, Herbert Simon declared in 1960 that "Machines will be capable, within twenty years, of doing any work that a man can do", and in 1967 Minsky forecast that "Within a generation...the problems of creating 'artificial intelligence' will be substantially solved" [@mitchell2021why]. Enthusiasm also surrounded early neural networks, until Minsky and Seymour Papert's 1969 book *Perceptrons* showed the limits of the single-layer networks of the time [@minsky1969perceptrons] (Chapter 2 gives the details).

### The first winter

The term **AI winter** describes a period of reduced funding, falling confidence and declining interest in AI research. The first is usually dated to the mid-1970s. In the United Kingdom the Science Research Council asked the mathematician Sir James Lighthill to assess the field. His report, dated July 1972 and published in 1973, concluded that "in no part of the field have the discoveries made so far produced the major impact that was then promised", and among the causes he named a "failure to recognise the implications of the combinatorial explosion" [@lighthill1973artificial]. The combinatorial explosion is the problem Shannon had quantified. The number of possibilities grows exponentially with the size of a problem, so methods that work on a toy version fail on a realistic one. According to Mitchell, the Lighthill report and a similarly negative review for the US Department of Defense "led to sharp funding decreases and a downturn in enthusiasm for AI in both countries" [@mitchell2021why]. How deep this winter was is disputed. The historian of computing Thomas Haigh argues, in a column for *Communications of the ACM*, that the research community grew steadily through the 1970s [@haigh2024how]. The computer scientist James Hendler, in a journal editorial, argues that the cuts acted slowly, their effects felt only in the mid-to-late 1980s, when "the seed corn had been consumed" [@hendler2008avoiding].

### Production rules: how MYCIN reasoned

The most successful symbolic idea of the 1970s was the **production rule**, a single if-then statement of expert knowledge, and the **expert system** built from hundreds of them. MYCIN, developed at Stanford University in the 1970s to advise on antibiotic treatment for serious infections, is the best-documented example, because its builders published a book-length account of it. A typical rule, as printed in that account, reads:

> IF: 1) The gram stain of the organism is gramneg, and 2) The morphology of the organism is rod, and 3) The aerobicity of the organism is anaerobic\
> THEN: There is suggestive evidence (.6) that the identity of the organism is bacteroides [@buchanan1984rule, p. 71]

The number 0.6 is a **certainty factor (CF)**: a number from −1 (definitely false) to +1 (definitely true) that the expert attached to the rule [@buchanan1984rule, p. 81]. The rule base grew to about 500 rules [@buchanan1984rule, p. 71]. MYCIN ran them by **backward chaining**. To establish a goal, such as the identity of an organism, it tried every rule whose conclusion bore on that goal, set up subgoals for facts it lacked, and asked the physician only when no rule could supply the answer [@buchanan1984rule, p. 71].

When several rules bore on the same hypothesis, their certainty factors had to be combined. In the original model of 1975, MYCIN kept a measure of belief (MB) and a measure of disbelief (MD). Each new piece of supporting evidence of strength *x* raised the belief *b* to *b* + *x*(1 − *b*), and the certainty factor was CF = MB − MD. Table 1.2 reproduces the worked example from the system's documentation.

| Step | Evidence about the hypothesis "the organism is a Streptococcus" | MB | MD | CF = MB − MD |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | First rule supports it with CF 0.3 | 0.30 | 0 | 0.30 |
| 2 | Second rule supports it with CF 0.2: 0.3 + 0.2 × (1 − 0.3) | 0.44 | 0 | 0.44 |
| 3 | Third rule counts against it with CF −0.1 | 0.44 | 0.10 | 0.34 |

: Table 1.2: MYCIN's original (1975) combining rule. Two weak supporting rules give more belief than either alone, but never more than 1; contrary evidence is kept separately and subtracted. Under the revised rule used from about 1977, step 3 gives 0.34/(1 − 0.1) ≈ 0.38. Source: [@buchanan1984rule, pp. 216, 260]. {#tbl:01-02}

The scheme was a pragmatic substitute for probability theory, and it proved brittle. Eight or nine supporting rules could drive belief to about 0.999, and a single contrary rule of strength 0.8 would then cut the certainty factor to 0.199, below the 0.2 threshold MYCIN used to accept a conclusion. The developers called this behaviour "counterintuitive" and noted that it "occasionally led MYCIN to reach incorrect inferences". From about 1977 they used a revised formula, CF = (MB − MD)/(1 − min(MB, MD)), and stored only a running certainty factor for each hypothesis [@buchanan1984rule, pp. 211, 215–216].

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: MYCIN's blinded evaluation (1979) and why it was never used**

In a study published in 1979, MYCIN's recommendations for ten real cases of meningitis were compared with those of nine human prescribers, including five infectious-disease faculty specialists at Stanford. Eight experts at other institutions rated each prescription without knowing which came from the computer. MYCIN's prescriptions were rated acceptable 65% of the time; the five faculty specialists scored between 42.5% and 62.5% [@yu1979antimicrobial]. The study was small, confined to one infection and judged by expert agreement rather than patient outcomes.

MYCIN never reached the wards. Its developers wrote that although testing on hospital wards had been "explicitly our initial intention", the infectious-disease knowledge base "was laid to rest in 1978" despite its performance [@buchanan1984rule, p. 673]. Their reasons were practical. MYCIN ran only on laboratory computers that were still "too expensive" for hospitals, and its narrow knowledge and awkward interface meant it "would not be a cost-effective tool for physicians to use on such narrow problem areas as meningitis and bacteremia" [@buchanan1984rule, pp. 698–699, 701–702]. They also declined to publish the rules as medical guidance, because the rules omitted the causal reasoning behind them and were "not readable as text" [@buchanan1984rule, p. 702]. The gap between expert-level accuracy and real use recurs in clinical AI today (Chapter 10).

## 1.5 The expert-systems boom and the second winter

### The boom

In the early 1980s expert systems became an industry. The key commercial example was XCON (also called R1), built by John McDermott of Carnegie Mellon University for Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC). From 1980 DEC used it to configure its VAX-11 computer systems from customers' orders [@bachant1984r1]. According to Haigh, its first release condensed expert knowledge into 480 configuration rules, and DEC claimed that XCON and a related system saved more than $40 million a year [@haigh2024how]. Startups sold expert-system "shells" (an inference engine with an empty rule base, as EMYCIN did for MYCIN's engine), consulting services and workstations designed to run the AI programming language Lisp efficiently [@haigh2024how].

Governments joined in. Japan's Fifth Generation computing project prompted the US Strategic Computing Initiative, launched by the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) in 1983, and the United Kingdom's Alvey programme [@haigh2024how; @mitchell2021why]. In Haigh's account, Strategic Computing rested on the assumption that existing approaches to expert systems, language understanding and vision were ready for large-scale use once hardware improved [@haigh2024how].

Some researchers were already worried. At the 1984 conference of the American Association for Artificial Intelligence, a panel discussed "deep unease among AI researchers" that expectations were too high and that this "will eventually result in disaster" [@mcdermott1985dark]. One panellist, Drew McDermott, sketched a collapse in which "everybody hurriedly changes the names of their research projects to something else", a scenario he said some called the "AI Winter". About five years later, in the late 1980s, a second winter set in [@mitchell2021why].

### Why expert systems failed economically

**Brittleness.** Expert systems were "often unable to generalize or adapt when faced with new situations", because the experts writing the rules relied on unspoken common-sense knowledge that never reached the program [@mitchell2021why]. MYCIN's builders made a related admission. Rule-based systems, they wrote, are "sometimes characterized as 'shallow' reasoning systems in which the rules encode no causal knowledge", which they called "largely (but not entirely) true of MYCIN" [@buchanan1984rule, p. 672].

**Maintenance.** Rules had to be extracted from experts by "knowledge engineers" and kept current by hand. Companies that bought expert-system software found that automating expertise required them to hire new experts to maintain the rules. By 1989 DEC had 59 technical staff assigned to maintaining the infrastructure and rules of its internal expert systems, the most publicised application of AI at the time, and a shortage of AI specialists had pushed up wages. Few companies could sustain such costs [@haigh2024how].

**Hardware.** The early boom ran on Lisp machines. Hendler recalls that ordinary computers soon ran Lisp "with good speed at a lower price", and that rule engines written in other languages "didn't need a $50,000 special-purpose machine to run on". In his account, "by roughly 1987, the Lisp machine market was dead" [@hendler2008avoiding].

**Funding and expectations.** At DARPA, speech recognition progressed but most other Strategic Computing projects disappointed. At the end of 1987 DARPA abandoned the programme's flagship, an autonomous land vehicle [@haigh2024how]. Hendler adds a self-inflicted wound. As funding tightened, researchers began to dismiss expert systems as "not really AI", so that the community "avowed that the stuff that worked well wasn't really AI" [@hendler2008avoiding].

The market for expert-system software, in Haigh's words, "proved unsustainable" [@haigh2024how]. The most ambitious attempt to escape brittleness, Douglas Lenat's Cyc project, set out in 1983 to write down common sense on a large scale. Lenat estimated that encoding an encyclopedia's worth of knowledge would take about 2,000 person-years; the project was carried on by a private company, its knowledge base growing to some 30 million rules, without fulfilling the original vision [@haigh2024how]. Many researchers concluded that knowledge written down by hand does not scale. Section 1.7 returns to whether that lesson was right.

## 1.6 The statistical turn

### Speech: from rules to hidden Markov models

The method that replaced hand-written rules came first from speech recognition, and it came partly from IBM. In 1976 the IBM researcher Frederick Jelinek described continuous speech recognition by statistical models whose parameters were estimated from data [@jelinek1976continuous], and Lalit Bahl, Jelinek and Robert Mercer later formulated it explicitly "as a problem of maximum likelihood decoding", giving "special attention to determining the parameters for such models from sparse data" [@bahl1983maximum].

The core model was the **hidden Markov model (HMM)**. The idea is that a word is produced by a sequence of hidden states (roughly, the stages of articulating its sounds), each of which emits observable acoustic measurements with some probability. Given an audio signal, the recogniser asks which sequence of hidden states, and hence which words, would most probably have produced it. All the probabilities are learned from recorded and transcribed speech by an iterative algorithm, so nobody has to write rules about how a vowel sounds. A retrospective by three of the field's builders, Xuedong Huang, James Baker and Raj Reddy, describes this as "a pivotal change in the representation of knowledge in speech recognition". In their account, systems such as Dragon, Harpy and Sphinx were built on "a single, relatively simple modeling principle of joint global optimization", in contrast to earlier designs of cooperating knowledge modules [@huang2014historical; @rabiner1989tutorial]. Progress was measured by the word error rate in DARPA-funded evaluations [@huang2014historical].

### Translation as decoding

At the end of the 1980s the IBM group applied the same thinking to translation. Peter Brown and colleagues treated a French sentence as an English sentence that had passed through a noisy channel. They searched for the English sentence *S* that maximised Pr(*S*) × Pr(*T*|*S*): the probability of *S* as English, given by a language model, times the probability that it would be translated as the observed French *T*, given by a translation model [@brown1990statistical]. Both were learned from the proceedings of the Canadian parliament, kept by law in English and French.

The learned parameters showed what data alone could reveal. The most probable French translation of "hear" turned out to be "bravo", because English-speaking members of parliament shout "Hear, hear!" where French speakers say "Bravo!" [@brown1990statistical]. The first results were modest. With a vocabulary of about a thousand words in each language, 48% of 73 new French sentences came out reasonable and only 5% exactly as the human translation [@brown1990statistical]. The paper mattered for its method, which learned everything from aligned text.

### More data, simpler models

Through the 1990s the field's centre of gravity moved from writing knowledge down to estimating it. Mitchell describes "the meteoric rise of machine learning" in the 1990s and 2000s, with methods "inspired by statistics rather than by neuroscience or psychology", and notes that practitioners distanced themselves from the "then-discredited" label AI [@mitchell2021why]. Support vector machines, introduced in their modern form by Corinna Cortes and Vladimir Vapnik in 1995, became a standard tool for classification [@cortes1995support]. Neural networks had not disappeared, but for two decades they were one method among many. John Hopfield showed in 1982 how a network of simple connected units could store memories and recall them from fragments [@hopfield1982neural]. A 1986 paper in *Nature* by David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams popularised **backpropagation**, the method still used to train neural networks [@rumelhart1986learning]. Long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, introduced in 1997, learned patterns spread over long sequences [@hochreiter1997long], and from the late 1990s a convolutional network read handwritten amounts on bank cheques commercially [@lecun1998gradient] (Chapter 2). Networks with many layers, however, remained hard to train until 2006, when Hinton and colleagues showed how to train them one layer at a time [@hinton2006fast]. That result revived interest in what came to be called deep learning.

Researchers at Microsoft and Google stated the new attitude towards data most plainly. In 2001 Michele Banko and Eric Brill of Microsoft Research trained four learning methods to choose between commonly confused words (such as "then" and "than") on up to one billion words of text, a thousand times more than had been used for this problem before. All four kept improving as the data grew, and the authors concluded that "we may want to reconsider the trade-off between spending time and money on algorithm development versus spending it on corpus development" [@banko2001scaling].

In 2009 three Google researchers, Alon Halevy, Peter Norvig and Fernando Pereira, pointed to a trillion-word corpus of web text, with counts of all word sequences up to five words long. Despite its errors, it was a million times larger than the classic Brown Corpus. They summarised the lesson: "simple models and a lot of data trump more elaborate models based on less data" [@halevy2009unreasonable].

### Deep Blue and ImageNet

The most widely publicised AI event of the 1990s belonged to the older tradition. The world champion Garry Kasparov had beaten IBM's chess computer Deep Blue by four games to two in 1996, but in the rematch in New York in May 1997 Deep Blue won by 3.5 to 2.5. IBM describes it as the first defeat of a reigning world champion by a computer in a match under standard tournament time controls. In IBM's words, Deep Blue "derived its chess prowess through brute force computing power": 32 processors evaluating 200 million chess positions per second, built with the goal of beating the world's best human player [@ibm2025deep]. It was a search engine in the mould Shannon had sketched in 1950, and the kind of system that the EU's 2025 guidelines suggest may fall outside the legal definition of AI.

A quieter development turned out to matter more. In 2009 Jia Deng, Fei-Fei Li and colleagues introduced ImageNet, a large database of labelled photographs organised by category [@deng2009imagenet]. From 2010 an annual competition, the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge (ILSVRC), asked teams to classify images into 1,000 categories using roughly 1.2 million training images [@russakovsky2015imagenet]. A large, shared dataset with agreed scoring gave computer vision a common yardstick of the kind the DARPA speech evaluations had provided, and it gave data-hungry methods something to learn from.

## 1.7 Deep learning, foundation models and assistants (2011–2026)

### Deep learning's breakthroughs, 2011–2017

Deep learning's first large success of the 2010s came in speech. An HMM recogniser contains a component that scores how well each short slice of audio matches each speech sound, traditionally a statistical model called a Gaussian mixture. In 2011 Frank Seide, Gang Li and Dong Yu of Microsoft replaced it with a deep neural network, pre-trained layer by layer in the manner introduced in 2006 and then trained on 309 hours of recorded telephone conversations. On a public benchmark of phone-call transcription (Switchboard), the word error rate fell from 27.4% for a conventional system trained on the same data to 18.5%, "a 33% relative improvement" [@seide2011conversational]. By 2014 researchers at both Microsoft and IBM were reporting their best Switchboard results with systems following this deep-learning framework [@huang2014historical].

In the 2012 ImageNet challenge, a University of Toronto team, Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton, entered a deep convolutional neural network (a design explained in Section 2.6) now called AlexNet. It achieved a top-5 error rate of 15.3%, against 26.2% for the second-best entry [@krizhevsky2012imagenet]. (The winning entry used additional pre-training data; with only the official training data the error was 16.4%, the value shown in Figure 1.2 [@russakovsky2015imagenet].) The network had 60 million adjustable parameters and was trained for five to six days on two Nvidia GeForce GTX 580 graphics cards [@krizhevsky2012imagenet]. Graphics processing units (GPUs) were designed for computer graphics, but they perform many simple calculations at once, which is what training a neural network requires (Section 2.6).

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: What "top-5 error" measures**

In the ImageNet classification task, each test image has one correct label from 1,000 categories, such as a particular breed of dog. A system may submit five guesses per image. The top-5 error is the percentage of test images for which none of the five guesses matches the correct label. Five guesses are allowed because many photographs contain several objects and only one is labelled. An error of 15.3% therefore means that AlexNet's five guesses all missed for about 15 of every 100 test images. The organisers note that top-5 error, top-1 error and a hierarchical measure ranked the entries in the same order, which is why they adopted the simplest [@russakovsky2015imagenet].
:::

The winning error rate (counting only entries that used the official training data) fell from 28.2% in 2010 to 6.7% in 2014, and the drop after 2012 coincided with the switch to deep convolutional networks [@russakovsky2015imagenet]. In 2015 a "residual network" with up to 152 layers reached 3.57% [@he2016deep].

The human comparison in Figure 1.2 needs care. The organisers estimated that one expert annotator, who had trained on 500 images, made top-5 errors on 5.1% of 1,500 test images; a second annotator labelled only 258 images [@russakovsky2015imagenet]. In 2015 a team led by Kaiming He reported 4.94% and described the result as "the first to surpass human-level performance (5.1%...)" on the challenge [@he2015delving]. The claim, and the headlines it inspired, rested on one benchmark and essentially one annotator, an early instance of a pattern Chapter 4 examines. Benchmark results are real but measure less than headlines suggest.

In January 2016 David Silver and colleagues at Google DeepMind described AlphaGo, which combined two deep networks, one proposing promising moves and one estimating the chance of winning from a position, with a tree search. The networks were trained first on games by human experts and then by "reinforcement learning from games of self-play", that is, by trial and error against versions of itself with winning as the reward. AlphaGo beat the European champion Fan Hui by five games to nothing, "a feat previously thought to be at least a decade away" [@silver2016mastering]. In March 2016 it beat Lee Sedol, one of the world's strongest players, by four games to one in Seoul, as *Science News* reported [@sumner2016alphago]. AlphaGo is a reminder that the traditions merged, since its learned networks guided a tree search, the core technique of symbolic game-playing programs.

In June 2017 eight researchers, most of them at Google, proposed the **transformer**, a network for processing sequences "based solely on attention mechanisms, dispensing with recurrence and convolutions entirely". On a standard English-to-German translation test it scored 28.4 on BLEU, a measure of how many word sequences a machine translation shares with human reference translations, more than two points above the best previous results, with significantly less training time [@vaswani2017attention]. Because a transformer processes all the words of a passage at once rather than one after another, at least during training and when reading a prompt, it uses parallel hardware efficiently, which made much larger models practical. Chapter 3 explains how attention works.

### Pre-training and foundation models

Between 2018 and 2020 a new recipe emerged: pre-train one large transformer on unlabelled text by self-supervision, then adapt it to many tasks. Table 1.4 traces the first generation, in which each model was larger than the last and needed less task-specific training. OpenAI's GPT-1 and Google's BERT, both from 2018, set new records on many language-understanding tasks after being adapted to each one. On GLUE, a suite of nine language-understanding tests, BERT scored 80.5 against GPT-1's 72.8 [@radford2018improving; @devlin2019bert]. GPT-1 and its successor were described in company reports that were not peer-reviewed. OpenAI reported that GPT-2 (2019) set state-of-the-art results on most language-modelling datasets "in a zero-shot setting", without any task-specific training [@radford2019language]. GPT-3 (2020), with 175 billion parameters, "10x more than any previous non-sparse language model", could carry out tasks such as translation, question answering and simple arithmetic when given only a few examples in its prompt, which its authors called few-shot learning [@brown2020language].

Between GPT-1 and GPT-3, in two years, model size grew by a factor of about 1,500. Chapter 4 explains the scaling laws, first set out in a 2020 preprint [@kaplan2020scaling], that describe how a language model's loss, its prediction error on held-out text, falls as parameters, data and compute increase. GPT-3 and its peers prompted the Stanford group mentioned in Section 1.3 to coin "foundation model" in 2021. Their report warned that the defects of a foundation model are "inherited by all the adapted models downstream", because many applications rest on the same few base models [@bommasani2021opportunities].

### Assistants, multimodal and open models

Three developments after 2021 turned these models into products. The first was training them to follow instructions. In 2022 OpenAI researchers described InstructGPT, a version of GPT-3 fine-tuned first on answers written by people and then on people's rankings of its answers, a method called **reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)**. Human raters preferred the answers of a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT model to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3 [@ouyang2022training]. ChatGPT, released as a "research preview" in November 2022, put a model post-trained with methods of this kind behind a free chat window (Section 3.5) [@chatterji2025how]. By July 2025 about 700 million people used it each week, roughly 10% of the world's adults, according to a working paper co-written by OpenAI staff from the company's own data [@chatterji2025how].

The second was the move to larger models that handle images as well as text. OpenAI released GPT-4 in March 2023 with a technical report that withheld its size, training data and training method. According to the company, it accepted images as input and passed a simulated bar exam with a score around the top 10% of test takers [@openai2023gpt4]. The third was the spread of **open-weight models**, whose trained parameters are published for anyone to download, run and modify. Meta released its LLaMA models in February 2023 and reported that the 13-billion-parameter version outperformed GPT-3 on most benchmarks [@touvron2023llama]. The Chinese company DeepSeek reported in December 2024 that its open-weight DeepSeek-V3 performed comparably to leading closed models; Chapter 7 examines its claims about training cost [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3].

Image generation followed a parallel path. Generative adversarial networks, introduced in 2014, train a generating network against a second network that tries to tell its output from real data [@goodfellow2014generative]. Diffusion models, which became practical in 2020, learn to turn random noise step by step into an image by reversing a process that gradually adds noise [@ho2020denoising]. By 2022 diffusion models guided by text descriptions produced detailed images from short prompts; latent diffusion, one widely used variant, runs the process on a compressed representation of the image to reduce cost [@rombach2022high].

Deep learning also produced a scientific landmark. DeepMind's AlphaFold 2 predicted the three-dimensional structure of proteins from their amino-acid sequences with accuracy "competitive with experimental structures in a majority of cases" [@jumper2021highly]. Recognition followed. The 2018 Turing Award, computing's highest honour, went to Yoshua Bengio, Geoffrey Hinton and Yann LeCun for breakthroughs that made deep neural networks "a critical component of computing" [@acm2019fathers]. In 2024 John Hopfield and Hinton shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for discoveries that enable machine learning with neural networks, and Demis Hassabis and John Jumper shared half of the chemistry prize for protein structure prediction [@royal2024nobelphysics; @royal2024nobelchemistry].

### Reasoning models and agents

Use of these assistants spread quickly, by measures that count different things. In a peer-reviewed article, Alexander Bick, Adam Blandin and David Deming found that, measured from each technology's first mass-market product, adoption of generative AI "has outpaced both PCs and the internet" [@bick2026rapid]. By May 2026, according to the same team's surveys as reported in a Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis blog post, 62% of US adults aged 18 to 64 used it [@bick2026what]. Microsoft's own usage telemetry, which counts users of its tools rather than survey answers, ranked the United States only 21st by the share of its population using AI in early 2026 [@lavistaferres2026state]. Chapter 8 examines adoption and its economic effects.

**Reasoning models**, introduced in 2024 and 2025, are language models trained to produce a long chain of intermediate steps before answering. OpenAI's o1 models, according to the company, are "trained with large-scale reinforcement learning to reason using chain of thought" [@openai2024o1]. DeepSeek reported in *Nature* that such abilities "can be incentivized through pure reinforcement learning". That held for an experimental model, DeepSeek-R1-Zero; the released DeepSeek-R1 was also trained on thousands of written examples of step-by-step reasoning [@guo2025deepseek]. Whether these step-by-step outputs are reasoning in the human sense is an open question (Section 3.7).

From late 2024 came agents built around language models. In November 2024 Anthropic released the Model Context Protocol, which it describes as "an open standard for connecting AI assistants to the systems where data lives" [@anthropic2024introducing]. Researchers at METR, an organisation that evaluates AI systems, measured the length of software tasks, in skilled-human working time, that AI agents complete with 50% success. In a study first posted in March 2025 they reported that this "time horizon" had been "doubling approximately every seven months since 2019" (Chapter 4 discusses later revisions) [@kwa2025measuring]. On OSWorld, a benchmark of real computer tasks published in 2024, people completed about 72% of tasks and the best model about 12% [@xie2024osworld]. By early 2026, according to Stanford's annual AI Index report, the best agent reached 66.3% [@sajadieh2026ai]. On the official leaderboard for a corrected task set, OSWorld-Verified, whose scores are not directly comparable with the original, the best general-purpose model reached about 86% by August 2026 [@osworld2026verified]. Chapter 5 examines how far such benchmark scores carry over to real work.

**Debate: Is hand-built knowledge a dead end?**

The history above can be read as one long argument. The reinforcement-learning researcher Richard Sutton, in an influential 2019 essay on his personal website, holds that "general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin". In his account, researchers repeatedly built human knowledge into their systems and were overtaken by simpler methods that used more computing power [@sutton2019bitter]. Several of his examples appear earlier in this chapter: Deep Blue's massive search in chess, statistical hidden Markov models defeating knowledge-based speech systems, and then deep learning in speech and vision. The later steps traced here fit the same pattern. GPT-3, one general model given a few examples in its prompt, was at times competitive with systems fine-tuned separately for each task [@brown2020language].

The cognitive scientist Gary Marcus, in a preprint essay, sets out ten concerns about deep learning and argues that it "must be supplemented by other techniques if we are to reach artificial general intelligence" [@marcus2018deep]. His side of the record is also documented. AlphaGo won by combining learned networks with explicit search [@silver2016mastering], today's agents call search engines, code interpreters and databases (Chapter 5), and current systems remain uneven. The 2026 AI Index notes that a model achieved a gold-medal-level score at the 2025 International Mathematical Olympiad, yet in March 2026 the best model read analogue clocks correctly only about half the time [@sajadieh2026ai]. By October 2026 the leading model scored 83.3% on ClockBench, a clock-reading leaderboard run by an independent developer, against 90.7% for the average human [@clockbench2026clockbench]. The gap closed fast, which is Sutton's point; it has not closed, which is Marcus's.

Settling the question needs a capability that keeps resisting scale but yields to added structure, which would favour Marcus, or one long said to need built-in knowledge, such as reliable reasoning about the physical world, that emerges from scale alone, which would favour Sutton.

## 1.8 Why "AI" is a moving target

### The AI effect

The computer scientist Larry Tesler is credited with what others later called the "AI effect". As commonly quoted, his theorem says "Artificial Intelligence is whatever hasn't been done yet"; by his own account, what he actually said, around 1970, was "Intelligence is whatever machines haven't done yet" [@tesler2024adages]. The 2016 report of the One Hundred Year Study on AI describes the same "repeating pattern known as the 'AI effect' or the 'odd paradox'": AI "brings a new technology into the common fold, people become accustomed to this technology, it stops being considered AI, and newer technology emerges". The report concludes that AI is best seen as "a continual endeavor to push forward the frontier of machine intelligence" [@stone2016artificial].

Chess is the clearest case. IBM presented Deep Blue's 1997 victory as heralding a future in which "supercomputers and artificial intelligence could simulate human thinking" [@ibm2025deep]. A generation later, the Commission's guidelines suggest that such a program may not count as an AI system at all, because it applies predefined rules without learning from data [@europeancommission2025guidelines, para 48]. Elsewhere the guidelines treat long, "consolidated" use of a statistical method such as regression as a sign that it falls outside [@europeancommission2025guidelines, para 42]. The two criteria are separate, but together they come close to writing the AI effect into regulatory practice.

The effect also works from inside the field. Hendler describes researchers in the 1980s dismissing working expert systems as "not really AI" [@hendler2008avoiding], and Mitchell notes that machine-learning researchers of the 1990s and 2000s kept their distance from the label [@mitchell2021why]. This is one reason why spam filters, recommender systems and statistical translation were rarely called AI when they were introduced, although they meet the definitions in Section 1.2.

### AI as a brand and a marketing claim

The label has commercial value, and its scope has followed money. Haigh notes that in the 1980s "expert systems" was often promoted as "a less controversial alternative to artificial intelligence", and that later neural-network work was pursued under brands such as machine learning and pattern recognition. Only in the last few years, he writes, has the AI label been "flipped to refer primarily to deep learning and generative systems" [@haigh2024how].

When the label is fashionable it is also claimed falsely, and regulators have started to penalise such claims. In March 2024 the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) fined two investment advisers, Delphia and Global Predictions, a total of $400,000 for telling clients that they used AI in ways that they did not; its chair called the practice "AI washing" [@sec2024sec]. In September 2024 the Federal Trade Commission announced five enforcement actions against firms that, in its words, had "seized on the hype surrounding AI". One of them, DoNotPay, had advertised "the world's first robot lawyer"; the FTC's chair said that "there is no AI exemption from the laws on the books" [@ftc2024announces]. Enforcement has continued. In January 2025 the SEC settled charges against Presto Automation over claims about how many drive-thru orders its AI system handled without human help [@sec2025presto], and in April 2025 federal prosecutors charged the founder of the shopping app Nate with fraud, alleging, according to TechCrunch, that purchases attributed to AI were completed by human contractors [@rollet2025fintech].

Scholars of technology go further. The data-journalism professor and former software developer Meredith Broussard warns against "technochauvinism", the belief that technology is always the solution [@broussard2018artificial], and Kate Crawford describes AI as a material and political system, built from minerals, labour and personal data, that tends to concentrate power [@crawford2021atlas]. The anthropologist Lucy Suchman makes the argument most relevant to this chapter. Treating AI as a single "thing", she argues, helps to attract further investment, and she asks instead for attention to the specific techniques that "travel under the sign of AI", where they are used and with what effects [@suchman2023uncontroversial]. The legal definitions in Section 1.2 do the opposite of what she asks: they gather regression-like learning, expert systems and language models under one name, and then need case-by-case exclusions to keep the name usable. Her question, which systems a single name should cover and who gains from grouping them, is therefore also a question about those definitions.

### Springs, winters and the language of progress

Mitchell describes the history of the field as a cycle of AI "springs" and "winters": periods of "optimistic predictions and massive investment" followed by "disappointment, loss of confidence, and reduced funding". Simon's twenty-year prediction did not come true, and neither did the commercial hopes of the 1980s. She traces over-optimism partly to systematic errors in reasoning about intelligence, one of which she calls "easy things are easy and hard things are hard": what humans do without thought, such as making sense of a visual scene, has proved among the hardest things for machines [@mitchell2021why]. The documented causes of past winters were specific: combinatorial explosion, costly and brittle rule bases, a hardware market undercut by general-purpose machines, and funding promised against applications that did not arrive. Whether the present boom shares any of these weaknesses is a question for Chapter 14.

The official definitions were written with this moving target in mind. By defining AI through inference and outputs rather than any technique, the OECD and the EU tried to cover both the symbolic and the learning traditions and whatever comes next [@oecd2024explanatory; @europeanparliament2024regulation]. The OECD memorandum states that "additional techniques and applications will likely emerge in the future" and asks users of its definition to "exercise judgement on its relevant scope" [@oecd2024explanatory, p. 6].

Over seventy years the name has covered heuristic search, hand-written rules, statistical models estimated from data and deep networks trained on vast corpora, and the traditions have kept borrowing from one another, as AlphaGo's tree search and today's tool-using agents show. A recurring gap separates results on bounded tasks from dependable use. MYCIN matched specialists and never reached a ward, and benchmark records have often measured less than the headlines they drew. The legal definitions now in force draw their boundary by function, which brings 1970s expert systems inside and may leave a classical chess program out. Figures on AI adoption, investment or risk therefore depend on which systems are counted, under which definition and at what date. Chapter 2 turns from names to mechanism, and explains how the neural networks behind today's systems learn.

## Further reading

- Turing, A. M. (1950). *Computing machinery and intelligence*. Short, readable and still the starting point for debates about machine intelligence and how to test it. [@turing1950computing]
- McCarthy, J., Minsky, M., Rochester, N., & Shannon, C. (1955/2006). *A proposal for the Dartmouth summer research project on artificial intelligence*. Twelve pages that show what the founders thought the problems were, and how small they expected the effort to be. [@mccarthy2006proposal]
- Buchanan, B. G., & Shortliffe, E. H. (Eds.) (1984). *Rule-based expert systems: The MYCIN experiments*. The fullest first-hand account of how an expert system was built, tested and abandoned, freely available online. [@buchanan1984rule]
- Halevy, A., Norvig, P., & Pereira, F. (2009). *The unreasonable effectiveness of data*. A five-page statement of why statistical methods trained on large corpora overtook hand-built models. [@halevy2009unreasonable]
- European Commission (2025). *Guidelines on the definition of an artificial intelligence system*. Shows how a regulator turns an abstract definition into concrete inclusions and exclusions. [@europeancommission2025guidelines]
- Mitchell, M. (2021). *Why AI is harder than we think*. An accessible account of AI's springs and winters and of the reasoning errors that drive over-optimism. [@mitchell2021why]
