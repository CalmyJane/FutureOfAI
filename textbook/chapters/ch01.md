# Chapter 1: What We Mean by "AI": Definitions and a Short History

::: {.objectives}
**After working through this chapter you should be able to:**

- distinguish machine learning, deep learning, generative AI, foundation models, large language models and AI agents, and describe how these terms relate to one another;
- describe the main phases in the history of AI, from symbolic AI and expert systems through the AI winters to deep learning and large language models;
- evaluate claims about rapid progress and adoption by asking what was measured, on which sample and when;

On 30 November 2022, the company OpenAI released ChatGPT to the public as a "research preview". By 5 December it had more than one million registered users, and by July 2025 about 700 million people were using it each week, around 10% of the world's adult population [@chatterji2025how]. That study is an NBER working paper, not yet peer-reviewed; its authors include economists at Duke and Harvard universities as well as OpenAI staff, and its figures rest on OpenAI's own data. In February 2026, according to press reports of a company blog post, OpenAI announced 900 million weekly active users, a figure that has not been independently verified [@malik2026chatgpt].

For many people, ChatGPT was their first conscious encounter with "artificial intelligence". The term itself, however, is about seventy years old. It appears in a proposal dated 31 August 1955 for a summer workshop at Dartmouth College, which conjectured that "every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it" [@mccarthy2006proposal]. Between that proposal and ChatGPT lie several waves of optimism, two periods of sharply reduced funding, and at least three different ideas about how intelligent machines should be built.

This chapter provides two things that the rest of the book relies on. The first is a shared vocabulary: what the official definitions say, and how terms such as machine learning, deep learning, generative AI, foundation model and agent relate to one another. The second is a short history, told through documented milestones, so that the trends discussed in later chapters can be seen against a longer arc. The chapter ends by asking why the boundary of what counts as "AI" keeps moving.

## 1.1 Defining artificial intelligence

### Turing's question

Alan Turing's paper "Computing machinery and intelligence" opens with the sentence "I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'" [@turing1950computing]. Turing judged the words "machine" and "think" too vague to define usefully, so he replaced the question with a test he called the imitation game. In this game a human interrogator exchanges typed messages with a hidden person and a hidden machine, and tries to tell which is which. Turing predicted that in about fifty years computers with a storage capacity of about 10^9^ would play the game well enough that an average interrogator would have no more than a 70 per cent chance of making the right identification after five minutes of questioning [@turing1950computing].

Turing's move matters for this book because it set a pattern that has lasted. He defined intelligence by what a system does, judged from the outside, instead of by what goes on inside it. Most modern definitions of AI, including the legal ones discussed below, follow the same behavioural approach. The weakness of the approach is also visible from the start: a behavioural test says nothing about whether the machine reaches its answers in a way that resembles human thought. Section 1.6 returns to this question in the debate about whether large language models "understand" language.

The Dartmouth proposal of 1955, written by John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon, gave the field its name. It proposed "a 2 month, 10 man study of artificial intelligence" in the summer of 1956 at Dartmouth College in Hanover, New Hampshire [@mccarthy2006proposal]. The workshop is usually treated as the founding event of AI as a research field. One widely quoted later definition, by the AI pioneer Nils Nilsson, describes AI as "that activity devoted to making machines intelligent", and intelligence as the quality that enables an entity "to function appropriately and with foresight in its environment" [@stone2016artificial].

### The OECD definition

Definitions written by researchers can afford to be loose. Definitions written for governments cannot, because they decide which systems a law or policy covers. The most influential international definition comes from the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), an intergovernmental organisation of mostly high-income countries. Its member countries approved a revised version in November 2023, and an explanatory memorandum published in March 2024 sets out the final text [@oecd2024explanatory]:

> An AI system is a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments. Different AI systems vary in their levels of autonomy and adaptiveness after deployment. [@oecd2024explanatory, p. 4]

Each element of this sentence does work. "Objectives" can be explicit, such as a target set by a programmer, or implicit, such as a goal that emerges from training. "Infers" is the central idea: the system works out how to produce its output from the input, instead of following a fixed list of instructions. The memorandum explains that inference here covers both the moment a system produces an answer and the earlier "build phase", in which a model is derived from data [@oecd2024explanatory, p. 9]. "Autonomy" refers to how far the system acts without human involvement, and "adaptiveness" to whether it keeps changing its behaviour after it has been deployed, as a speech recogniser that adapts to one person's voice does [@oecd2024explanatory].

The 2023 revision added "content" to the list of outputs. The memorandum says this was done because generative AI systems that produce text, images, audio and video "have gained significant momentum", and had become important enough to deserve their own category [@oecd2024explanatory, p. 9]. The OECD's earlier 2019 definition had spoken only of predictions, recommendations and decisions for "human-defined objectives" [@oecd2024explanatory]. The change is a small, documented example of a definition being rewritten to keep up with the technology.

### The EU AI Act definition

The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, is a comprehensive EU-wide law on AI (Chapter 13 discusses it in detail). Its Article 3(1) defines an AI system as "a machine-based system that is designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy and that may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment, and that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments" [@european2024regulation, art. 3(1)]. The wording deliberately mirrors the OECD text. Recital 12 of the Act states that the definition should be "closely aligned with the work of international organisations working on AI" [@european2024regulation, recital 12].

Recital 12 also clarifies what the definition leaves out. It should not cover "simpler traditional software systems" or systems "based on the rules defined solely by natural persons to automatically execute operations" [@european2024regulation, recital 12]. A spreadsheet formula that calculates tax from fixed rates is therefore not an AI system under the Act. The same recital, however, includes "logic- and knowledge-based approaches that infer from encoded knowledge or symbolic representation of the task to be solved" alongside machine learning [@european2024regulation, recital 12]. So the legal definition covers older rule-based AI of the kind described in Section 1.3, provided the system draws inferences instead of merely executing a programmer's instructions. Deciding on which side of that line a particular product falls requires legal judgement.

::: {.box .concept}
**Key concept: AI system (OECD and EU definition)**

In both the OECD definition and the EU AI Act, an AI system is a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers from its input how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions that can affect physical or virtual environments. The key word is "infers": the system derives how to respond, from data or from encoded knowledge, instead of only executing rules a person wrote down. A spam filter trained on examples of junk mail fits the definition. A fixed formula in a spreadsheet does not.
:::

## 1.2 A family of terms

Public discussion often uses "AI" as if it named a single technology. In practice it is an umbrella term for a family of methods, and the terms in that family overlap. Figure 1.1 shows the most common way of arranging them as nested circles, which this section explains term by term.

![Figure 1.1: The usual nesting of AI terms. Machine learning is one approach within AI; deep learning is one kind of machine learning; large language models and other generative foundation models are, today, mostly built with deep learning. Agents are drawn across the boundaries because the term describes how a model is used. Schematic, not to scale. Diagram by the author.](figures/fig-01-01.svg){#fig:01-01}

**Machine learning** is the approach in which a system improves at a task by learning from data or experience, instead of being given explicit rules. Arthur Samuel's 1959 study of the game of checkers (draughts) is a classic early example. Samuel showed that "a computer can be programmed so that it will learn to play a better game of checkers than can be played by the person who wrote the program", and that it could do so in 8 to 10 hours of playing time [@samuel1959studies]. An everyday analogy is learning to recognise ripe fruit by handling many examples, instead of memorising a rulebook. The analogy breaks down because a machine-learning system needs far more examples than a person does and has no common-sense knowledge to fall back on when an example is unusual.

**Deep learning** is machine learning with artificial neural networks that have many layers. A neural network is a computing structure loosely inspired by the brain, made of simple connected units whose connection strengths are adjusted during training (Chapter 2 explains how). In the words of a widely cited review, deep learning "allows computational models that are composed of multiple processing layers to learn representations of data with multiple levels of abstraction" [@lecun2015deep]. In an image-recognition network, for example, earlier layers pick out simple visual features and later layers combine them into more abstract ones, which is what "levels of abstraction" means here (Chapter 2 gives examples). Writing in 2021, the computer scientist Melanie Mitchell observed that deep neural networks powered all the major AI advances of the previous decade [@mitchell2021why].

**Generative AI** refers to systems whose main output is new content: text, images, audio, video or computer code. As noted above, the OECD added "content" to its definition precisely because such systems had become so prominent [@oecd2024explanatory]. ChatGPT, image generators and music generators are generative AI. A system that only sorts emails into "spam" and "not spam" is not, because it produces a label instead of new content.

A **foundation model** is a large model trained on broad data, usually with self-supervision at scale, that can be adapted to a wide range of downstream tasks. The term was introduced in 2021 in a report by more than 100 researchers, who chose the word to stress the models' "critically central yet incomplete character" [@bommasani2021opportunities]. Their examples included BERT, DALL-E and GPT-3. Self-supervision means that the training signal comes from the data itself, for instance by hiding a word in a sentence and asking the model to predict it, so no human has to label each example. The EU AI Act uses a closely related legal term, the **general-purpose AI model**: "an AI model, including where such an AI model is trained with a large amount of data using self-supervision at scale, that displays significant generality and is capable of competently performing a wide range of distinct tasks" [@european2024regulation, art. 3(63)].

A **large language model (LLM)** is a foundation model trained mainly on text to predict the next word, or more precisely the next "token", in a sequence. GPT-3, described in Section 1.6, is a well-documented example [@brown2020language]. Chapter 3 explains how LLMs work.

An **AI agent** is a system that pursues a goal over several steps by taking actions in an environment, such as searching the web, running code or filling in forms, and observing the results. The idea of an agent is old in AI research: a 1995 survey already described it as having "become important in both artificial intelligence and mainstream computer science" [@wooldridge1995intelligent]. What is new since 2024 is that agents are commonly built by placing an LLM inside a loop with tools and memory (Chapter 5 covers this in depth).

Figure 1.1 is useful but imperfect, and its limits are instructive. First, not every generative model is a deep neural network, and not every foundation model is generative: BERT, one of the original examples, was mainly used for understanding and classifying text [@bommasani2021opportunities]. Second, "agent" describes how a model is used, not a technique, so it cuts across the circles. Third, the outer circle contains far more than machine learning, including the symbolic methods described next.

## 1.3 Symbolic AI, expert systems and the AI winters (1956 to the early 1990s)

### Early optimism

For its first three decades, most AI research followed what is now called **symbolic AI**. In this approach, knowledge is written down explicitly as symbols and rules (for example, "if the patient has a fever and a stiff neck, consider meningitis"), and the computer reasons by manipulating them. The approach suited the logic-based programming of the time and matched a view of thinking as rule-following. An analogy is a very large and carefully cross-referenced instruction manual. The analogy breaks down because human experts often act on knowledge they cannot put into words, a problem that proved central to symbolic AI's difficulties.

Predictions in this period were bold. In 1960, Herbert Simon declared that "Machines will be capable, within twenty years, of doing any work that a man can do", and in 1967 Marvin Minsky forecast that "Within a generation...the problems of creating 'artificial intelligence' will be substantially solved" [@mitchell2021why]. Enthusiasm also surrounded early neural networks. In 1958 the New York Times reported that the US Navy expected Frank Rosenblatt's "perceptron" to be able to "walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself, and be conscious of its existence" [@mitchell2021why]. In 1969, Minsky and Seymour Papert's book *Perceptrons* showed that the simple, single-layer networks of the time could solve only a limited class of problems [@mitchell2021why].

### The first AI winter

The term **AI winter** describes a period of reduced funding, falling confidence and declining interest in AI research. The first such period followed critical government reviews in the early 1970s. In the United Kingdom the Science Research Council asked the mathematician Sir James Lighthill to assess the field. His report, dated July 1972 and published in 1973, concluded that "in no part of the field have the discoveries made so far produced the major impact that was then promised" [@lighthill1973artificial]. Among the causes he named was a "failure to recognise the implications of the combinatorial explosion" [@lighthill1973artificial]. The combinatorial explosion is the way the number of possibilities to check grows extremely fast as a problem gets bigger, as when each extra move in a board game multiplies the number of possible continuations.

According to Mitchell's account (an essay circulated as an arXiv preprint), the Lighthill report and a similarly negative review for the US Department of Defense "led to sharp funding decreases and a downturn in enthusiasm for AI in both countries" [@mitchell2021why]. The research did not stop, but it continued at a smaller scale and with more modest claims.

### Expert systems and the second winter

Interest revived in the early 1980s, driven largely by **expert systems**. An expert system is a program that encodes the knowledge of human specialists in a narrow field as hundreds or thousands of if-then rules, and uses them to give advice or make decisions. Such systems fit the symbolic approach well: specialists were interviewed, their knowledge was written down as rules, and an inference engine chained the rules together. A medical and an industrial example show what they could do.

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: MYCIN's blinded evaluation (1979)**

MYCIN was an expert system developed at Stanford University in the 1970s to recommend antibiotic treatment for serious infections. In a study published in 1979, its recommendations for ten real cases of meningitis were compared with those of nine human prescribers, including five infectious-disease faculty specialists [@yu1979antimicrobial]. Eight outside experts rated each prescription without knowing which came from the computer. MYCIN's prescriptions were rated acceptable 65% of the time; the five faculty specialists scored between 42.5% and 62.5% [@yu1979antimicrobial]. The study shows that a carefully built rule-based system could match specialists on a narrow, well-defined task. It was also small (ten cases), confined to a single type of infection, and judged by agreement among experts instead of by patient outcomes, which are common limits of early AI evaluations.
:::

Mitchell describes "the rise of 'expert systems' in industry" in the early 1980s [@mitchell2021why]. From 1980, the computer manufacturer Digital Equipment Corporation used a rule-based system called R1, later XCON, to configure its VAX-11 computer systems for customers, and over the following years its knowledge base grew substantially [@bachant1984r1]. Japan launched its "Fifth Generation" computing project, and the United States responded with its Strategic Computing Initiative, both of which placed large bets on AI [@mitchell2021why].

Some researchers were already worried. At the 1984 conference of the American Association for Artificial Intelligence, a panel discussed "deep unease among AI researchers" that expectations were too high and that this "will eventually result in disaster" [@mcdermott1985dark]. One panellist, Drew McDermott, described the danger of a funding collapse in which "everybody hurriedly changes the names of their research projects to something else", a condition he called the "AI Winter" [@mitchell2021why]. About five years later, in the late 1980s, a second winter set in [@mitchell2021why].

Mitchell attributes the downturn largely to **brittleness**. Expert systems were "often unable to generalize or adapt when faced with new situations", because the experts writing the rules relied on unspoken common-sense knowledge that never made it into the program [@mitchell2021why]. The XCON team's own account, published after four years of use, describes the continuing work needed to extend the system and keep improving its performance [@bachant1984r1]. The lesson many researchers drew was that knowledge written down by hand does not scale well. Whether that lesson was fully correct is still debated, as Section 1.7 shows.

## 1.4 The statistical turn: learning from data (1990s to 2000s)

From the 1990s, much of the field moved from writing knowledge down to learning it from data. Mitchell describes "the meteoric rise of machine learning" in the 1990s and 2000s, with methods "typically inspired by statistics rather than by neuroscience or psychology" and aimed at specific tasks instead of general intelligence [@mitchell2021why]. She also notes that machine-learning researchers often kept their distance from the label "AI", which had become discredited [@mitchell2021why]. This is one reason why everyday technologies of the period, such as spam filters and recommendation systems, were rarely described as AI at the time.

Several techniques from this period remain in use. Support vector machines, introduced in their modern form by Corinna Cortes and Vladimir Vapnik in 1995, find the boundary that best separates two categories of examples [@cortes1995support]. Neural networks had not disappeared either. A 1986 paper in *Nature* by David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams popularised **backpropagation**, the method still used to train neural networks today [@rumelhart1986learning]. Chapter 2 explains it in plain language. For two decades, however, neural networks were one method among many and often not the best performing.

The most widely publicised AI event of the 1990s involved a specialised chess machine. In May 1997, IBM's chess computer Deep Blue beat the world champion Garry Kasparov 3.5 to 2.5 in a six-game match in New York, which IBM describes as the first time a computer defeated a reigning world champion in a match under standard tournament time controls [@ibm2025deep]. The match was played under intense media attention [@ibm2025deep]. Deep Blue had been built with a single goal, to beat the world's best human chess player [@ibm2025deep], and it could do nothing else.

A quieter development turned out to matter more. In 2009, Jia Deng, Fei-Fei Li and colleagues introduced ImageNet, a large database of labelled photographs organised by object categories [@deng2009imagenet]. From 2010, an annual competition, the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge (ILSVRC), asked teams to classify images into 1,000 categories, using roughly 1.2 million training images [@russakovsky2015imagenet]. Large, shared datasets with agreed scoring rules gave researchers a common yardstick, and they gave data-hungry methods something to learn from.

## 1.5 The deep learning breakthrough (2012 to 2017)

### ImageNet 2012

In the 2012 ImageNet challenge, a team from the University of Toronto, Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton, entered a deep convolutional neural network now usually called AlexNet. It achieved a top-5 error rate of 15.3%, compared with 26.2% for the second-best entry [@krizhevsky2012imagenet]. The 15.3% entry used additional pre-training data; using only the official training data, the team's error was 16.4%, the value shown in Figure 1.2 [@russakovsky2015imagenet]. The network had 60 million adjustable parameters and was trained for five to six days on two NVIDIA GTX 580 graphics cards [@krizhevsky2012imagenet]. Graphics processing units (GPUs) were developed for computer graphics, but they can carry out many simple calculations at the same time, which is what training a neural network requires.

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: What "top-5 error" measures**

In the ImageNet classification task, each test image has one correct label from 1,000 categories, such as a particular breed of dog. A system may submit five guesses per image. The top-5 error is the percentage of test images for which none of the five guesses matches the correct label [@russakovsky2015imagenet]. Five guesses are allowed because many photographs contain several objects and only one is labelled. The figure 15.3% therefore means that AlexNet's five guesses all missed for about 15 of every 100 test images. The organisers note that top-5 error, top-1 error and a hierarchical measure ranked the entries in the same order, which is why they adopted the simplest one [@russakovsky2015imagenet].
:::

Figure 1.2 places the 2012 result in context. The winning error rate (counting only entries that used the official training data) fell from 28.2% in 2010 to 6.7% in 2014, and the drop after 2012 coincided with the switch to deep convolutional networks [@russakovsky2015imagenet]. In 2015 a "residual network" with up to 152 layers reached 3.57% [@he2016deep].

![Figure 1.2: Winning top-5 classification error in the ImageNet challenge, 2010–2015 (lower is better). The largest single drop came in 2012, when a deep neural network won for the first time. The dashed line marks one trained human annotator's estimated error of 5.1% on a sample of 1,500 test images; it is a single-person estimate, not a general human benchmark. The human comparison in Figure 1.2 deserves care. The organisers estimated that one expert annotator, who had trained on 500 images, made top-5 errors on 5.1% of 1,500 test images [@russakovsky2015imagenet]. A second annotator labelled only 258 images [@russakovsky2015imagenet]. In 2015, a team led by Kaiming He reported a 4.94% error and described its result as "the first to surpass human-level performance (5.1%...)" on the challenge [@he2015delving]. That claim, and the headlines it inspired, rested on this narrow comparison: one benchmark and one main annotator. It is an early example of a recurring pattern, discussed in Chapter 4: benchmark results are real, but they measure less than the headlines suggest.

### AlphaGo

The board game Go had long been regarded as the hardest of the classic games for computers, because the number of possible positions is enormous and it is hard to judge who is winning in a given position. In January 2016, David Silver and colleagues at Google DeepMind described AlphaGo, which combined deep neural networks with a search method [@silver2016mastering]. One network suggested promising moves and another estimated the chance of winning from a position. They were trained first on games played by human experts and then by "reinforcement learning from games of self-play", in which the program improved by playing against versions of itself [@silver2016mastering].

AlphaGo won 99.8% of its games against other Go programs and beat the European champion, Fan Hui, by five games to nothing [@silver2016mastering]. The authors described this as the first time a computer program had defeated a human professional player on a full-sized board, "a feat previously thought to be at least a decade away" [@silver2016mastering]. In March 2016 AlphaGo then beat Lee Sedol, a world champion player from South Korea, by four games to one in Seoul [@sumner2016alphago]. Because AlphaGo learned much of its skill from expert games and self-play, its victory was widely read as a demonstration of machine learning.

### The transformer

In June 2017, eight researchers, most of them at Google, published "Attention is all you need" [@vaswani2017attention]. They proposed the **transformer**, a neural-network design for processing sequences such as sentences, "based solely on attention mechanisms, dispensing with recurrence and convolutions entirely" [@vaswani2017attention]. Attention, roughly speaking, lets the model weigh how relevant every other word in a passage is when interpreting each word. On a standard English-to-German translation test, the transformer scored 28.4 on the BLEU measure, more than two points above the best previous results, and it trained in a fraction of the time [@vaswani2017attention].

The paper's practical importance came from that last point. Because a transformer processes all the words in a passage at once, instead of one after another, it makes good use of parallel hardware such as GPUs. That made it practical to train much larger models on much more text. Almost every large language model discussed in this book is built on the transformer. Chapter 3 explains attention in more detail.

## 1.6 From language models to agents (2018 to 2026)

### GPT-3 and foundation models

In May 2020, a team at OpenAI, with Tom Brown as first author, described GPT-3, a transformer language model with 175 billion parameters, "10x more than any previous non-sparse language model" [@brown2020language]. A parameter is one of the adjustable numbers inside a network that training sets. The paper's main finding was that a model this large could carry out many tasks it had not been specifically trained for, such as translation, answering questions or simple arithmetic, when given a few examples in the prompt [@brown2020language]. The authors called this few-shot learning. They also reported tasks on which GPT-3 still struggled [@brown2020language].

GPT-3 and similar models prompted the research group mentioned in Section 1.2 to coin the term "foundation model" in 2021 [@bommasani2021opportunities]. Their report argued that the defects of a foundation model are "inherited by all the adapted models downstream", because many applications are built on the same few base models [@bommasani2021opportunities]. This concern about concentration reappears in Chapter 7.

### ChatGPT and the speed of adoption

ChatGPT put a large language model behind a simple chat window and made it free to use. Its user numbers, given at the start of this chapter, come from the company. Independent surveys point the same way, although they measure something different. A nationally representative series of US surveys by the economists Alexander Bick, Adam Blandin and David Deming found that, as of late 2024, "nearly 40 percent of the U.S. population age 18-64 uses generative AI" [@bick2025rapid]. Measured from each technology's first mass-market product, they found that overall adoption "has been faster than either PCs or the internet" [@bick2025rapid]. The paper is an NBER working paper, which means it has not been peer-reviewed.

The 2026 edition of the Stanford AI Index, an annual report that compiles data on AI, builds on the same line of research. Drawing on the same US survey series of adults aged 18 to 64, it reports that generative AI reached about 53% adoption within three years of its first mass-market product, well above the early paths of the personal computer and the internet [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 4]. The same report also uses a quite different measure, Microsoft's data on the share of each country's population using AI tools. On that measure adoption varies widely between countries and "correlates strongly with GDP per capita", and the United States ranks only 24th, at 28.3%, in the second half of 2025 [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 4]. The two US figures differ because they count different things in different ways, not because one of them is wrong. Adoption figures depend heavily on what counts as "use": a single trial in the past month and daily use at work are very different things. Chapter 8 examines adoption and its economic effects.

### Reasoning models

A second shift came in 2024 and 2025 with so-called **reasoning models**. These are language models trained to produce a long internal chain of intermediate steps before giving a final answer, which has improved their performance on verifiable tasks such as mathematics, coding competitions and science questions [@guo2025deepseek]. OpenAI's o1 models, according to the company, are "trained with large-scale reinforcement learning to reason using chain of thought", and "o1 thinks before it answers" [@openai2024o1]. In January 2025 the Chinese company DeepSeek published DeepSeek-R1. A peer-reviewed version in *Nature* reports that reasoning abilities "can be incentivized through pure reinforcement learning", without human-written examples of step-by-step reasoning [@guo2025deepseek].

Reasoning models change where computing effort is spent. Earlier gains came mainly from training ever-larger models. Reasoning models also spend more computation each time they answer a question, which Chapter 3 and Chapter 4 discuss as "test-time compute". The word "reasoning" is the developers' term. Whether these step-by-step outputs reflect reasoning in the human sense is an open question, closely related to the debate below.

### Agents

The most recent shift, during 2025 and 2026, is towards agents built around language models. In November 2024 the company Anthropic released the Model Context Protocol, which it describes as "an open standard for connecting AI assistants to the systems where data lives, from content repositories to business tools" [@anthropic2024introducing]. Standards like this make it easier to give a model access to tools, files and other software. Chapter 5 covers the technical details.

Measurements suggest rapid, but uneven, progress. Researchers at METR, an organisation that evaluates AI systems, proposed measuring an AI system by the length of tasks it can complete. Their measure is the time a skilled human typically needs for tasks that the AI completes with 50% success [@kwa2025measuring]. In a study first posted as a preprint in March 2025 and later presented at the NeurIPS 2025 conference, they reported that this "time horizon" had been "doubling approximately every seven months since 2019" on their set of mainly software tasks [@kwa2025measuring]. The 2026 AI Index reports that on OSWorld, a benchmark of real computer tasks across operating systems, the best agent's success rate rose from roughly 12% to 66.3%, within about 6 percentage points of human performance. It adds that agents "still fail roughly one in three attempts on structured benchmarks" [@sajadieh2026ai]. Benchmarks are controlled settings, so they may overstate how well agents perform in everyday use, a problem Chapter 5 examines.

**Debate: Do large language models understand language?**

A review by Melanie Mitchell and David Krakauer describes a "heated debate" in the research community on whether large language models understand language "in any humanlike sense" [@mitchell2023debate]. On one side, Emily Bender and Alexander Koller argue that "a system trained only on form has a priori no way to learn meaning" [@bender2020climbing]. Form here means the sequence of words; meaning means what the words refer to in the world. On their view, fluent output shows skill with patterns in text, not understanding.

On the other side, some researchers point to evidence that language-model-style networks build internal models of what they process. In one experiment, a GPT-style network trained only to predict legal moves in the board game Othello developed an internal representation of the board state, which the researchers could edit to change the network's predictions [@li2023emergent]. Supporters of this view argue that such internal models are at least a partial form of understanding.

The research community is split. In a 2022 survey of 480 natural-language-processing researchers, reported by Mitchell and Krakauer, 51% agreed and 49% disagreed that a model trained only on text could, in principle, understand language "in some nontrivial sense" [@mitchell2023debate]. Settling the question would require an agreed, testable definition of understanding, together with tests that a system cannot pass by surface pattern-matching alone. Mitchell and Krakauer suggest that there may be several distinct modes of understanding instead of one [@mitchell2023debate].

## 1.7 Why "AI" is a moving target

### The AI effect

Chess offers an illustration. IBM presented Deep Blue's 1997 victory as heralding a future in which "supercomputers and artificial intelligence could simulate human thinking" [@ibm2025deep]. Chess software is now an ordinary consumer product. The 2016 report of the One Hundred Year Study on Artificial Intelligence, a long-term project based at Stanford University, describes a "repeating pattern known as the 'AI effect' or the 'odd paradox'": AI "brings a new technology into the common fold, people become accustomed to this technology, it stops being considered AI, and newer technology emerges" [@stone2016artificial]. The report concludes that AI is best seen as "a continual endeavor to push forward the frontier of machine intelligence" [@stone2016artificial].

The AI effect has practical consequences. Statistics on "AI investment" or "AI adoption" depend on what is counted, and the boundary shifts over time. It also affects how history is told. As Section 1.4 showed, many machine-learning applications of the 1990s and 2000s were built by researchers who avoided the label "AI" [@mitchell2021why], so they are often missing from popular histories of the field. The reverse also happens: when the label is fashionable, products that use only simple statistics may be marketed as AI.

### Springs, winters and the language of progress

The history in this chapter shows a cycle that Mitchell calls AI "springs" and "winters": periods of "optimistic predictions and massive investment" followed by "disappointment, loss of confidence, and reduced funding" [@mitchell2021why]. Simon's twenty-year prediction of 1960 did not come true, and neither did the commercial hopes of the 1980s. Mitchell argues that over-optimism stems partly from systematic errors in how people reason about intelligence. One of these she calls "easy things are easy and hard things are hard": things humans do without much thought, such as looking around and making sense of a scene, have proved among the hardest for machines [@mitchell2021why]. Whether the current period will end in another winter is a question for Chapter 14. The record of past predictions is a reason to treat confident forecasts in either direction with care.

### Two traditions, one argument

A deeper reason why the meaning of AI keeps shifting is that the field has never agreed on how intelligence should be built. The symbolic tradition writes knowledge down; the learning tradition extracts it from data. The reinforcement-learning researcher Richard Sutton argues in an influential essay that "general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin" [@sutton2019bitter]. In his account, researchers have repeatedly built human knowledge into their systems, only to be overtaken by simpler methods that used more computing power [@sutton2019bitter]. The cognitive scientist Gary Marcus takes the opposing view. He sets out ten concerns about deep learning and argues that it "must be supplemented by other techniques if we are to reach artificial general intelligence" [@marcus2018deep].

The evidence in this chapter can be read in favour of both positions. The rise of deep learning after 2012 and the success of scaled-up language models fit Sutton's argument. The uneven performance of current systems fits parts of Marcus's argument: the 2026 AI Index notes that models can win a gold medal at the International Mathematical Olympiad yet the best model reads analogue clocks correctly only about half the time [@sajadieh2026ai]. Agents that combine a learned model with search engines, code interpreters and other conventional software (Chapter 5) are one practical way of supplementing deep learning with other techniques. Chapter 4 examines how far scaling can go, and Chapter 12 looks at what researchers can and cannot yet see inside these models.

The legal definitions in Section 1.1 were written with this moving target in mind. By defining AI through inference and outputs, instead of through any particular technique, the OECD and the EU tried to cover both traditions and whatever comes next [@oecd2024explanatory; @european2024regulation]. The OECD memorandum states that "additional techniques and applications will likely emerge in the future" and asks users of its definition to "exercise judgement on its relevant scope" [@oecd2024explanatory, p. 6]. Chapter 13 examines how the EU AI Act's version of the definition is being applied in practice.

## Summary

- "Artificial intelligence" was named in a 1955 proposal for a 1956 Dartmouth workshop; Turing's 1950 paper had already framed machine intelligence in terms of observable behaviour.
- The OECD (revised 2023, memorandum 2024) and the EU AI Act (2024) define an AI system as a machine-based system that infers from its input how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions; "content" was added to cover generative AI.
- Machine learning, deep learning, generative AI, foundation models, large language models and agents form an overlapping family of terms; the usual nested diagram is helpful but imperfect.
- Symbolic AI and expert systems achieved real successes in narrow domains, such as MYCIN's 1979 evaluation, but proved brittle; two AI winters followed periods of over-optimism, in the 1970s and from the late 1980s.
- From the 1990s, statistical machine learning became dominant; large shared datasets such as ImageNet then set the stage for deep learning.
- AlexNet (2012), AlphaGo (2016) and the transformer (2017) mark the deep-learning breakthrough; GPT-3 (2020) and ChatGPT (2022) brought large language models to a mass audience, with adoption faster than the PC or the internet in US survey data.
- Reasoning models (2024–25) and LLM-based agents (2025–26) are the most recent shifts; benchmark progress is fast but agents still fail often.
- The "AI effect" means that the label moves as technologies become familiar, so claims about "AI" always need to specify which systems they include.

## Discussion questions

1. Apply the OECD/EU definition of an AI system to three examples: a thermostat that switches the heating on below 19 °C, a bank's credit-scoring model trained on past loans, and a chatbot. Which of them are AI systems under the definition, and which words in the definition decide each case?
2. The ImageNet "human error" of 5.1% was estimated from one expert annotator labelling 1,500 images. Evaluate how strongly this evidence supports the claim that machines "surpassed humans at image recognition" in 2015. What additional evidence would you want?
3. MYCIN matched or exceeded specialists in a blinded evaluation in 1979. Using the idea of brittleness, explain why a strong evaluation result might still not lead to widespread use. Can you find a similar pattern in a current AI product?
4. Bick, Blandin and Deming find that generative AI was adopted faster than PCs or the internet. List at least two ways in which "adoption" could be defined, and explain how the choice could change the conclusion.

6. Sutton and Marcus disagree about whether general learning methods will be enough. Which developments described in Sections 1.5 and 1.6 support each position, and what kind of future evidence would change your mind?
