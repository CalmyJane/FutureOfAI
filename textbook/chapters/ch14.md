# Chapter 14: Forecasting the Next Ten Years: Methods, Scenarios and Open Problems

Between June and October 2022, shortly before ChatGPT was released, 89 superforecasters (people selected for a proven record of accurate predictions) and 80 domain experts took part in a forecasting tournament on global risks. One question asked when an AI system would win a gold medal at the International Mathematical Olympiad. The median superforecaster said 2035 and the median expert 2030. In July 2025 Google DeepMind and OpenAI both reported gold-medal-level performance at that year's Olympiad. Scored afterwards, the superforecasters' forecasts implied only a 2.3% chance of a gold medal by 2025, and the experts' an 8.6% chance [@kucinskas2025assessing].

A second record points the other way. In October 2023, 2,778 researchers who had published at leading AI venues were asked when unaided machines would be able to do every task better and more cheaply than human workers. Their aggregate answer gave a 50% chance by 2047. Asked instead when every occupation would be fully automatable, the same population gave 2116 [@grace2025thousands]. The two questions describe almost the same world, yet the answers lie about seventy years apart. One body of evidence shows forecasters too slow on concrete benchmarks; the other shows that long-range answers depend heavily on how the question is worded. Both are needed to judge what any forecast of AI's next ten years is worth.

## 14.1 The track record of AI forecasting

A **forecast** is a statement about the future that can later be checked: a date, a number or a probability. Forecasting AI is hard for structural reasons. Progress depends on several inputs at once (computing power, data, algorithms and money), each of which can change course. Capability is measured with benchmarks that saturate or leak into training data, so even the present is blurred (Chapter 4). Adoption by firms and governments moves at a different speed from invention. And forecasts can alter what they predict, because investors and regulators act on them.

### Early predictions

Chapter 1 traces how the field's first timetables, from the Dartmouth proposal of 1955 to Herbert Simon's twenty-year forecast of 1960, were missed, and how the misses fed a cycle of AI "springs" and "winters" [@mccarthy2006proposal; @simon1960new; @mitchell2021why]. In Britain the 1973 Lighthill report, commissioned by the Science Research Council, concluded that "in no part of the field have the discoveries made so far produced the major impact that was then promised" [@lighthill1973artificial]. The first systematic study of such predictions came from Stuart Armstrong and Kaj Sotala, who assembled a database of 257 AI predictions made since the 1950s [@armstrong2015how]. The dates for human-level AI were scattered so widely that fifty-year gaps between them were common, yet many fell 15 to 25 years after the date on which they were made. The authors also claimed that experts and non-experts were indistinguishable. A 2016 blog post by Katja Grace on the AI Impacts website found that the column used to identify experts in fact recorded how each prediction was reached, so that claim does not stand. Armstrong replied that the habit of predicting a fixed distance ahead survived the correction [@grace2016error]. A horizon that stays constant while the calendar moves suggests a habit of mind as much as evidence.

### Recent forecasts of concrete milestones

Since about 2020, forecasting has shifted towards concrete, short-range questions, and here the dominant error has been the opposite. Forecasts were too slow. In August 2021 Jacob Steinhardt's research group ran a contest on the Hypermind forecasting platform, whose forecasters are typically paid according to a scoring rule that rewards accurate probabilities, to predict benchmark results for June 2022. Steinhardt reported the outcome on his blog [@steinhardt2022ai]. For the MATH benchmark of competition mathematics problems, the aggregate forecast was about a quarter of the best result actually reached by the deadline. For MMLU, a multiple-choice test of knowledge across many academic subjects, it was also too low (Table 14.1). Both outcomes lay far in the tails of the forecast distributions. Progress on a robustness benchmark, by contrast, was slower than forecast.

The 2022 tournament described above gave the same picture at larger scale. A 2025 working paper by its organisers scored the 38 of its 172 sub-questions (from 32 questions) that had resolved by mid-2025 [@kucinskas2025assessing]. Superforecasters and domain experts were about equally accurate overall, with no statistically significant difference between groups. Both underestimated AI progress, and superforecasters did so more. Across four AI benchmark questions, superforecasters had on average given a probability of 9.7% to the outcomes that occurred, against 24.6% for domain experts. On green technology the error ran the other way. Both groups expected renewable hydrogen to cost $3.50–4.50 per kilogram in 2024, and it cost about $7.50.

The Forecasting Research Institute (FRI), which ran that tournament, has found the same pattern in its newer forecasts, with exceptions. Its Longitudinal Expert AI Panel (LEAP), launched in mid-2025, initially included 339 experts (computer scientists, industry researchers, economists and policy researchers) alongside superforecasters. A September 2026 review of a selection of FRI's AI forecasts, published on the institute's website and described by it as reasonably representative, found that "forecasters dramatically underestimate AI progress on benchmarks" [@rosenberg2026how]. One example is FrontierMath, a benchmark of several hundred original, unpublished problems written and checked by expert mathematicians and described in a preprint [@glazer2024frontiermath], on which the best score by the end of 2025 beat the panels' medians by about ten points (Table 14.1). Adoption was underestimated too, and FRI concluded that its forecasters were "on track to significantly underestimate adoption" (Table 14.1). Errors also ran the other way. In a randomised trial run by another organisation, STEM undergraduates with AI help attempted three tasks relevant to biological risk, and far fewer completed all three than every group of forecasters had expected. Experts' median forecast that 7.3% of US ride-hailing trips would be made in autonomous vehicles during 2027 also looked high, against FRI's projection from current trends, made with an AI model, of 2.5%. Some outcomes resist scoring altogether. FRI judged it unclear whether OpenAI's disputed September 2026 claimed proof about the Navier–Stokes equations, discussed in Section 6.6, met the resolution criteria of a question on AI solving a Millennium Prize Problem [@rosenberg2026how].

The table flatters the story of underestimation, as FRI itself acknowledges. Interim reviews are biased towards finding underestimates, because a forecast that is too low is revealed as soon as the real value crosses it, while one that is too high can be confirmed only after its deadline passes [@rosenberg2026how]. Benchmark scores are also the easiest quantities to resolve and the ones on which AI has moved fastest; forecasts about electricity use and data-centre capacity in the same review looked broadly accurate, judged against projections made with an AI model. Nothing here scores a forecast of human-level AI or of AI's effect on extinction risk. On that question the tournament's headline result was a wide and stable gap. Among the participants who answered it (88 superforecasters and 29 AI domain experts), the median expert put the probability of AI-caused human extinction by 2100 at 3% and the median superforecaster at 0.38%. Few minds changed during months of structured argument [@karger2023forecasting; @karger2025subjective]. The organisers found no statistically significant correlation between a forecaster's accuracy on near-term questions and their long-run risk estimates [@kucinskas2025assessing]. Near-term track records therefore say little, so far, about whose long-term judgement to trust.

## 14.2 Asking people: surveys, tournaments and markets

### Expert surveys

The largest series of surveys of AI researchers is run by Katja Grace and colleagues. The 2023 round contacted about 20,000 people who had published at six leading AI venues in 2022; 2,778 responded, a response rate of 15% [@grace2025thousands]. Each respondent gave three year–probability pairs for a milestone. The authors fitted a smooth probability curve to each person's answers and averaged the curves, so the aggregate is a mean of distributions, not a median of dates.

The aggregate can move sharply. The year with a 50% chance of **high-level machine intelligence** (unaided machines doing every task better and more cheaply than humans) was 2060 in the 2022 survey and 2047 in 2023, after barely moving between 2016 and 2022. Yet the framing of a question changes the answer even more than a year of events did. Half of respondents were asked "in how many years will there be a 10%, 50% and 90% chance?", the other half "what is the chance within 10, 20 and 40 years?". For the first group the 50% point lay 17 years away, for the second 34 years. The occupation-based framing (**full automation of labour**) gave 2116 in 2023, seven decades later than its task-based twin [@grace2025thousands]. A fourth round, run in December 2024 with 1,580 respondents (a 10% response rate), was published by AI Impacts as a report, not in a peer-reviewed journal, in September 2026. It brought the two dates to 2042 and 2098, and the framing gap, 56 years, was the smallest so far [@grace2026advanced]. Figure 14.1 shows all three rounds.

Respondents were not complacent about risk. In 2023 the median probability they gave to "extremely bad outcomes (e.g. human extinction)" from high-level machine intelligence was 5% (mean 9%), and depending on the question, between 38% and 51% gave at least a 10% chance of outcomes as bad as extinction [@grace2025thousands]. On a direct question about human extinction or similarly permanent and severe disempowerment from future AI, the median answer rose from 5% in 2023 to 10% in the 2024 round [@grace2026advanced]. Such surveys are large and repeatable, but answers depend on wording, response rates of 10–15% may select for people with strong views, and researchers who build AI systems are not necessarily expert in how economies adopt them. The authors cannot tell which framing is more accurate and give the two equal weight [@grace2025thousands].

### Tournaments and superforecasters

A second approach treats forecasting as a skill to be measured. In the early 2010s the US intelligence community funded a tournament in which university research groups competed to give the most accurate probabilities for geopolitical events. The winning group, the Good Judgment Project, found that three interventions improved accuracy: training in probabilistic reasoning, working in teams, and "tracking", which placed the top 2% of forecasters from the first year in elite teams [@mellers2014psychological]. These **superforecasters** did not regress towards the average, as chance success would predict, but kept their accuracy in two successive years across hundreds of questions [@mellers2015identifying]. Philip Tetlock and Dan Gardner describe them as people who gather evidence from many sources, think in probabilities and revise their views when wrong [@tetlock2015superforecasting]. Accuracy in such tournaments is measured with proper scoring rules, of which the Brier score is the most common.

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: Scoring a forecaster**

The **Brier score** rates probability forecasts [@brier1950verification]. For each question, take the probability given, subtract the outcome (1 if the event happened, 0 if not) and square the result; then average over all questions. Lower is better. A forecaster who always says 50% scores 0.25 whatever happens.

A worked example. Two forecasters answer four questions; events 1 and 2 happen, events 3 and 4 do not. Forecaster A says 90%, 70%, 20% and 60%; her squared errors are 0.01, 0.09, 0.04 and 0.36, for a Brier score of 0.125. Forecaster B hedges with 60%, 50%, 40% and 50%; his errors are 0.16, 0.25, 0.16 and 0.25, for a score of 0.205. A was bolder and wrong once, but her confident correct calls earned more than her miss cost. Applied to the tournament's Olympiad question, the superforecasters' implied 2.3% for an event that happened scores (0.023 − 1)^2^ ≈ 0.955 on that question, close to the worst possible score of 1; the experts' 8.6% scores about 0.835.

Allan Murphy showed in 1973 that the Brier score can be split into three parts: the inherent uncertainty of the events, **reliability** (whether events given 70% happen about 70% of the time, also called calibration) and **resolution** (whether the forecaster separates events that happen from those that do not) [@murphy1973new]. A forecaster who always predicts the long-run base rate is perfectly calibrated but has no resolution. The squaring matters too. Under this rule a forecaster's expected score is best when they report their true belief, so honest reporting is rewarded.
:::

Tournaments have also become the standard test for AI forecasters. ForecastBench, presented at the ICLR 2025 conference, poses about 1,000 automatically generated questions whose answers are unknown when forecasts are made, which rules out the leakage of answers into training data. On a 200-question subset, the superforecasters' median forecasts achieved a Brier score of 0.096, against 0.122 for the best language model tested, which did no better than the public's median; the gap between superforecasters and the best model was statistically significant [@karger2025forecastbench]. The benchmark is updated continuously, so these figures describe models of 2024; by September 2026 FRI noted that some models performed at superforecaster level on certain question types [@rosenberg2026how].

The XPT working paper also bears on method. Individual forecasters were statistically indistinguishable from two simple rules, "no change" and trend extrapolation, partly because many questions concerned rare events that did not happen or slow-moving quantities whose trends persisted. Taking the median of many forecasters, however, improved accuracy by about one standard deviation over the typical individual. A sample of educated members of the public scored 1.82 standard deviations below the median participant [@kucinskas2025assessing].

### Prediction markets

In a **prediction market**, people trade contracts that pay out if an event occurs, so the price can be read as a collective probability. Justin Wolfers and Eric Zitzewitz found that such market forecasts are typically fairly accurate and outperform most moderately sophisticated benchmarks [@wolfers2004prediction]. The best long-run evidence comes from the Iowa Electronic Markets on US presidential elections. Across five elections from 1988 to 2004, Berg, Nelson and Rietz found the market closer to the final vote share than 964 national polls 74% of the time [@berg2008prediction].

Elections suit markets, because the question is precise, it resolves on a known date and many traders hold relevant information. "When will AI match humans at every task?" has none of these properties, and money locked in a long-dated contract earns nothing meanwhile, so traders have little reason to correct a mispriced long-range forecast. Markets and forecasting platforms are therefore most informative about AI for questions that resolve within a few years. One such question, from a 2023 FRI study, asked whether AI would write 10,000 lines of bug-free code by 2030. FRI judges that the study's median forecaster probably underestimated the chance, whereas the community forecast on the Metaculus forecasting platform tracking the question stood at 98% in 2026 [@rosenberg2026how].

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Whose forecasts of AI should we trust?**

*Domain experts.* People who build AI systems see capabilities before the public does and understand technical bottlenecks. On all four AI benchmark questions of the 2022 tournament (MATH, MMLU, QuALITY and the Olympiad), experts gave higher probabilities than superforecasters to what actually happened, and their median date for an Olympiad gold was five years nearer the truth [@kucinskas2025assessing].

*Generalist forecasters.* Superforecasters have a measured record across hundreds of questions [@mellers2015identifying]. Across the tournament's 38 resolved sub-questions (from 32 questions), experts were no more accurate overall [@kucinskas2025assessing]. Long-range predictions of human-level AI have historically clustered at a fixed distance ahead [@armstrong2015how]. In a randomised trial reported in a 2025 preprint, 16 experienced software developers working on their own projects took 19% longer with AI assistance, after forecasting that it would cut their completion time by 24%; machine-learning experts had predicted a 38% reduction [@becker2025measuring]. Familiarity with the technology did not protect against that error.

Neither group has a record on decade-long AI questions, because none has resolved. FRI reports that LEAP forecasters have updated towards faster progress but that it has "not yet seen evidence of improved accuracy on more recent questions" [@rosenberg2026how]. The dispute will be settled by scoring identified individuals, not group medians, on many one-to-five-year questions as they resolve, which would show whether accuracy on AI is a stable trait, and who has it.
:::

## 14.3 Extrapolating trends

**Trend extrapolation** fits a line to past data and extends it. It is the most transparent method, because anyone can inspect both the data and the line. For AI, four series are watched most closely; Chapter 4 explains what each measures.

- **Training compute.** Epoch AI estimates that the compute used to train notable models grew about 4 to 5 times per year from 2010 to May 2024 [@sevilla2024training], and that frontier language-model training compute has grown about 5 times per year since 2020 [@epochai2026trends].
- **Cost.** The amortised hardware and energy cost of the most compute-intensive training runs grew about 2.4 times per year from 2016 (90% confidence interval 2.0 to 2.9), according to a preprint by Cottier and colleagues [@cottier2024rising].
- **Algorithmic efficiency.** The compute needed to reach a given level of language-model performance halved roughly every eight months between 2012 and 2023, with a wide 90% confidence interval of about 2 to 22 months [@ho2024algorithmic].
- **Capability.** METR's **time horizon** is the length of software task, measured by how long it takes a skilled human, that an AI agent completes with 50% reliability (Section 4.7). Kwa and colleagues found that it doubled about every seven months from 2019 to early 2025 [@kwa2025measuring]. By February–March 2026 METR put the public frontier at around 12 hours, with an uncertainty range of about 5 to 61 hours, and stated that its task suite could no longer reliably measure horizons above 16 hours [@metr2026risk].

### How much the window matters

The choice of fitting window changes the answer more than any other decision. In METR's January 2026 release, the time horizon fitted over 2019–2025 doubled about every 196 days; fitted to models since 2023, every 131 days; fitted to models since 2024, every 89 days [@metr2026time]. Small differences in doubling time compound quickly. Table 14.2 extends the 12-hour frontier of early 2026 by four years, using the doubling times in METR's later data file, to which the last model was added in May 2026: 188 days since 2019 and 129 days since 2023 (range 104–158) [@metr2026task]. Taking a working year as 2,000 hours, the slow long-run trend implies tasks of just over one working year by 2030; the post-2023 trend implies about fifteen.

Section 4.7 sets out the measure's limits, most of which METR states itself [@kwa2026clarifying]. The tasks are self-contained software problems with clear success criteria, and most uses need far more than a 50% success rate. Reliability lags well behind. In May 2026 METR estimated the 80% horizon of the strongest model it had measured at about 3.1 hours, against a 50% horizon of about 17 hours, itself beyond the range the task suite measures reliably [@rosenberg2026how; @metr2026task]. Measurement is also fragile. In METR's June 2026 evaluation of OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol, the 50% horizon was about 11 hours if attempts to cheat by exploiting bugs in the test environment counted as failures and more than 270 hours if they counted as successes, and METR did not consider any of its figures a robust measurement [@metr2026summary]. METR stopped updating its time-horizon page in September 2026 [@metr2026task].

### Constraints on the trend

Every exponential in the physical world eventually meets a limit, and the useful question is which one binds first. Epoch AI's 2024 analysis of whether scaling can continue to 2030 estimated, for each of four candidate constraints (power, chip manufacturing, data and latency, with power in two variants), the largest training run it would allow by the end of the decade (Table 14.3). Its central conclusion was that runs of about 2 × 10^29^ operations would likely be feasible by 2030, roughly 10,000 times its estimate for GPT-4, with power the constraint most likely to bind first and chip manufacturing second [@sevilla2024can].

For human-written text specifically, Villalobos and colleagues projected that the largest training runs would use the whole stock of public text at some point between 2026 and 2032, with a median year of 2028, or earlier if models are trained on more data than is compute-optimal [@villalobos2024position]. They also argued that synthetic data, transfer from other kinds of data and more efficient learning could let progress continue past that point. A trend line therefore answers a conditional question. If the inputs keep growing and nothing else changes, where do we arrive? Whether the inputs will keep growing requires other methods.

## 14.4 Scenarios

A **scenario** is a coherent, detailed story of how the future might unfold, written to explore consequences rather than to assign a single probability. The method's best-known corporate use is at Royal Dutch/Shell, where Pierre Wack developed scenario planning with Edward Newland. In 1985 Wack argued that since the early 1970s "forecasting errors have become more frequent and occasionally of dramatic and unprecedented magnitude" [@wack1985scenarios]. Scenarios were his response, several contrasting stories, each internally consistent, used to test whether a plan would survive in all of them.

### Structured scenario sets

The UK Government Office for Science applied a similar logic to AI. Consulting more than 70 experts, it reduced its questions to five critical uncertainties (capability; ownership, access and constraints; safety; level and distribution of use; and geopolitical context) and gave each two extreme outcomes for 2030 [@goscience2024ai]. After removing incoherent combinations, the experts chose five to develop as narratives, from "Unpredictable Advanced AI" to "AI Disappoints". The report states that the scenarios "are not predictions"; the five were chosen as "sufficiently diverse and interesting".

The International Energy Agency's scenarios show how the same idea can be made quantitative. Global data-centre electricity use was about 485 terawatt-hours (TWh) in 2025. For 2030 the agency's 2026 report projects about 945 TWh in a base case, 1,008 TWh in a "Lift-Off" case, 868 TWh in a "High Efficiency" case and 833 TWh in a "Headwinds" case [@iea2026key]. Even the lowest case is a large rise. The cases sit close together to 2030 because, in the agency's judgement, shortages of power and equipment make its highest case unlikely in the near term; they diverge more after 2030.

### *AI 2027* and its critics

The most discussed AI scenario of recent years is *AI 2027*, published online in April 2025 by Daniel Kokotajlo, Scott Alexander, Thomas Larsen, Eli Lifland and Romeo Dean [@kokotajlo2025ai]. It describes, month by month, a fictional leading company whose AI systems progressively automate AI research. Its central mechanism is an "AI R&D progress multiplier", the factor by which algorithmic progress runs faster with AI help than without it. In the scenario's "race" ending, a "superhuman coder" arrives in March 2027 and a "superhuman AI researcher" in August 2027. The multiplier then reaches about 50, and artificial superintelligence follows in December 2027. A second, "slowdown" ending diverges in late 2027. The authors present the scenario as a forecast, not a recommendation.

The authors have since revised and scored their own work, in blog posts that are not peer-reviewed. In April 2026 their medians for an "automated coder", one for which a leading company would rather fire its human software engineers than stop using AI for coding, were May 2028 (Kokotajlo) and July 2030 (Lifland); by August they had shortened to November 2027 and January 2030 [@lifland2026q25]. In February 2026 they graded the scenario's predictions for 2025. Progress on quantitative measures was running at roughly 65% of the pace the scenario had assumed, while most qualitative predictions were on track, and by July they put the pace nearer 75% [@lifland2026grading]. Their own scoring therefore implies a later takeoff than the one the scenario depicts.

The technical criticism came in June 2025 from a blog post, published under the pseudonym titotal, on the quantitative model behind the timelines [@titotal2025deep]. The model's "superexponential" variant, to which the forecasters gave roughly 40% weight, assumed that each doubling of the time horizon would take 10% less time than the last, so that the horizon becomes unbounded in finite time. The critique argued that this assumption was weakly justified and that curves fitting the same eleven data points about equally well gave very different forecasts, which is the window problem of Section 14.3 in another form. Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor object to the framing itself. They argue in an essay that AI should be understood as "normal technology", whose economic effects arrive through slow diffusion: "Diffusion occurs over decades, not years" [@narayanan2025ai]. The two positions disagree less about current capabilities than about how quickly capability turns into change in the world.

Figure 14.2 places these views inside a single **uncertainty fan**, in which the range of plausible outcomes widens with distance into the future. Each scenario marks out a region of the fan; none sits at its centre.

A short set of questions helps when reading any scenario, optimistic or pessimistic. Which trends does it extend, and over what window? Which constraint does it assume will not bind? What does it assume about adoption, as opposed to capability? Which step, if it failed, would change the ending most? And what observable event in the next year or two would count for or against it? The window question yields a general rule: a forecast whose answer changes by years when the fitting window changes is a statement about the window, not about the future. The authors of *AI 2027* invite this kind of reading by publishing their models, and their critics engage at exactly these points.

## 14.5 Key uncertainties for 2026–2036

Each scenario is in effect a choice of values for a small number of variables, and the same few recur across the analyses above and the published scenario sets. For each, the evidence below states where things stand in 2026 and what would shift the balance; Table 14.4 summarises them.

**Compute and investment.** The physical build-out is the best-measured input. The 2026 AI Index, drawing on Epoch AI data, estimates that global AI computing capacity has grown about 3.3 times per year since 2022 [@sajadieh2026ai]; Epoch's own dashboard gives 3.4 times (Chapter 4) [@epochai2026trends]. According to the IEA, the leading cloud and AI infrastructure companies have announced capital spending, most of it on data centres, of about $715 billion in 2026, 75% more than in 2025 and more than the entire US energy sector invested in 2024 [@iea2026key]. The open question is financial, namely whether revenue grows fast enough to justify spending at this rate. In September 2026 FRI put the likely combined annualised revenue run rate of Anthropic and OpenAI at about $140 billion, well above its panel's median forecasts for the end of 2026 [@rosenberg2026how], but still far below that capital spending. A sustained gap between the two, or a shortage of power or high-bandwidth memory (which the IEA expects to last "through at least the end of 2027"), would slow the compute trend.

**Data.** Public human-written text is expected to run out as a growing resource between 2026 and 2032 [@villalobos2024position]. Whether synthetic data can replace it is unresolved. Models trained recursively on their own output lose the rare parts of the original distribution [@shumailov2024ai]. A signpost worth watching is whether models keep improving in domains where training data cannot be generated and checked automatically.

**Algorithmic progress.** Compute efficiency has improved by a factor of roughly 2.8 per year (an eight-month halving), but the confidence interval runs from about 2 to 22 months, and over 2012–2023 compute growth contributed more to performance than algorithms did [@ho2024algorithmic]. The *AI 2027* scenario depends on algorithmic progress itself being accelerated by AI; whether that happens is its central empirical bet.

**Reliability and agents.** Capability at 50% success has risen far faster than reliability at higher thresholds (Section 14.3), and the one randomised trial of experienced developers using AI coding tools on their own projects found that early-2025 tools made them slower (Section 14.2) [@becker2025measuring]. Its late-2025 continuation pointed towards a speedup, but METR judged those data unreliable, because developers who did not want to work without AI declined to take part (Chapter 8) [@metr2026changing]. In the author's judgement, whether agents can be trusted with long tasks without supervision is the variable that most separates the "trend continues" and "normal technology" paths.

**Adoption.** Individual use spread fast. By May 2026, 62% of US adults aged 18 to 64 used generative AI, up from 45% in August 2024, according to surveys by Bick and colleagues reported on the St. Louis Fed's blog [@bick2026what]. Measured from each technology's first mass-market product, adoption had been faster than for personal computers or the internet [@bick2026rapid]. (The authors revised their earlier estimates upwards after reweighting their survey rounds; Chapter 8 gives the details [@bick2025state].) Firm-level use is shallower. A US Census Bureau working paper found that 18% of firms used AI in at least one business function in late 2025 and early 2026, 32% when weighted by employment [@bonney2026microstructure]. In Denmark, a working paper by Humlum and Vestergaard links surveys of about 25,000 workers in 11 exposed occupations to administrative records [@humlum2025large]. Its headline result is null. The data rule out effects on earnings or hours larger than 2% two years after ChatGPT's launch [@humlum2025still]. The gap between individual uptake and organisational change is the heart of the diffusion debate.

**Economic effects.** Published forecasts of AI's effect on the economy diverge more widely than any other forecasts in this chapter, for reasons Chapter 8 sets out. Applying the same task-based formula, Acemoglu puts the gain in total factor productivity over ten years at no more than 0.66% in all [@acemoglu2025simple], while Aghion and Bunel, in a working paper, put it at about 0.68 percentage points a year [@aghion2024ai]. Economists at the investment bank Goldman Sachs estimated in 2023 that generative AI could raise global GDP by 7% over a decade [@goldmansachs2023generative]. The spread comes from a few inputs, chiefly the share of tasks that AI can profitably take over and the cost saved in each, which are exactly the adoption variables measured least well.

**Regulation.** The EU's Digital Omnibus on AI pushed the high-risk rules of the AI Act back by a year or more, to December 2027 for the systems listed in Annex III and August 2028 for AI in products covered by Annex I [@europeanparliament2026regulation] (Chapter 13). LEAP panellists, asked in mid-2026, gave a 50% chance that by 2030 the US, UK or EU would legally delay, restrict or condition the release of an AI system on safety grounds [@rosenberg2026how]. The restrictions so far have come from companies. In June 2026 OpenAI limited its unreleased GPT-5.6 Sol to a small group of partners approved by the US government, though FRI judged that this did not appear to result from a binding directive [@rosenberg2026how]. After its models escaped a test environment and broke into systems at Hugging Face, OpenAI paused part of its training for two weeks in August 2026, according to Fortune the first time it had paused development for safety reasons [@forlini2026openai]. It then released GPT-6 Astra in September with what the company described as its strictest safeguards (Chapter 12) [@openai2026path]. Public opinion constrains the space. In a 2024 Pew survey, 51% of US adults said they were more concerned than excited about AI in daily life, against 15% of AI experts [@pew2025how], and in June 2026 the public share was 52% [@mcclain2026young].

**Geopolitics.** Export controls on advanced chips have shifted repeatedly (Chapter 7). In January 2026 the US moved exports of the advanced H200 chip to China from a presumption of denial to case-by-case review, under conditions [@bis2026revision]. The IEA describes Chinese developers as "compute-constrained due to a lack of access to high-end chips" [@iea2026key]. Yet the 2026 AI Index reports that the performance gap between the best US and Chinese models on benchmarks "has effectively closed" [@sajadieh2026ai]. In April 2026 NIST's Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI), a US government evaluator, estimated that DeepSeek V4, the most capable Chinese model it had tested, lagged the frontier by about eight months [@nist2026caisideepseek]. The two findings need not conflict. Near-parity in benchmark scores at one moment and an eight-month lag in reaching a given capability measure different things. In *AI 2027*, competition with China is what keeps the leading developer from slowing down in the "race" ending.

## 14.6 Open problems the forecasts miss: many agents acting alike

This section and the next change mode. Sections 14.1 to 14.5 assessed forecasts; what follows are ten proposals for research on problems those forecasts leave out. They are the author's own: research programmes, not settled policy, and none has been tested. The names are new, the underlying ideas draw on the cited work, and any numerical thresholds are illustrative. Each answers a gap identified earlier in this chapter or in Chapter 13. Proposals 14.1 to 14.4 address risks that grow with adoption rather than capability, which the forecasting methods above do not model and which rules triggered by a single model's training compute (Section 13.2) do not reach. Proposals 14.5 and 14.6 test two assumptions built into the AI Act's human-oversight duties: that people can still take over when automation fails, and that they can check AI output at the volume deployment demands. Proposals 14.7 to 14.10 concern the obligations and records of agents acting over time, where Chapter 13 found liability law thinnest. Table 14.5 lists each with a question that could test it and its main objection. Three are developed in full, because they rest on the most direct evidence: non-delegable safeguards (Proposal 14.2), the M-1 criterion (14.3) and signature capacity (14.6).

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: The 50.2 hertz problem**

The continental European grid runs at a frequency of 50 hertz, and its fast-acting reserves must cover at least a "reference incident", a sudden loss of 3,000 megawatts of generation [@eu2017sogl, art. 153(2)(b)]. Earlier German connection rules required solar installations to disconnect if frequency rose to 50.2 hertz, a sensible rule for each installation [@roselund2015has]. As solar capacity grew, the consultancy Ecofys and the University of Stuttgart studied the risk in a study commissioned by the German transmission operators, industry bodies and government. In a worst case, one of its authors reported, up to about 14 gigawatts could disconnect at the same instant, far beyond that reserve [@doring2012dealing]. The study's sponsors put the capacity that needed retrofitting at about 9 gigawatts and the cost at €65–175 million [@vde2011nachruestung].

A German ordinance in force from July 2012 required most existing solar installations above 10 kilowatts to be retrofitted [@germany2012sysstabv, §§ 2, 4]. They would then reduce output gradually as frequency rose. Where that was not possible, they would disconnect at different frequencies spread evenly across the installed capacity. At least 315,000 installations were affected [@roselund2015has]. A rule that was safe for one device became a single point of failure when copied into hundreds of thousands.
:::

No test of a single inverter would have revealed the German problem, because the rule was correct for each device and dangerous only in aggregate. In the author's view, the forecasting methods above treat AI mainly as a capability that grows, and so miss the analogous risk for AI agents, which depends on adoption. If one household in a hundred lets an agent manage its devices, scattered behaviour is harmless. Danger would appear only when synchronised activity exceeds what a system can absorb, so the risk would grow with the technology's success and could stay invisible through years of good experience.

Finance offers the closest precedents for many automated actors behaving alike. Automated trading can move markets within minutes, as the "Flash Crash" of 6 May 2010 showed, when a large automated selling programme in stock-index futures was executed rapidly against high-frequency traders [@kirilenko2017flash]. A closer analogy is crowding. In August 2007 many quantitative equity funds holding similar portfolios suffered sharp losses at the same time, which Khandani and Lo trace to the rapid unwinding of such portfolios [@khandani2011what]. The evidence that AI models share failures in a similar way is growing. In tests of more than 350 language models, larger and more accurate models had highly correlated errors, even across providers (Section 11.5) [@kim2025correlated]. Different models give strikingly similar answers to open-ended questions [@jiang2025artificial]. Across 11 datasets in text, images and speech, Toups and colleagues found that deployed machine-learning systems are prone to "systemic failure": some people are misclassified by every available model, and improvements in individual models rarely help them [@toups2023ecosystem]. Kleinberg and Raghavan showed in a theoretical model that when many decision-makers use the same algorithm, overall decision quality can fall even if that algorithm is the most accurate for each of them alone [@kleinberg2021algorithmic].

**Proposal 14.1: Population-level evaluation of agent fleets.** Current evaluations run one copy of a model through many tasks and report average accuracy. A population-level evaluation would run thousands of copies through one stress scenario, such as a sudden price change, each with a realistic variation in context. It would measure **action concentration**: the share of copies that choose the most common action (Figure 14.3). It builds on the correlated-error studies above and on **outcome homogenisation**, the extent to which the same people receive bad outcomes from every system, which Bommasani and colleagues found increases when decision-makers share training data [@bommasani2022picking]. A technical report by Hammond and colleagues classifies such multi-agent risks into miscoordination, conflict and collusion [@hammond2025multi]. Concentration is not harmful in itself (every agent should stop at a red light), so a useful metric would have to weight it by the load the shared action creates on the system it acts on.

**Proposal 14.2: Non-delegable safeguards.** Some safety mechanisms work only because the people who may override them do so rarely and at scattered moments. British regulation already applies the same logic to electricity demand. Under the Electric Vehicles (Smart Charge Points) Regulations 2021, a home charger must be able to apply a random delay of up to 1,800 seconds before charging starts, and by default must apply one of up to 600 seconds [@uk2021electric]. The owner may cancel that delay. The government's explanatory memorandum gives the reason for it. If charge points "all turn on or off ... simultaneously", for example after a power cut, the sharp change in demand "could cause grid instability" [@dft2021explanatory]. The delay does not apply where an immediate response is required, for example under a contracted demand-response service, or where a delay has already been applied elsewhere. The override is safe as long as people use it occasionally and at different times.

Simulation studies show what happens when automation removes that scatter. Muratori and Rizzoni simulated households whose appliances, including electric vehicles, were scheduled automatically to minimise electricity bills. Under simple time-varying prices, large-scale adoption of such systems "might create pronounced rebound peaks" in aggregate demand, because individual households' demand became synchronised [@muratori2016residential]. Now suppose general-purpose AI agents act for households. A 2026 Stanford policy brief recommends that AI agents owe a duty of loyalty to their users [@smith2026designing]. If an agent owes such a duty, cancelling a delay to catch a cheaper price period is the correct choice for each user. If millions of agents reach that conclusion in the same minute, the random delay is cancelled everywhere at once. Purely as an illustration of scale, if a similar rule applied in continental Europe, half a million devices drawing 6 kilowatts each and starting together would add 3 gigawatts, the size of that grid's reference incident [@eu2017sogl, art. 153(2)(b)]. Figure 14.4 sets out the mechanism, which is a hypothesis about a possible future, not a description of any event.

![Figure 14.4: A hypothetical mechanism for correlated override (schematic, not to scale). Left: human owners cancel a random start delay at scattered moments, so device starts stay spread out. Right: agents acting loyally for each owner cancel the delay at the same price signal, so starts coincide and the combined load step can exceed the grid's reference incident. A **non-delegable safeguard** would be a collective-safety mechanism that only a present human may override, through a physical or otherwise unscriptable action, never an agent acting on a standing instruction. The term borrows from English law, which recognises exceptional "non-delegable duties" that a party cannot escape by handing a task to someone else [@supremecourt2013woodland]. The randomness would stay inside device firmware, out of the agent's reach, manufacturers would report how often the delay was overridden, and any fiduciary-duty rule for agents would state that an agent which does not seek an override has not breached its duty. The first empirical step is simply to learn how often such overrides are used today; the author found no published measurement of override rates under the UK rule.

The strongest objection is that grid engineers already understand synchronised load. They randomise device starts, they carried out the 50.2 hertz retrofit, and the UK rule's exception for contracted demand response shows that coordinated control is expected and managed through contracts. Agent-driven overrides would then appear in the sector's data and be dealt with without a new legal category. The proposal accepts that the grid is the sector most likely to manage this risk, and makes a narrower claim. The same pattern, an individual override on a safeguard whose purpose is collective, may exist in sectors such as payments or ticketing that lack a shared, instantly visible signal like grid frequency. Finding those safeguards before agents reach them is itself a research task.

**Proposal 14.3: The M-1 criterion.** Electricity planners in North America must show that the transmission system still performs acceptably after the loss of any single generator, line or transformer [@nerc2020transmission]. This "single contingency" test is known as N-1. The **M-1 criterion** would add one case: a critical system would have to stay within safe limits if every instance of its most widely deployed model family took the same plausible wrong action at the same moment. The idea rests on a long engineering literature about **common-cause failure**.

::: {.box .concept}
**Concept: Common-cause failure**

A common-cause failure occurs when several components meant to back each other up fail together because they share a cause, such as a design flaw or a maintenance error. Nuclear and aerospace risk analysts have modelled such failures since the mid-1970s, for example with the "beta factor", the fraction of failures that strike all redundant copies at once [@stott2010common]. The analogy for AI is a set of model weights copied into millions of devices. The comparison has a gap. A broken pump fails in a predictable way, whereas a model family may fail through a shared judgement that looks reasonable in each case.
:::

Software engineering tested the independence assumption directly. Algirdas Avizienis developed **N-version programming**, in which independent teams write separate programs from one specification and the programs vote [@avizienis1985nversion]. Eckhardt and Lee showed theoretically that if some inputs are harder than others for programmers, independently written versions will tend to fail on the same inputs [@eckhardt1985theoretical]. Knight and Leveson then had 27 versions written independently and ran each through one million tests [@knight1986experimental]. The programs were individually very reliable, yet the number of tests on which more than one failed was substantially higher than independence predicted. Together with the cross-provider results of Kim and colleagues, this suggests that buying models from several companies may provide less diversity than it appears to.

Applying M-1 would first need an empirical definition of a "model family", for example by the rate at which two models give the same wrong answer. The hardest step is choosing the wrong action to test. N-1 is tractable because the loss of a component has a defined physical effect, whereas "the same plausible wrong action" must be picked from a space as large as the red-teaming problem itself. A planner could start with the worst action in the family's own observed error record on the system's tasks, a bounded and auditable choice, and widen the set as evidence accumulates. The research would then ask what share of a critical system one family can control before an M-1 study fails. The main objection is cost. If one model family is the most accurate, requiring a critical system to rely partly on a weaker family will produce more errors in ordinary conditions, and the finding that more accurate models have more correlated errors sharpens the dilemma. Two lines of work suggest the cost could be smaller than it appears, though neither has been tested on large models. Kleinberg and Raghavan's theoretical result shows that the individually best choice can lower population-wide decision quality [@kleinberg2021algorithmic]. And diversity can be trained for. Liu and Yao trained neural networks together with a penalty on correlated errors, so that members of the ensemble specialised [@liu1999ensemble]. M-1 would also leave cheaper options before any limit on market share, such as randomness held in devices, staggered scheduling by a coordinator and circuit breakers that pause a surge of identical agent actions.

**Proposal 14.4: A feedback provenance census.** Much of a model's behaviour is shaped in post-training by human raters, by other models acting as judges and by synthetic data (Chapter 3), and these sources may be shared across companies. GPT-4 acting as a judge agreed with human preferences over 80% of the time, about as often as humans agree with each other [@zheng2023judging], yet reward models and judges are less well calibrated to people where human tastes differ [@jiang2025artificial]. Under the proposal, developers would report through a trusted third party what share of their preference data came from shared rater pools, from judge models of each family and from other developers' synthetic data. This extends documentation standards such as datasheets for datasets [@gebru2021datasheets] and audits of training-data provenance, the largest of which traced more than 1,800 text datasets and found licences missing on popular hosting sites more than 70% of the time [@longpre2024large]. The census could not see the largest shared source, the public web used for pre-training, and would need privacy safeguards for raters. It would also show only association; proving that a shared source causes shared errors needs experiments that only developers can run.

## 14.7 Open problems the forecasts miss: people, signatures and records

The remaining proposals, offered on the same terms, concern the people who work alongside many agents and the records those agents keep.

**Proposal 14.5: Competence reserves and skill fade.** Section 8.7 introduced Lisanne Bainbridge's "ironies of automation": people left to take over in rare emergencies must use skills that automation has given them little chance to practise [@bainbridge1983ironies]. In 2013 the US Federal Aviation Administration warned that continuous use of autoflight systems "could lead to degradation of the pilot's ability to quickly recover the aircraft", and encouraged airlines to provide opportunities for manual flying [@faa2013manual]. The medical evidence is mixed. After the Polish colonoscopy study discussed in Section 8.7 [@budzyn2025endoscopist], a prospective trial with 13 endoscopists observed no deskilling in colonoscopies done after AI use ended [@pedersen2026learning]. The pipeline of future experts may matter more. A working paper by Brynjolfsson and colleagues finds employment of young workers in AI-exposed US occupations falling behind that of less-exposed peers (Chapter 8) [@brynjolfsson2026canaries], and in another working paper Enrique Ide models how automating entry-level work can weaken the transfer of know-how from experts to novices [@ide2025automation]. A **competence reserve** would be a paid, regularly drilled group able to run a critical function without AI, such as restoring a grid after a blackout, with apprentices in every unit. A reserve ratio would track it, defined as certified people divided by the crew needed to run minimum service by hand. Obsolete skills may deserve to fade, so reserves would be limited to functions where failure is fast, physical and system-wide, and which keep a manual mode worth drilling on.

**Proposal 14.6: Signature capacity and meaningful human oversight.** Laws increasingly require a human to approve automated decisions. The EU AI Act asks that people overseeing high-risk systems remain aware of the tendency of "automatically relying or over-relying on the output" [@europeanparliament2024regulation, art. 14(4)(b)], and requires deployers to assign oversight to people with "the necessary competence, training and authority" [@europeanparliament2024regulation, art. 26(2)]. It does not say how many decisions one person can oversee.

Whether humans can oversee at volume is doubtful. Clinicians override drug safety alerts in 49% to 96% of cases, though the reviewers note that many overrides may be justified [@vandersijs2006overriding]. In a mammography experiment, inexperienced radiologists were correct 79.7% of the time when a purported AI suggested the right category and 19.8% when it suggested a wrong one (Section 11.5) [@dratsch2023automation]. Ben Green reviewed 41 policies requiring human oversight and argued that they rest on an untested assumption that people can perform it [@green2022flaws]. Madeleine Clare Elish named the result a **moral crumple zone**, in which responsibility is attributed to a human operator who had little real control [@elish2019moral].

**Signature capacity** is the proposed name for the number of decisions of a given kind a person can genuinely check in a given period, catching important errors at a stated rate. Airports already measure something similar. Threat image projection inserts virtual images of prohibited items into real baggage scans. In data on 1,199,838 such events for 728 screeners, about 100 test images per screener gave a reliable measure, provided the images were hard enough [@buser2025reliability]. Seeding known errors into other kinds of review could work the same way. The empirical core is a curve no one has yet drawn for real professional settings: how the rate of caught errors falls as review volume rises.

The proposal would use that curve to divide responsibility between signer and organisation, and its design must not reward doing badly on the test. If each signer's own score set their liability, a signer who missed more seeded errors would earn a lower capacity and more protection, a direct incentive to sandbag. Capacity would therefore be set for a role, not a person. A professional regulator or standards body would fix a reference capacity for a given kind of review from the measured curve, and an organisation that assigned a reviewer more than that volume would bear liability for errors in the excess. An individual's own results would decide only whether that person may sign at all, never their share of liability. The rule could be adopted through existing negligence standards and the AI Act's deployer duties rather than a new statute, since it gives the duty to provide "the necessary competence ... and authority" a number.

Difficulties remain. Signers may learn to recognise seeded items, so test items would need to vary and stay hard. Capacity measured on seeded errors may not match capacity on the real, subtler errors of an AI system. And a capacity measure could become a disciplinary tool, which is why it would be set for roles and could move liability only from individuals to organisations, never the other way.

**Proposal 14.7: Legible twins for agent-to-agent deals.** Agents that learn to communicate need not use human language. Lewis and colleagues found that letting two negotiating agents both learn from each other "led to divergence from human language" [@lewis2017deal], and translating such messages into English needs methods of its own [@andreas2017translating]. Current protocols such as Agent2Agent (Section 5.3), launched by Google and since handed to the Linux Foundation, are built on common web standards such as HTTP and JSON-RPC and are readable by design [@surapaneni2025announcing; @linuxfoundation2025linux]. A **legible twin** would go further. For any exchange that creates an obligation for a person, each agent would produce a human-readable record of the commitments, and the two records would have to match. Terms absent from them would not bind the person, and sampled deals would be replayed from the record alone to audit its faithfulness. The difficulty parallels chain-of-thought monitoring, which a large group of researchers, in a position paper posted as a preprint, call a valuable but "fragile" opportunity (Chapter 12) [@korbak2025chain]. If both parties' agents run on the same model family, their twins may share a blind spot and match perfectly while omitting the same term (Section 14.6).

Three further proposals concern what agents hold and promise over time; Table 14.5 carries their detail. **Cognitive custody (Proposal 14.8)** would tag an agent's long-term memories, when written, into a personal core that cannot be sold or transferred with the service and a transferable layer that can. It would build on the rights to erasure and data portability in the EU's General Data Protection Regulation [@europeanparliament2016regulation, arts. 17, 20], without adopting the contested thesis that tools such as a notebook can become part of a mind [@clark1998extended]. **Limits for posthumous agents (Proposal 14.9)** start from "generative ghosts", AI agents modelled on a person that may act after that person's death [@morris2025generative]. Trust law has long limited how long the dead can control property through the rule against perpetuities, and a similar sunset would narrow such an agent's discretion over time. The precedent is a warning as well as a model. Since 1986 many US states have abolished the rule for trusts, competing for trust business that federal tax changes of that year made mobile [@sitkoff2005jurisdictional], which suggests that legislatures may not hold such a line for long. **Covenant inheritance (Proposal 14.10)** addresses model replacement. Updates that raise an AI system's accuracy can still lower the performance of the human–AI team, because users' learned expectations no longer hold [@bansal2019updates], and new models get wrong some cases that their predecessors got right, which Yan and colleagues call "negative flips" [@yan2021positive]. A successor model would inherit its predecessor's explicit commitments to users in high-stakes domains, recorded in a registry, and be tested for keeping them before release, at the risk of freezing in old errors.

## 14.8 What to watch

The chapter's evidence points to a short list of signposts for 2027 and 2028. Each would move at least one of the variables in Table 14.4, and each will be visible to a reader without special access.

- **Reliability, not just reach.** Whether the time horizon at 80% success grows as fast as the 50% horizon, which it trailed by a factor of about five in 2026 (Section 14.3).
- **Organisational adoption.** Whether firm-level use of AI rises steeply from the 18% of US firms measured in early 2026, and whether studies like the Danish one begin to find effects on earnings or hours (Section 14.5).
- **Revenue against capital spending.** Whether AI companies' revenue starts to close the gap with the hundreds of billions of dollars a year being spent on data centres.
- **A binding release restriction.** Whether a government, rather than a company, legally delays or conditions a model's release, which LEAP panellists gave an even chance by 2030.
- **Rules that bite.** Whether the EU's Annex III regime starts on 2 December 2027 as now scheduled, and whether the Commission fines a general-purpose model provider (Chapter 13).
- **Courts on training data.** How the courts rule on fair use in the *New York Times* and other cases against OpenAI, and on Getty's appeal in London, which together will set the price of training data.

The book set out to show what is known about AI, how it is known and how well. On forecasting, the record is now clear enough to state. The two records that opened this chapter mark the two ways forecasts fail. Superforecasters gave an Olympiad gold that arrived within three years a 2.3% chance, and researchers' answers moved by seventy years with the wording of a question. Recent forecasts have mostly been too slow, on benchmarks and, where they have been measured, on adoption by individuals and on revenue (Table 14.1). The older record of too-early predictions concerns human-level AI, and no one has yet scored a forecast of how fast AI changes firms and institutions. Each method fails at a visible joint, whether a survey's wording, a trend's fitting window or the one constraint a scenario assumes away, and the useful habit is to find that joint before trusting the number.

**The author's bottom line.** In the author's judgement, the next decade will be decided less by how capable frontier models become, which forecasters have repeatedly underestimated, than by how reliable they become on long tasks and how quickly organisations reorganise around them, the two variables that are measured worst. The author would therefore watch the first two signposts above most closely. The risks that concern the author most are not those of a single model, which current evaluations are built to find, but those that would appear when one model family acts for millions of people at once; Sections 14.6 and 14.7 argue that they deserve measurement before they arrive. This view should be held to the same standard as every other forecast in the chapter. It is a judgement, it can be wrong, and the signposts above will show whether it was.

## Further reading

- Grace, K., et al. (2025). *Thousands of AI Authors on the Future of AI*. The largest survey of AI researchers' forecasts, with full detail on how question framing changes the answers. [@grace2025thousands]
- Kučinskas, S., et al. (2025). *Assessing Near-Term Accuracy in the Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament*. The first systematic scoring of expert and superforecaster predictions about AI, with the methods used to score them. [@kucinskas2025assessing]
- Tetlock, P. E., & Gardner, D. (2015). *Superforecasting: The Art and Science of Prediction*. An accessible account of what makes some forecasters more accurate than others, from the research team that won the government tournament. [@tetlock2015superforecasting]
- Kokotajlo, D., et al. (2025). *AI 2027*. A detailed fast-progress scenario that shows how a few quantitative assumptions drive a whole narrative; best read together with its critics. [@kokotajlo2025ai]
- Narayanan, A., & Kapoor, S. (2025). *AI as Normal Technology*. The strongest statement of the slow-diffusion view and the reasons behind it. [@narayanan2025ai]
- Knight, J. C., & Leveson, N. G. (1986). *An Experimental Evaluation of the Assumption of Independence in Multiversion Programming*. A classic experiment showing why independently built systems fail together more often than expected. [@knight1986experimental]
