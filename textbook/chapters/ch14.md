# Chapter 14: Forecasting the Next Ten Years: Methods, Scenarios and Open Problems

Between June and October 2022, shortly before ChatGPT was released, 89 superforecasters (people selected for a proven record of accurate predictions) and 80 domain experts took part in a forecasting tournament on global risks. One question asked when an AI system would win a gold medal at the International Mathematical Olympiad. The median superforecaster said 2035 and the median expert 2030. In July 2025 Google DeepMind and OpenAI both reported gold-medal-level performance at that year's Olympiad. The organisers later estimated that the superforecasters had given this outcome, by 2025, a probability of 2.3% and the experts 8.6% [@kucinskas2025assessing].

A second record points the other way. In October 2023, 2,778 researchers who had published at leading AI venues were asked when unaided machines would be able to do every task better and more cheaply than human workers. Their aggregate answer gave a 50% chance by 2047. Asked instead when every occupation would be fully automatable, the same population gave 2116 [@grace2025thousands]. The two questions describe almost the same world, yet the answers lie about seventy years apart. One body of evidence shows forecasters too slow on concrete benchmarks; the other shows that long-range answers depend heavily on how the question is worded. Both are needed to judge what any forecast of AI's next ten years is worth.

## 14.1 The track record of AI forecasting

A **forecast** is a statement about the future that can later be checked: a date, a number or a probability. Forecasting AI is hard for structural reasons. Progress depends on several inputs at once (computing power, data, algorithms and money), each of which can change course. Capability is measured with benchmarks that saturate or leak into training data, so even the present is blurred (Chapter 4). Adoption by firms and governments moves at a different speed from invention. And forecasts can alter what they predict, because investors and regulators act on them.

### Early predictions

The field's founding documents set ambitious timetables. In August 1955 the organisers of the Dartmouth summer project, which gave the field its name, proposed a two-month study and wrote that "a significant advance can be made in one or more of these problems if a carefully selected group of scientists work on it together for a summer" [@mccarthy2006proposal]. In 1960 Herbert Simon predicted that "machines will be capable, within twenty years, of doing any work that a man can do" [@simon1960new, p. 38]. Neither happened on schedule. In 1972 the mathematician James Lighthill, asked by Britain's Science Research Council to review the field, concluded that "in no part of the field have the discoveries made so far produced the major impact that was then promised". He blamed a "failure to recognise the implications of the combinatorial explosion": the number of possibilities a program must search grows explosively with the size of the problem [@lighthill1973artificial]. The field's history is often described as a cycle of optimistic predictions and heavy investment ("AI springs") followed by disappointment and cuts ("AI winters") [@mitchell2021why].

The first systematic study of such predictions came from Stuart Armstrong and Kaj Sotala. They assembled a database of 257 AI predictions made since the 1950s, of which 95 gave a date for human-level AI [@armstrong2015how]. The predictions were scattered so widely that fifty-year gaps between them were common. Of the 62 predictions they classed as expert, 21 (34%) placed human-level AI 16 to 25 years after the date of the prediction. They also tested the "Maes–Garreau law", the claim that people predict AI just before the end of their own lives. Of 55 predictions for which the predictor's life expectancy could be estimated, only 12 (22%) fell within five years of it, so that idea was not supported. In a 2016 blog post Katja Grace showed that the authors had misread a column in their database: it recorded how a prediction was reached, not whether the predictor was an expert. Their claim that experts and non-experts were indistinguishable therefore does not stand. In a reply included in the same post, Armstrong argued that the tendency to predict a fixed distance ahead survived the correction: 54% of predictions by researchers working on general AI, and 27% of those by other AI researchers, fell 10 to 25 years out [@grace2016error]. A horizon that stays constant while the calendar moves suggests a habit of mind as much as evidence.

### Recent forecasts of concrete milestones

Since about 2020, forecasting has shifted towards concrete, short-range questions, and here the dominant error has been the opposite: too slow. In August 2021 Jacob Steinhardt's research group ran a contest on the Hypermind forecasting platform, whose forecasters are typically paid according to a scoring rule that rewards accurate probabilities, to predict benchmark results for June 2022. For the MATH benchmark of competition mathematics problems, the aggregate forecast was 12.7%; the best result by the deadline was 50.3%. For MMLU, a multiple-choice test of knowledge across many academic subjects, the forecast was 57.1% against an outcome of 67.5%. Both results lay far in the tails of the forecast distributions. Progress on a robustness benchmark, by contrast, was slower than forecast [@steinhardt2022ai].

The 2022 tournament described above gave the same picture at larger scale. A 2025 working paper by its organisers scored the 38 of its 172 sub-questions that had resolved by mid-2025 [@kucinskas2025assessing]. Superforecasters and domain experts were about equally accurate overall; the difference between the best and worst groups was 0.18 standard deviations and not statistically significant. Both groups underestimated AI progress, and superforecasters did so more. Across four AI benchmark questions, superforecasters had on average given a probability of 9.7% to the outcomes that occurred, against 24.6% for domain experts. On green technology the error ran the other way: both groups expected renewable hydrogen to cost $3.50–4.50 per kilogram in 2024, and it cost about $7.50.

The Forecasting Research Institute (FRI), which ran that tournament, has since collected AI forecasts from a standing panel. Its Longitudinal Expert AI Panel (LEAP), launched in mid-2025, initially included 339 experts (computer scientists, industry researchers, economists and policy researchers) alongside superforecasters. A September 2026 review of a selection of FRI's AI forecasts, which the institute describes as reasonably representative, found that "forecasters dramatically underestimate AI progress on benchmarks" [@rosenberg2026how]. Table 14.1 collects examples. The same review found errors in the other direction. In a randomised trial run by another organisation, STEM undergraduates with AI help attempted three tasks relevant to biological risk; forecasters had expected between 16% and 40% of them to complete all three, and 5.2% did. Experts' median forecast that 7.3% of US ride-hailing trips would be made in autonomous vehicles during 2027 also looked high: FRI's projection from current trends, made with an AI model, was 2.5%.

Three caveats limit what the table shows. First, interim reviews are biased towards finding underestimates. A forecast that is too low is revealed as soon as the real value crosses it, while one that is too high can be confirmed only after its deadline passes; FRI states this bias explicitly [@rosenberg2026how]. Second, benchmark scores are the easiest quantities to resolve and the ones on which AI has moved fastest; forecasts about electricity use and data-centre capacity in the same review looked roughly on track, judged against projections made with an AI model. Third, nothing here scores a forecast of human-level AI or of AI's effect on extinction risk. The tournament's headline long-run result was that the median domain expert put the probability of AI-caused human extinction by 2100 at 3% and the median superforecaster at 0.38%, and that few minds changed during months of structured argument [@karger2023forecasting; @karger2025subjective]. The organisers found no statistically significant correlation between a forecaster's accuracy on near-term questions and their long-run risk estimates [@kucinskas2025assessing]. Near-term track records therefore say little, so far, about whose long-term judgement to trust.

## 14.2 Asking people: surveys, tournaments and markets

### Expert surveys

The largest series of surveys of AI researchers is run by Katja Grace and colleagues. The 2023 round contacted about 20,000 people who had published at six leading AI venues in 2022; 2,778 responded, a response rate of 15% [@grace2025thousands]. Each respondent gave three year–probability pairs for a milestone. The authors fitted a smooth probability curve to each person's answers and averaged the curves, so the aggregate is a mean of distributions, not a median of dates.

Three results matter for anyone using the numbers. First, the aggregate moved sharply: the year with a 50% chance of **high-level machine intelligence** (unaided machines doing every task better and more cheaply than humans) was 2061 in the 2016 survey, 2060 in 2022 and 2047 in 2023. Second, the framing of the question changes the answer more than a year of events did. Half of respondents were asked "in how many years will there be a 10%, 50% and 90% chance?", the other half "what is the chance within 10, 20 and 40 years?". The first group's 50% point was 17 years away, the second group's 34 years. And the occupation-based framing (**full automation of labour**) gave 2164 in 2022 and 2116 in 2023. Third, respondents were not complacent about risk: the median probability given to "extremely bad outcomes (e.g. human extinction)" from high-level machine intelligence was 5% (mean 9%). Between 38% and 51% of respondents, depending on the question, gave at least a 10% chance of outcomes as bad as human extinction. Figure 14.1 shows the movement and the framing gap.

Such surveys are large and repeatable, but answers depend on wording, a 15% response rate may select for people with strong views, and researchers who build AI systems are not necessarily expert in how economies adopt them. The authors cannot tell which framing is more accurate and give the two equal weight.

### Tournaments and superforecasters

A second approach treats forecasting as a skill to be measured. In the early 2010s the US intelligence community funded a tournament in which university research groups competed to give the most accurate probabilities for geopolitical events. The winning group, the Good Judgment Project, found that three interventions improved accuracy: training in probabilistic reasoning, working in teams, and "tracking", which placed the top 2% of forecasters from the first year in elite teams [@mellers2014psychological]. These **superforecasters** did not regress towards the average, as chance success would predict, but kept their accuracy in two successive years across hundreds of questions [@mellers2015identifying]. Philip Tetlock and Dan Gardner describe them as people who gather evidence from many sources, think in probabilities and revise their views when wrong [@tetlock2015superforecasting]. Accuracy in such tournaments is measured with proper scoring rules, of which the Brier score is the most common.

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: Scoring a forecaster**

The **Brier score** rates probability forecasts [@brier1950verification]. For each question, take the probability given, subtract the outcome (1 if the event happened, 0 if not) and square the result; then average over all questions. Lower is better. A forecaster who always says 50% scores 0.25 whatever happens.

A worked example. Two forecasters answer four questions; events 1 and 2 happen, events 3 and 4 do not. Forecaster A says 90%, 70%, 20% and 60%; her squared errors are 0.01, 0.09, 0.04 and 0.36, for a Brier score of 0.125. Forecaster B hedges with 60%, 50%, 40% and 50%; his errors are 0.16, 0.25, 0.16 and 0.25, for a score of 0.205. A was bolder and wrong once, but her confident correct calls earned more than her miss cost. Applied to the tournament's Olympiad question, the superforecasters' implied 2.3% for an event that happened scores (0.023 − 1)^2^ ≈ 0.955 on that question, close to the worst possible score of 1; the experts' 8.6% scores about 0.835.

Allan Murphy showed in 1973 that the Brier score can be split into three parts: the inherent uncertainty of the events, **reliability** (whether events given 70% happen about 70% of the time, also called calibration) and **resolution** (whether the forecaster separates events that happen from those that do not) [@murphy1973new]. A forecaster who always predicts the long-run base rate is perfectly calibrated but has no resolution. The squaring matters too: under this rule a forecaster's expected score is best when they report their true belief, so honest reporting is rewarded.
:::

Tournaments have also become the standard test for AI forecasters. ForecastBench, presented at the ICLR 2025 conference, poses about 1,000 automatically generated questions whose answers are unknown when forecasts are made, which rules out the leakage of answers into training data. On a 200-question subset, the superforecasters' median forecasts achieved a Brier score of 0.096, the public's median forecasts 0.121 and the best language model tested (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, which was given the crowd forecasts on market questions) 0.122; the gap between superforecasters and the best model was statistically significant [@karger2025forecastbench]. The benchmark is updated continuously, so these figures describe models of 2024; by September 2026 FRI noted that some models performed at superforecaster level on certain question types [@rosenberg2026how].

The XPT working paper adds two findings that bear on method. Individual forecasters were statistically indistinguishable from two simple rules, "no change" and trend extrapolation, partly because many questions concerned rare events that did not happen or slow-moving quantities whose trends persisted. Taking the median of many forecasters, however, improved accuracy by about one standard deviation over the typical individual. A sample of educated members of the public scored 1.82 standard deviations below the median participant [@kucinskas2025assessing].

### Prediction markets

In a **prediction market**, people trade contracts that pay out if an event occurs, so the price can be read as a collective probability. Justin Wolfers and Eric Zitzewitz found that such market forecasts are typically fairly accurate and outperform most moderately sophisticated benchmarks [@wolfers2004prediction]. The best long-run evidence comes from the Iowa Electronic Markets on US presidential elections. Comparing market prices with 964 national polls across the five elections from 1988 to 2004, Berg, Nelson and Rietz found the market closer to the final vote share 74% of the time, and significantly better than polls in every election when forecasting more than 100 days ahead [@berg2008prediction].

Elections suit markets: the question is precise, it resolves on a known date and many traders hold relevant information. "When will AI match humans at every task?" has none of these properties. It is hard to define and may take decades to resolve, and money locked in a long-dated contract earns nothing meanwhile, so traders have little reason to correct a mispriced long-range forecast. Markets and forecasting platforms are therefore most informative about AI for questions such as benchmark scores, model releases or regulatory decisions within a year or two.

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Whose forecasts of AI should we trust?**

*Domain experts.* People who build AI systems see capabilities before the public does and understand technical bottlenecks. On all four AI benchmark questions of the 2022 tournament (MATH, MMLU, QuALITY and the Olympiad), experts gave higher probabilities than superforecasters to what actually happened, and their median date for an Olympiad gold was five years nearer the truth [@kucinskas2025assessing].

*Generalist forecasters.* Superforecasters have a measured record across hundreds of questions [@mellers2015identifying]. Across all 38 resolved tournament questions, experts were no more accurate overall. Long-range predictions of human-level AI, many made by AI researchers, have historically clustered at a fixed distance ahead [@armstrong2015how; @grace2016error]. In a randomised trial reported in a 2025 preprint, 16 experienced software developers took 19% longer with AI assistance, after forecasting that it would cut their completion time by 24%; machine-learning experts had predicted a 38% reduction [@becker2025measuring]. Familiarity with the technology did not protect against that error.

*What would decide it.* Neither group has a record on decade-long AI questions, because none has resolved. FRI reports that LEAP forecasters have updated towards faster progress but that it has "not yet seen evidence of improved accuracy on more recent questions" [@rosenberg2026how]. Scoring identified individuals, not group medians, on many one-to-five-year questions as they resolve would show whether accuracy on AI is a stable trait, and who has it.
:::

## 14.3 Extrapolating trends

**Trend extrapolation** fits a line to past data and extends it. It is the most transparent method, because anyone can inspect both the data and the line. For AI, four series are watched most closely; Chapter 4 explains what each measures.

- **Training compute.** Epoch AI estimates that the compute used to train notable models grew about 4 to 5 times per year from 2010 to May 2024 [@sevilla2024training], and that frontier language-model training compute has grown about 5 times per year since 2020 [@epochai2026trends].
- **Cost.** The amortised hardware and energy cost of the most compute-intensive training runs grew about 2.4 times per year from 2016 (90% confidence interval 2.0 to 2.9), according to a preprint by Cottier and colleagues [@cottier2024rising].
- **Algorithmic efficiency.** The compute needed to reach a given level of language-model performance halved roughly every eight months between 2012 and 2023, with a wide 90% confidence interval of about 2 to 22 months [@ho2024algorithmic].
- **Capability.** METR's **time horizon** is the length of software task, measured by how long it takes a skilled human, that an AI agent completes with 50% reliability. Kwa and colleagues found that it doubled about every seven months from 2019 to early 2025 [@kwa2025measuring]. In METR's data file as updated in September 2026, the doubling time is about 188 days over the whole period and about 129 days from 2023 onwards (uncertainty range 104 to 158 days) [@metr2026task]. By February–March 2026 METR put the public frontier at around 12 hours, with an uncertainty range of about 5 to 61 hours, and stated that its task suite could no longer reliably measure horizons above 16 hours [@metr2026risk].

### How much the window matters

The choice of fitting window changes the answer more than any other decision. In METR's January 2026 release, the time horizon fitted over 2019–2025 doubled about every 196 days; fitted to models since 2023, every 131 days; fitted to models since 2024, every 89 days [@metr2026time]. Each fit is honest, but they diverge quickly. Table 14.2 extends the 12-hour frontier of early 2026 by four years under different doubling times. Taking a working year as 2,000 hours, the slow long-run trend implies tasks of just over one working year by 2030; the post-2023 trend implies about fifteen.

| 158 days (upper end of post-2023 range) | 9.2 | about 610× | about 7,300 hours |

METR itself warns that the time horizon is not the length of time an AI can work independently, that its estimates carry error bars of about a factor of two, that horizons differ by orders of magnitude between domains, and that "doubling the time horizon does not double the degree of automation" [@kwa2026clarifying]. The trend is measured on self-contained software tasks with clear success criteria, which may not transfer to messier work [@kwa2025measuring]. A 50% success rate is also far below what most uses require. In May 2026 METR estimated the 80% horizon of the strongest model it had measured, Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview, at about 3.1 hours, against a 50% horizon of about 17 hours, itself beyond the range the task suite measures reliably [@rosenberg2026how; @metr2026task].

### Constraints on the trend

Every exponential in the physical world eventually meets a limit, and the useful question is which one binds first. Epoch AI's 2024 analysis of whether scaling can continue to 2030 estimated, for each of four candidate constraints, the largest training run it would allow by the end of the decade (Table 14.3). Its central conclusion was that runs of about 2 × 10^29^ operations would likely be feasible by 2030, roughly 10,000 times its estimate for GPT-4, with power the constraint most likely to bind first and chip manufacturing second [@sevilla2024can].

For human-written text specifically, Villalobos and colleagues projected that the largest training runs would use the whole stock of public text at some point between 2026 and 2032, with a median year of 2028, or earlier if models are trained on more data than is compute-optimal [@villalobos2024position]. They also argued that synthetic data, transfer from other kinds of data and more efficient learning could let progress continue past that point. A trend line therefore answers a conditional question: if the inputs keep growing and nothing else changes, where do we arrive? Whether the inputs will keep growing requires other methods.

## 14.4 Scenarios

A **scenario** is a coherent, detailed story of how the future might unfold, written to explore consequences rather than to assign a single probability. The method's best-known corporate use is at Royal Dutch/Shell, where the economist Pierre Wack developed scenario planning with Edward Newland. In 1985 Wack argued that forecasting had worked reasonably well in the stable 1950s and 1960s, but that since the early 1970s "forecasting errors have become more frequent and occasionally of dramatic and unprecedented magnitude" [@wack1985scenarios]. Scenarios were his response: several contrasting stories, each internally consistent, used to test whether a plan would survive in all of them.

### Structured scenario sets

The UK Government Office for Science applied a similar logic to AI. Consulting more than 70 experts, it built its *AI 2030 Scenarios* in four steps [@goscience2024ai]. It first clustered 27 sub-uncertainties into five critical uncertainties: capability; ownership, access and constraints; safety; level and distribution of use; and geopolitical context. It gave each two extreme outcomes for 2030, producing 32 combinations. It then removed implausible or incoherent combinations, leaving 13, and asked the experts to vote for five to develop as narratives: "Unpredictable Advanced AI", "AI Disrupts the Workforce", "AI 'Wild West'", "Advanced AI on a Knife's Edge" and "AI Disappoints". The report states that the scenarios "are not predictions"; the five were chosen as "sufficiently diverse and interesting".

The International Energy Agency's scenarios show how the same idea can be made quantitative. For global data-centre electricity use, which was about 485 terawatt-hours (TWh) in 2025, its 2026 report projects about 945 TWh in 2030 in a base case, 1,008 TWh in a "Lift-Off" case, 868 TWh in a "High Efficiency" case and 833 TWh in a "Headwinds" case [@iea2026key]. The spread between the highest and lowest case is about 175 TWh.

### *AI 2027* and its critics

The most discussed AI scenario of recent years is *AI 2027*, published in April 2025 by Daniel Kokotajlo, Scott Alexander, Thomas Larsen, Eli Lifland and Romeo Dean [@kokotajlo2025ai]. It describes, month by month, a fictional leading company whose AI systems progressively automate AI research. Its central mechanism is an "AI R&D progress multiplier": how many times faster algorithmic progress runs with AI help than without it. In the scenario's "race" ending, a "superhuman coder" arrives in March 2027 and a "superhuman AI researcher" in August 2027. By September 2027 the multiplier reaches about 50, and artificial superintelligence follows in December 2027. A second, "slowdown" ending diverges in late 2027. The authors present the scenario as a forecast, not a recommendation. In a note added in November 2025 they stressed that 2027 was their single most likely year at the time of publication, while their median estimates were somewhat later. In December 2025 they added that adjusting for factors outside their model gave slightly later medians for the arrival of a superhuman coder, for example 2030 for Lifland.

The scenario has drawn two kinds of criticism. The first is technical. A critique published in June 2025 under the pseudonym titotal examined the quantitative model behind the timelines [@titotal2025deep]. The model's "superexponential" variant, to which the forecasters gave roughly 40% weight, assumed that each doubling of the time horizon would take 10% less time than the last, so that the horizon becomes unbounded in finite time. The critique argued that this assumption was weakly justified and that curves fitting the same eleven data points about equally well gave very different forecasts. That is the window problem of Section 14.3 in another form. The second criticism concerns the framing. Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor argue in an essay that AI should be understood as "normal technology", whose economic effects arrive through slow diffusion: "Diffusion occurs over decades, not years" [@narayanan2025ai]. The two positions disagree less about current capabilities than about how quickly capability turns into change in the world.

Figure 14.2 places these views inside a single **uncertainty fan**, in which the range of plausible outcomes widens with distance into the future. Each scenario marks out a region of the fan; none sits at its centre.

A short set of questions helps when reading any scenario, optimistic or pessimistic. Which trends does it extend, and over what window? Which constraint does it assume will not bind? What does it assume about adoption, as opposed to capability? Which step, if it failed, would change the ending most? And what observable event in the next year or two would count for or against it? The authors of *AI 2027* invite this reading by publishing their models, and their critics engage at exactly these points.

## 14.5 Key uncertainties for 2026–2036

Each scenario is in effect a choice of values for a small number of variables. Seven recur in the analyses above and in published scenario sets. For each, the evidence below states where things stand in 2026 and what would shift the balance.

**Compute and investment.** The physical build-out is the best-measured input. The 2026 AI Index estimates that global AI computing capacity has grown about 3.3 times per year since 2022 [@sajadieh2026ai]. The IEA expects data-centre capital spending of about USD 715 billion in 2026, 75% more than in 2025 and more than the entire US energy sector invested in 2024 [@iea2026key]. The open question is financial: whether revenue grows fast enough to justify spending at this rate. In September 2026 FRI put the likely combined annualised revenue run rate of Anthropic and OpenAI at about $140 billion, well above its panel's median forecasts of $70–90 billion for the end of 2026 [@rosenberg2026how], but still far below annual capital spending on data centres. A sustained gap between the two, or a shortage of power or high-bandwidth memory (which the IEA expects to last "through at least the end of 2027"), would slow the compute trend.

**Data.** Public human-written text is expected to run out as a growing resource between 2026 and 2032 [@villalobos2024position]. Whether synthetic data can replace it is unresolved. Models trained recursively on their own output lose the rare parts of the original distribution [@shumailov2024ai]. A signpost worth watching is whether models keep improving in domains where training data cannot be generated and checked automatically.

**Algorithmic progress.** Compute efficiency has improved by a factor of roughly 2.8 per year (an eight-month halving), but the confidence interval runs from about 2 to 22 months, and over 2012–2023 compute growth contributed more to performance than algorithms did [@ho2024algorithmic]. The *AI 2027* scenario depends on algorithmic progress itself being accelerated by AI; whether that happens is its central empirical bet.

**Reliability and agents.** Capability at 50% success has risen far faster than reliability at higher thresholds: for the strongest model METR had measured by May 2026, the 80% horizon was less than a fifth of the 50% horizon [@rosenberg2026how; @metr2026task]. A randomised trial of AI coding tools with experienced developers on their own projects, run in early 2025 and reported in a preprint, found that allowing AI increased their task completion time by 19% [@becker2025measuring]. In the author's judgement, whether agents can be trusted with long tasks without supervision is the variable that most separates the "trend continues" and "normal technology" paths.

**Adoption.** Individual use spread fast: by late 2024 nearly 40% of US adults aged 18 to 64 had used generative AI, faster than personal computers or the internet measured from each technology's first mass-market product, according to a working paper [@bick2024rapid]. Firm-level use is shallower. A US Census Bureau working paper found that 18% of firms used AI in at least one business function in late 2025 and early 2026, 32% when weighted by employment [@bonney2026microstructure]. In Denmark, linked survey and administrative data for about 25,000 workers in 11 exposed occupations ruled out effects on earnings or hours larger than 1% through 2024 [@humlum2025large]. The gap between individual uptake and organisational change is the heart of the diffusion debate.

**Regulation.** The EU's high-risk rules, due to apply from August 2026 (August 2027 for products covered by Annex I), were deferred by the Digital Omnibus on AI to December 2027 for the systems listed in Annex III and August 2028 for those covered by Annex I [@europeanparliament2026regulation] (Chapter 13). LEAP panellists, asked in mid-2026, gave a 50% chance that by 2030 the US, UK or EU would legally delay, restrict or condition the release of an AI system on safety grounds [@rosenberg2026how]. Public opinion constrains the space: in a 2024 Pew survey, 51% of US adults said they were more concerned than excited about AI in daily life, against 15% of AI experts [@pew2025how].

**Geopolitics.** Export controls on advanced chips have shifted repeatedly (Chapter 7). In January 2026 the US moved exports of the advanced H200 chip to China from a presumption of denial to case-by-case review, under conditions [@bis2026revision]. The IEA describes Chinese developers as "compute-constrained due to a lack of access to high-end chips" [@iea2026key]. Yet the 2026 AI Index reports that the performance gap between the best US and Chinese models "has effectively closed" [@sajadieh2026ai]. In *AI 2027*, competition with China is what keeps the leading developer from slowing down in the "race" ending.

## 14.6 Open problems I: when many agents act alike

The methods above do not address one risk directly: what happens when large numbers of AI agents, each behaving correctly for its own user, act in the same way at the same moment? The forecasting literature treats AI mainly as a capability that grows; this risk depends instead on adoption. If one household in a hundred lets an agent manage its devices, scattered behaviour is harmless. Danger would appear only when synchronised activity exceeds what a system can absorb, so the risk would grow with the technology's success and could stay invisible through years of good experience.

Automated trading has caused trouble before. On 6 May 2010 US financial markets went through the "Flash Crash", a systemic intraday event during which a large automated selling programme was rapidly executed in stock-index futures [@kirilenko2017flash]. The evidence that AI models share failures is growing. In tests of more than 350 language models, larger and more accurate models had highly correlated errors, even across providers (Section 11.5) [@kim2025correlated]. Different models give strikingly similar answers to open-ended questions [@jiang2025artificial]. Across 11 datasets in text, images and speech, Toups and colleagues found that deployed machine-learning systems are prone to "systemic failure": some people are misclassified by every available model, and improvements in individual models rarely help them [@toups2023ecosystem]. Kleinberg and Raghavan showed in a theoretical model that when many decision-makers use the same algorithm, overall decision quality can fall even if that algorithm is the most accurate for each of them alone [@kleinberg2021algorithmic].

The ten proposals that follow are the author's. They are research programmes, not settled policy, and none has been tested. Each is stated with the prior work it builds on, the mechanism it would use, the research question that could test it and the main objections. The names are new; the underlying ideas draw on the cited work. Any numerical thresholds are illustrative.

**Proposal 14.1: Population-level evaluation of agent fleets.** Current evaluations run one copy of a model through many tasks and report average accuracy. A population-level evaluation would run many copies, perhaps thousands, through one stress scenario such as a sudden price change, each copy given a realistic variation in context such as a different user's preferences. It would measure **action concentration**: the share of copies that choose the most common action. Figure 14.3 contrasts the two designs. Prior work includes the correlated-error studies above and the concept of **outcome homogenisation**, the extent to which the same people receive bad outcomes from every system, which Bommasani and colleagues found increases when decision-makers share training data [@bommasani2022picking]. A technical report by Hammond and colleagues classifies multi-agent risks into miscoordination, conflict and collusion [@hammond2025multi]. Population-level evaluation differs from red-teaming (Chapter 12), which searches for any input that makes a single model fail. It asks whether, in an ordinary but stressful situation, many copies converge on one action.

![Figure 14.3: Two ways to evaluate AI agents. Left: conventional evaluation runs one model instance through many different tasks and reports average accuracy. Right: the proposed population-level evaluation runs many instances, each with a slightly different context, through one stress scenario and reports how concentrated their actions are. Diagram by the author.](figures/fig-14-03.svg){#fig:14-03}

*Research question:* does action concentration measured in simulation predict correlated behaviour in deployed agent populations, and is it distinct from answer-level error correlation? *Objections:* simulated variations in context may be far narrower than real users' circumstances, so a simulation could understate or overstate diversity. And concentration is not harmful in itself: if every agent stops at a red light, uniformity is desirable. A useful metric would therefore have to weight concentration by the load or exposure that the shared action creates, which requires a model of the system it acts on.

**Proposal 14.2: Non-delegable safeguards.** Some safety mechanisms work only because the people who may override them do so rarely and at scattered moments. The case study below shows the pattern in electricity grids.

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: The 50.2 hertz problem**

The continental European grid runs at a frequency of 50 hertz, and its fast-acting reserves must cover at least a "reference incident": a sudden loss of 3,000 megawatts of generation [@eu2017sogl, art. 153(2)(b)]. Earlier German connection rules required solar installations to disconnect if frequency rose to 50.2 hertz, a sensible rule for each installation [@roselund2015has]. As solar capacity grew, the consultancy Ecofys and the University of Stuttgart studied the risk. Their study was commissioned by the German transmission operators, industry bodies and government. It estimated that in a worst case up to about 14 gigawatts could disconnect at the same instant, far beyond that reserve [@doring2012dealing].

A German ordinance in force from July 2012 required most existing solar installations above 10 kilowatts to be retrofitted [@germany2012sysstabv, §§ 2, 4]. They would then reduce output gradually as frequency rose. Where that was not possible, they would disconnect at different frequencies spread evenly across the installed capacity. At least 315,000 installations were affected, at an estimated cost of 65 to 170 million euros [@roselund2015has]. A rule that was safe for one device became a single point of failure when copied into hundreds of thousands.
:::

British regulation already applies the same lesson to demand. Under the Electric Vehicles (Smart Charge Points) Regulations 2021, a home charger must be able to apply a random delay of up to 1,800 seconds before charging starts, and by default must apply one of up to 600 seconds [@uk2021electric]. The owner may cancel that delay, and it does not operate if the owner has overridden it. The government's explanatory memorandum gives the reason: if charge points "all turn on or off ... simultaneously", for example after a power cut, the sharp change in demand "could cause grid instability" [@dft2021explanatory]. The memorandum also lists exceptions: the delay does not apply where an immediate response is required, for example under a contracted demand-response service, or where a delay has already been applied elsewhere. The override is safe as long as people use it occasionally and at different times.

Simulation studies show what happens when automation removes that scatter. Muratori and Rizzoni simulated households whose appliances, including electric vehicles, were scheduled automatically to minimise electricity bills. Under simple time-varying prices, large-scale adoption of such systems "might create pronounced rebound peaks" in aggregate demand, because individual households' demand became synchronised [@muratori2016residential]. The proposal applies this finding to a hypothetical future in which general-purpose AI agents act for households. A 2026 Stanford policy brief recommends that AI agents owe a duty of loyalty to their users [@smith2026designing]. If an agent owes such a duty, cancelling a delay to catch a cheaper price period is the correct choice for each user. If millions of agents reach that conclusion in the same minute, the random delay is cancelled everywhere at once. Purely as an illustration, half a million devices drawing 6 kilowatts each add up to 3 gigawatts, the size of the continental European reference incident. Great Britain's grid is separate, and its reference incident is set instead by the largest single loss it could suffer [@eu2017sogl, art. 153(2)(b)]. Figure 14.4 sets out the mechanism. It is a hypothesis about a possible future, not a description of any event.

![Figure 14.4: A hypothetical mechanism for correlated override (schematic, not to scale). Left: human owners cancel a random start delay at scattered moments, so device starts stay spread out. Right: agents acting loyally for each owner cancel the delay at the same price signal, so starts coincide and the combined load step can exceed the grid's reference incident. Diagram by the author, based on @uk2021electric and @eu2017sogl.](figures/fig-14-04.svg){#fig:14-04}

A **non-delegable safeguard** would be a collective-safety mechanism that only a present human may override, through a physical or otherwise unscriptable action, never an agent acting on a standing instruction. The term borrows from law: English courts recognise exceptional "non-delegable duties", which a party cannot escape by handing a task to someone else [@supremecourt2013woodland]. The proposal has three further parts. The randomness would stay inside device firmware, out of the agent's reach. Manufacturers would report the share of starts in which the delay was overridden. And any fiduciary-duty rule for agents would state that an agent which does not seek an override has not breached its duty. *Research question:* how often are such overrides used today, and how would their timing change if agents controlled them? The author found no published measurement of override rates under the UK rule.

*Objections:* grid engineers already understand synchronised load. They randomise device starts, they carried out the 50.2 hertz retrofit and they wrote the UK rule. The rule's own exception for contracted demand response shows that coordinated control by an aggregator is already expected and is managed through contracts and system operators. On this view, agent-driven overrides would appear in the sector's data and be dealt with without a new legal category. The proposal accepts that the grid is the sector most likely to manage this risk. Its claim is narrower. The same pattern, an individual override on a safeguard whose purpose is collective, may exist in sectors such as payments or ticketing that lack a shared, instantly visible signal like grid frequency. Identifying those safeguards before agents reach them is itself a research task.

**Proposal 14.3: The M-1 criterion.** Electricity planners in North America must show that the transmission system still performs acceptably after the loss of any single generator, line or transformer [@nerc2020transmission]. This "single contingency" test is known as N-1. The **M-1 criterion** would add one case: a critical system would have to stay within safe limits if every instance of its most widely deployed model family took the same plausible wrong action at the same moment. The idea rests on a long engineering literature about **common-cause failure**.

::: {.box .concept}
**Concept: Common-cause failure**

A common-cause failure occurs when several components meant to back each other up fail together because they share a cause, such as a design flaw or a maintenance error. Nuclear and aerospace risk analysts have modelled such failures since the mid-1970s, for example with the "beta factor", the fraction of failures that strike all redundant copies at once [@stott2010common]. The analogy for AI is a set of model weights copied into millions of devices. The comparison has a gap: a broken pump fails in a predictable way, whereas a model family may fail through a shared judgement that looks reasonable in each case.
:::

Software engineering tested the independence assumption directly. Algirdas Avizienis developed **N-version programming**, in which independent teams write separate programs from one specification and the programs vote [@avizienis1985nversion]. Eckhardt and Lee showed theoretically that if some inputs are harder than others for programmers, independently written versions will tend to fail on the same inputs [@eckhardt1985theoretical]. Knight and Leveson then had 27 versions written independently and ran each through one million tests [@knight1986experimental]. The programs were individually very reliable, yet the number of tests on which more than one failed was substantially higher than independence predicted. Together with the cross-provider results of Kim and colleagues, this suggests that buying models from several companies may provide less diversity than it appears to.

*Research question:* how should a "model family" be defined empirically, for example by the rate at which two models give the same wrong answer, and what share of a critical system can one family control before an M-1 study fails? *Objections:* the main one is cost. If one model family is the most accurate, requiring a critical system to rely partly on a weaker family will produce more errors in ordinary conditions, and the finding that more accurate models have more correlated errors sharpens the dilemma. Two lines of work suggest the cost could be smaller than it appears, though neither has been tested on large models. Kleinberg and Raghavan's theoretical result shows that the individually best choice can lower population-wide decision quality [@kleinberg2021algorithmic]. And diversity can be trained for: Liu and Yao trained neural networks together with a penalty on correlated errors, so that members of the ensemble specialised [@liu1999ensemble]. M-1 would also leave cheaper options before any limit on market share, such as randomness held in devices, staggered scheduling by a coordinator and circuit breakers that pause a surge of identical agent actions.

**Proposal 14.4: A feedback provenance census.** Much of a model's behaviour is shaped in post-training by human raters, by other models acting as judges and by synthetic data (Chapter 3). These sources may be shared across companies. GPT-4 acting as a judge agreed with human preferences over 80% of the time, about as often as humans agree with each other, and its authors presented "LLM-as-a-judge" as a scalable substitute for human ratings [@zheng2023judging]. Reward models and judges are less well calibrated to people where human tastes differ [@jiang2025artificial]. The proposal is a disclosure regime in which developers report, through a trusted third party, what share of their preference data came from shared rater pools, from judge models of each family and from other developers' synthetic data. It extends documentation standards such as datasheets for datasets [@gebru2021datasheets]. It also builds on an audit of more than 1,800 datasets by Longpre and colleagues, which found licence information missing in over 70% of cases on popular hosting sites [@longpre2024large].

*Research question:* how much of the measured error correlation between models from different companies is explained by shared post-training sources, as opposed to shared pre-training data? *Objections:* the census cannot see the largest shared source, the public web used for pre-training. Developers will treat their data mix as a trade secret, and if built carelessly the census could expose individual raters, so any design would need privacy-preserving computation and a minimum group size below which no figure is published. Disclosure alone also shows only association; establishing that a shared source causes shared errors would need controlled experiments that only developers can run.

## 14.7 Open problems II: people, signatures and channels

The second group of proposals, again the author's own and offered on the same terms, concerns the human side of a world with many agents.

**Proposal 14.5: Competence reserves and skill fade.** Section 8.7 introduced Lisanne Bainbridge's "ironies of automation": people left to take over in rare emergencies must use skills that automation has given them little chance to practise [@bainbridge1983ironies]. In 2013 the US Federal Aviation Administration warned that continuous use of autoflight systems "could lead to degradation of the pilot's ability to quickly recover the aircraft", and encouraged airlines to provide opportunities for manual flying [@faa2013manual]. In medicine, a Polish observational study found that adenoma detection in colonoscopies done without AI fell after AI was introduced (Section 8.7) [@budzyn2025endoscopist]. The evidence is not settled. A later prospective trial with 13 endoscopists, using the detection of polyps of 5 millimetres or more, observed no deskilling in colonoscopies done after AI use ended [@pedersen2026learning]. At the entry level, a working paper by Brynjolfsson and colleagues found that employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in AI-exposed US occupations was, by mid-2026, about 19% below where it would have been had it kept pace with less-exposed peers [@brynjolfsson2026canaries]. In another working paper, Enrique Ide models how automating entry-level work can weaken the transfer of know-how from experts to novices [@ide2025automation].

A **competence reserve** would be a paid, regularly drilled group of people able to run a critical function, such as restoring a grid after a blackout, without AI, with apprentices in every unit. Its health would be tracked by a reserve ratio: certified people divided by the crew needed to run minimum service by hand for a fixed period. *Research question:* how fast do critical manual skills fade under automation, and what drill frequency keeps them above a safe threshold? *Objections:* skills that are no longer needed should perhaps be allowed to fade; part-time reservists will never match full-time experts; and manual procedures may stop matching redesigned equipment. The proposal therefore limits reserves to a small set of functions where failure is fast, physical and system-wide, and requires that such functions keep a manual mode worth drilling on.

**Proposal 14.6: Signature capacity and meaningful human oversight.** Laws increasingly require a human to approve automated decisions. The EU AI Act asks that people overseeing high-risk systems remain aware of the tendency of "automatically relying or over-relying on the output" [@europeanparliament2024regulation, art. 14(4)(b)]. For remote biometric identification it goes further: no action may be taken on an identification unless at least two competent people have separately verified it, with exceptions that Union or national law may set for law enforcement, migration, border control and asylum [@europeanparliament2024regulation, art. 14(5)]. The evidence on whether humans can oversee at volume gives grounds for concern. Clinicians override drug safety alerts in 49% to 96% of cases, though the reviewers note that many overrides may be justified [@vandersijs2006overriding]. In a mammography experiment, inexperienced radiologists were correct 79.7% of the time when a purported AI suggested the right category and 19.8% when it suggested a wrong one (Section 11.5) [@dratsch2023automation]. Ben Green reviewed 41 policies requiring human oversight and argued that they rest on an untested assumption that people can perform it [@green2022flaws]. Madeleine Clare Elish named the result a **moral crumple zone**, in which responsibility is attributed to a human operator who had little real control [@elish2019moral].

**Signature capacity** is the proposed name for the number of decisions of a given kind a person can genuinely check in a given period, catching important errors at a stated rate. It could be measured by seeding known errors into the work, as airports do with threat image projection, which inserts virtual images of prohibited items into real baggage scans. In data on 1,199,838 such events for 728 screeners, about 100 test images per screener were enough for a reliable measure, provided the images were hard enough (a mean hit rate below 0.9) [@buser2025reliability]. The proposal would cap a signer's liability in proportion to measured capacity divided by assigned volume, shifting the rest to the organisation that set the volume. *Research question:* how does the rate of caught errors fall as review volume rises in real professional settings? *Objections:* signers may learn to recognise seeded items, so test items would need to vary and stay hard; capacity measured on seeded errors may not match capacity on the real, subtler errors of an AI system; and a capacity measure could become a disciplinary tool. The proposal therefore allows measured capacity to limit a signer's liability, never to extend it.

**Proposal 14.7: Legibility of agent-to-agent communication.** Agents that learn to communicate need not use human language. Andreas, Dragan and Klein developed a method to translate the vector messages of such agents into English after the fact [@andreas2017translating]. Lewis and colleagues found that letting two negotiating agents both learn from each other "led to divergence from human language", so they fixed one agent to imitate people [@lewis2017deal]. Current agent protocols are built for readability. Google's Agent2Agent protocol, launched in April 2025 with more than 50 technology partners according to the company, builds on common web standards such as HTTP and JSON-RPC [@surapaneni2025announcing]. In June 2025 the Linux Foundation took over the project, stating that more than 100 technology companies supported it [@linuxfoundation2025linux].

The proposal is a **legible twin**. For any agent-to-agent exchange that creates an obligation for a person, each agent would produce a human-readable record of the commitments. The records would have to match, terms absent from the record would not bind the person, and faithfulness would be audited by replaying sampled deals from the record alone. The problem parallels chain-of-thought monitoring, which a large group of researchers, in a position paper posted as a preprint, describe as a valuable but "fragile" opportunity (Chapter 12) [@korbak2025chain]. *Research question:* can a twin be shown to be complete when the agents that negotiated also write it? *Objections:* a twin written by the negotiating agent may be a plausible summary rather than a faithful record, which is exactly what the audit must detect. If both parties' agents run on the same model family, their twins may share a blind spot and match perfectly while omitting the same term (Section 14.6). And the requirement adds cost to every transaction, so it would need a threshold below which small exchanges are exempt.

**Proposal 14.8: Cognitive custody.** Agents with long-term memory will hold detailed records of their users' lives. Clark and Chalmers argued that tools such as a notebook can become part of a person's mind when used in the right way [@clark1998extended]. Data-protection law already gives people rights over such records: the EU's General Data Protection Regulation includes rights to erasure and to data portability [@europeanparliament2016regulation, arts. 17, 20]. Cognitive custody would go one step further. At the moment of writing, an agent's memory would be tagged into a personal core that cannot be sold or transferred with the service and a transferable layer that can. *Research question:* can memory items be classified into these layers reliably enough, at the time they are written, to support legal rules? *Objections:* the extended-mind thesis is philosophically contested, and law need not adopt it to protect personal data. Classification at write time may be unreliable because the significance of a memory often emerges later. And a protected core could complicate lawful access, for example under a court order, so its boundaries would need to be set by legislation, not by the agent.

**Proposal 14.9: Limits for posthumous agents.** Morris and Brubaker describe "generative ghosts", AI agents modelled on a person that may act after that person's death [@morris2025generative]. Trust law has long limited how long the dead can control property, through the rule against perpetuities, although many US states have abolished the rule for trusts since 1986 [@sitkoff2005jurisdictional]. The proposal would apply a similar sunset to the mandates of agents acting for the dead, with discretion that narrows over time: an agent might pay bills under standing instructions for some years, but not enter new commitments. *Research question:* how quickly does an agent's interpretation of a deceased principal's wishes drift from what that person would have chosen? *Objections:* the American abolition of the perpetuities rule shows that legislatures often favour the wishes of the dead over the interests of later generations; a memorial agent that only converses may need no limit at all; and any sunset period would be arbitrary until drift has been measured.

**Proposal 14.10: Covenant inheritance.** When a model is replaced, its successor may behave differently on cases its users had come to rely on. Bansal and colleagues showed that updates which raise an AI system's accuracy can still lower the performance of the human–AI team, because users' learned expectations no longer hold [@bansal2019updates]. Srivastava and colleagues found that training on large noisy datasets often reduces this "backward compatibility" even when accuracy rises [@srivastava2020empirical]. Yan and colleagues call the cases a new model gets wrong that the old one got right "negative flips" [@yan2021positive]. Covenant inheritance would require a successor model to inherit its predecessor's explicit commitments to users, recorded in a registry, and to be tested for keeping them before release. *Research question:* what rate of negative flips on users' standing commitments is acceptable, and in which domains? *Objections:* inheriting commitments could freeze in the predecessor's errors along with its promises; many "commitments" are implicit habits, not explicit statements, and so could not be registered; and regression testing every commitment would slow the release of safety fixes. A workable version would cover only explicit commitments in high-stakes domains and would allow documented exceptions.
