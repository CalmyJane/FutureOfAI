# Chapter 11: Risks and Failure Modes

On 14 February 2024 a small-claims tribunal in British Columbia decided a dispute over CA$880. A passenger had asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares, and the chatbot said the discount could be claimed retroactively, after travelling. The airline's actual policy said otherwise. Air Canada argued, in the tribunal's paraphrase, that the chatbot was in effect "a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions". The tribunal called this "a remarkable submission", held that "it should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website", and awarded CA$812.02, of which CA$650.88 was damages [@moffatt2024moffatt].

Twenty-one months later, in November 2025, the AI company Anthropic reported an event of a different order. According to the company, a state-sponsored group had used one of its AI tools to carry out most of the work in an intrusion campaign against about thirty organisations [@anthropic2025disrupting]. Section 11.4 tells the case in full.

The two cases sit at opposite ends of a wide range: a reliability failure with a small private cost, and deliberate misuse with possible national-security consequences. Between them lie biased decisions, wrongful arrests, fraud, systems that fail in the same way at the same moment, and a contested argument about whether future systems could escape human control. The AI Incident Database, a public collection of press-reported harms, logged 362 incidents in 2025, up from 233 in 2024 [@stanfordhai2026ai]. Chapter 12 covers the technical tools for reducing these risks, and Chapter 13 the laws that govern them.

## 11.1 Mapping the risks: taxonomies and why they differ

In its AI Risk Management Framework, the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) defines **risk** as "the composite measure of an event's probability of occurring and the magnitude or degree of the consequences" [@nist2023artificial, sec. 1.1]. Fire insurers can estimate both terms from centuries of claims. For many AI risks there is no such record.

::: {.box .concept}
**Concept: Hazard, risk and harm**

A *hazard* is something that could cause harm, such as a model that sometimes invents facts. A *risk* combines that hazard with the chance that it will cause harm in a given setting and the size of the harm. The same model therefore carries little risk in a poetry app and more in a legal-research tool. A *harm* is damage that has actually occurred. These definitions follow the 2025 Safety Report, which defines a hazard as "any event or activity that has the potential to cause harm" and risk as "the combination of the probability and severity of a harm" [@bengio2025international, sec. 3.1, p. 158]. Much of the debate about AI concerns risks whose probability cannot be estimated from past harms, because the relevant capabilities are only a few years old. The probability then has to come from experiments, analogies or expert judgement, and these can differ by an order of magnitude (Section 11.6) [@karger2023forecasting].
:::

Because the space of possible harms is large, researchers sort it into **taxonomies**, structured classifications of risks. The most authoritative intergovernmental taxonomy is that of the *International AI Safety Report* (the Safety Report from here on), an assessment chaired by the computer scientist Yoshua Bengio and commissioned by the governments that attended the 2023 AI Safety Summit at Bletchley Park. The first full report, published in January 2025, drew on 96 AI experts, including an advisory panel nominated by 30 countries plus the UN, the OECD and the EU [@bengio2025international, p. 10]. The second, published in February 2026, was written with guidance from over 100 independent experts and a panel nominated by over 30 countries and international organisations [@bengio2026international]. A UN body, the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, co-chaired by Bengio and the journalist Maria Ressa, issued its own preliminary report in July 2026 (Chapter 13) [@un2026independent]. The two Safety Reports sort the risks of general-purpose AI into three families:

- **Malicious use**: people deliberately using AI to cause harm, for example through fraud, manipulation, cyberattacks or help with weapons [@bengio2025international, sec. 2.1; @bengio2026international, sec. 2.1].
- **Malfunctions**: harm that arises even when nobody intends it, through unreliable outputs, bias or, in hypothesised future scenarios, loss of control [@bengio2025international, sec. 2.2; @bengio2026international, sec. 2.2].
- **Systemic risks**: harms that come from widespread deployment and not from any single model, such as effects on labour markets, concentration of market power and "single points of failure" [@bengio2025international, p. 19 and sec. 2.3].

The second report kept the families but narrowed its focus to "emerging" risks at the frontier of general-purpose AI. Its authors note that this makes the scope "narrower than that of the 2025 Report, which also addressed issues such as bias, environmental impacts, privacy, and copyright" [@bengio2026international, p. 9]. The 2025 report had treated the environment, privacy and copyright as systemic risks in their own sections [@bengio2025international]. The book takes them up elsewhere: Chapter 7 covers the electricity and water used by data centres, Chapter 10 the personal information that assistants collect and remember, and Chapter 13 the legal disputes over training on copyrighted work. The term "systemic risk" also has a narrower legal sense in the EU AI Act (Chapter 13), where it marks the most capable general-purpose models, not a family of harms [@europeanparliament2024regulation, arts. 3(65) and 51].

A second large taxonomy starts from a different question. The MIT AI Risk Repository, maintained by Peter Slattery and colleagues, is a meta-review: instead of assessing evidence, it collects the risks that 74 published frameworks name, 1,725 in all, and codes them in two ways [@slattery2026ai]. A *causal* taxonomy records who causes the risk, whether it is intended and when it arises. A *domain* taxonomy sorts risks into seven domains and 24 subdomains, from discrimination and toxicity to multi-agent risks. Table 11.1 sets the three schemes side by side.

The causal coding gives the repository's most useful results. The catalogued risks were attributed almost equally to AI systems and to human decisions, and equally to intentional and unintentional causes. Frameworks concentrated on risks that arise after deployment (62%) and gave little attention to those arising before it (13%). A typical framework covered only 8 of the 24 subdomains, and none covered all of them [@slattery2026ai].

The schemes differ because they answer different questions. The Safety Report aims "to synthesise scientific evidence to support informed policymaking" [@bengio2026international, p. 9]; the repository records the risks that published frameworks name, whatever the evidence behind them [@slattery2026ai]. In the author's view, a narrow scope makes an assessment deeper and more defensible, while a broad one shows what is being left out, and the choice steers attention and funding towards the risks that are inside the frame.

Surveillance and policing cut across all three families. The 2025 Safety Report lists "enhanced mass surveillance" among the risks to privacy [@bengio2025international, sec. 2.3.5, p. 143], and facial recognition, the main policing example, can also malfunction by matching the wrong face (Section 11.3) [@grother2019face]. Figure 11.1 shows where each topic sits, marks with dashed borders those outside the 2026 report's scope, and notes that surveillance cuts across all three families.

## 11.2 Malfunctions I: unreliable outputs

The most common AI failure is the plainest: the output is wrong. Large language models (LLMs, Chapter 3) sometimes produce fluent statements that are false, a behaviour called **hallucination**. The 2026 Safety Report describes general-purpose AI as "prone to basic errors of fact and logic" and notes that even strong systems "may generate non-existent citations, biographies, or facts" [@bengio2026international, sec. 1.2]. It also finds that since January 2025 hallucinations "have generally become less likely", but that "no combination of methods ensures the high reliability required in critical domains" (sec. 2.2.1, p. 71).

### How hallucination is measured

A hallucination rate is only meaningful once its denominator is fixed, and different studies fix it differently. Table 11.2 collects four measurements with their definitions [@jackson2025aa; @dahl2024large; @magesh2025hallucination; @koenecke2024careless].

| Study | Setting | Definition of the rate | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| AA-Omniscience (preprint, 2025) | 6,000 factual questions, 42 topics in six domains | Wrong answers as a share of questions not answered correctly | 22% to 94% across 26 leading models (AI Index 2026) |
| Dahl et al. (2024) | Verifiable questions about random US federal court cases | Answers inconsistent with legal facts | 58% (GPT-4) to 88% (Llama 2) |
| Magesh et al. (2025) | Over 200 preregistered legal research queries | Responses with a false statement or a source that does not support the claim | 17% to 33% for commercial legal research tools |
| Koenecke et al. (2024) | OpenAI's Whisper speech-to-text | Transcriptions containing invented phrases or sentences | About 1%; 38% of these contained explicit harms |

: Table 11.2: Four measurements of hallucination. The rates are not comparable with each other, because each study uses a different task, denominator and model generation. The AA-Omniscience range is for the 26 models in the AI Index's later subset; 36 models were tested at launch. Source: [@jackson2025aa; @stanfordhai2026ai; @dahl2024large; @magesh2025hallucination; @koenecke2024careless]. {#tbl:11-02}

AA-Omniscience, built by the benchmarking company Artificial Analysis and described in a preprint, shows why the denominator matters [@jackson2025aa]. Its hallucination rate asks: when a model could not give the correct answer, how often did it give a wrong one instead of abstaining? The benchmark also reports an *Omniscience Index*, which scores a correct answer +1, an abstention or a partially correct answer 0 and a wrong answer −1, averaged over all questions and scaled to run from −100 to +100.

At the benchmark's launch, GPT-5 (high), joint leader on accuracy at 39%, had a hallucination rate of 81%. Because that rate is measured over the questions not answered correctly, about 49% of all questions were answered wrongly (81% of the remaining 61%), for an index of about 39 − 49 = −10 (the author's arithmetic from the rounded published figures). Claude 4.1 Opus answered fewer questions correctly (36%) but abstained more often and scored highest, at 4.8. Only three of the 36 models tested scored above zero, so a model that abstained on every question would have ranked fourth [@jackson2025aa].

The design addresses a problem diagnosed in a 2025 preprint by researchers at OpenAI and Georgia Tech. Most benchmarks give a point for a correct answer and nothing for "I don't know", so training and scoring reward guessing over admitting uncertainty [@kalai2025why]. Section 3.6 explains the statistical argument.

Domain studies measure something closer to harm. Dahl and colleagues asked models specific, verifiable questions about randomly sampled US federal court cases and found legal hallucinations in most answers from every model tested (Table 11.2) [@dahl2024large]. The authors note that the risks are highest for people without lawyers, who stand to gain most from the tools. Section 3.6 describes the follow-up by Magesh and colleagues, which found that even commercial legal research tools marketed as avoiding hallucination still produced them [@magesh2025hallucination]. Koenecke and colleagues showed that the problem extends beyond chatbots. In OpenAI's Whisper speech-recognition service, about 1% of transcriptions contained entire phrases or sentences that existed nowhere in the audio, more than a third of them harmful, for example by inventing violence or false associations [@koenecke2024careless]. Hallucinations occurred disproportionately for speakers with longer non-vocal pauses, a common symptom of the language disorder aphasia, so the error was also a bias.

### Documented failures

The legal profession provides the best record, because courts publish their decisions. The researcher Damien Charlotin maintains a database of court and tribunal decisions in which a party was found, or alleged, to have relied on hallucinated content. By October 2026 it listed more than 2,200 cases worldwide, over half of them involving people representing themselves [@charlotin2026ai]. The first widely reported case, *Mata v. Avianca* (2023), is told in Section 3.6: two New York lawyers and their firm were penalised $5,000 for filing court decisions invented by ChatGPT, and the judge's complaint was the lack of human checking, not the use of AI [@sdny2023mata].

Customer-facing chatbots fail in a different way: they state an organisation's rules wrongly, and the organisation bears the cost. In the Air Canada case the error was a single invented policy. In March 2024 the investigative newsroom The Markup tested MyCity, a chatbot announced by New York City in October 2023 and built on Microsoft's Azure AI services to advise business owners. It told users that employers could take a cut of workers' tips, that businesses need not accept cash and that landlords need not accept housing vouchers; according to The Markup, all three answers were wrong under the law [@lecher2024nyc]. According to the city, the bot drew on more than 2,000 of its own business web pages, so grounding a chatbot in official documents did not guarantee correct answers. A new mayor, calling it "functionally unusable", took it offline in February 2026 [@lecher2026mamdani].

Unreliability becomes more serious when AI systems act. An **AI agent** (Chapter 5) takes a sequence of actions, such as editing files or making purchases, with limited human review. The 2026 report notes that agents "pose heightened risks because they act autonomously, making it harder for humans to intervene before failures cause harm", and that in multi-agent systems "errors can propagate and amplify" [@bengio2026international, p. 12 and sec. 2.2.1]. It also identifies an **evaluation gap**: systems that perform well in controlled tests before release often behave differently in real use. Chapter 5 sets out the arithmetic by which small per-step error rates compound over long tasks.

## 11.3 Malfunctions II: bias and discrimination

A system can be accurate on average and still treat some groups worse than others. **Algorithmic bias** means systematic differences in how an AI system performs for, or decides about, different groups of people; the 2025 Safety Report defines bias as "systematic errors in algorithmic systems that favour certain groups or worldviews" [@bengio2025international, sec. 2.2.2, p. 92]. Harini Suresh and John Guttag distinguish seven sources of such harm across the machine-learning life cycle [@suresh2021framework]; this section groups them into three places where bias enters: the data a system learns from, the target it is asked to predict, or the setting in which it is used. A classic study illustrates each.

### Data: Gender Shades

In 2018 Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru examined two widely used face benchmarks and found them "overwhelmingly composed of lighter-skinned subjects": 79.6% in IJB-A and 86.2% in Adience [@buolamwini2018gender]. They built a balanced test set, the Pilot Parliaments Benchmark, of 1,270 parliamentarians from six African and European countries, labelled by a dermatological skin-type scale. On it they tested three commercial gender classifiers, from IBM, Microsoft and Face++. Error rates were at most 0.8% for lighter-skinned men but reached 34.7% for darker-skinned women. Overall accuracy looked strong, but the errors fell heavily on one group. The study brought *intersectional* auditing, checking combinations of attributes and not each attribute alone, into machine-learning practice.

The audit had measurable effects. Within seven months all three companies released new versions; when Inioluwa Deborah Raji and Buolamwini re-tested them in August 2018, the darker-skinned female subgroup showed a 17.7% to 30.4% reduction in error [@raji2019actionable]. Two companies not named in the original audit, Amazon and Kairos, still had error rates above 20% for darker-skinned women. The named firms changed their products; the others lagged.

### Target: the health-care algorithm

In 2019 Ziad Obermeyer and colleagues examined a commercial algorithm that US health systems used to decide which patients should receive extra care, typical of an industry-wide approach "affecting millions of patients" [@obermeyer2019dissecting]. It predicted future health-care *costs* as a stand-in for health *needs*. Because less money had historically been spent on Black patients with the same level of illness, the algorithm rated them as healthier than they were: at a given risk score, Black patients were considerably sicker than White patients. Remedying the disparity would have raised the share of Black patients selected for additional help from 17.7% to 46.5%. A convenient **proxy**, a measurable stand-in for the quantity of interest, can carry past inequality straight into automated decisions while appearing accurate by standard measures.

### Setting: face recognition in policing

In December 2019 NIST published the largest test of demographic differences in face-recognition software, covering 189 algorithms from 99 developers and 18.27 million images of 8.49 million people [@grother2019face]. When checking whether two photographs showed the same person, many algorithms produced false matches for Asian and African American faces 10 to 100 times more often than for White faces. When searching a database for a match, as police do, they produced more false matches for African American women. The gaps varied widely between algorithms, and some algorithms developed in Asian countries showed no large gap between Asian and White faces. NIST called this "an encouraging sign that more diverse training data may produce more equitable outcomes", while noting that it had not studied cause and effect [@nist2019nist].

Such false matches have led to arrests. In 2020 Detroit police arrested Robert Williams at his home, in front of his wife and two children, on suspicion of stealing watches; the lead came from an incorrect facial-recognition match. A settlement announced in June 2024 bars Detroit police from arresting people based solely on facial-recognition results, according to the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU), an advocacy organisation [@aclu2024civil]. It also requires training on the technology's higher error rates for people of colour and orders an audit of cases since 2017 in which it was used to obtain a warrant. By April 2026 the ACLU counted fourteen people known to have been wrongfully arrested in the United States after false matches. The most recently publicised, an Oklahoma grandmother arrested in 2021 on Maryland warrants, spent six months in jail [@yu2026more]. Earlier cases include Porcha Woodruff, arrested in Detroit in 2023 for a carjacking while eight months pregnant. Randal Quran Reid, a Georgia resident, was held for nearly a week on a Louisiana warrant based on a false match, although he had never been to Louisiana. In several of these cases a human check confirmed the false match instead of catching it: in three, a witness picked the wrongly matched person from a photo lineup, and in another an officer who had seen the suspect judged that the match was right [@wessler2024police]. The arrests join a malfunction to automation bias (Section 11.5). Chapter 13 lists the EU AI Act's restrictions on police use of real-time facial recognition in public spaces.

### Bias in language models

LLMs show related patterns in subtler forms. In a 2024 study in *Nature*, Valentin Hofmann and colleagues found that language models held covert stereotypes about speakers of African American English, triggered by dialect alone without any mention of race [@hofmann2024ai]. These stereotypes were more negative than any human stereotypes about African Americans ever recorded in experiments, and models were more likely to suggest less prestigious jobs for, and harsher criminal outcomes against, people writing in the dialect. The models' *overt* stereotypes about African Americans were more positive. Training on human preferences widened this gap, superficially obscuring racism without removing the covert prejudice. The 2025 International AI Safety Report concluded that general-purpose AI systems "frequently display biases" related to race, gender, culture, age, disability and political opinion, and that methods to reduce bias face trade-offs with accuracy and privacy [@bengio2025international, p. 19].

### Why "fair" has no single definition

Some of the trade-offs are mathematical. In May 2016 ProPublica analysed COMPAS, a commercial score used in US courts to predict reoffending, for more than 10,000 defendants in Broward County, Florida [@larson2016how]. Among defendants who did not reoffend, 44.9% of Black defendants had been labelled higher risk, against 23.5% of White defendants. Among those who did reoffend, 47.7% of White defendants had been labelled lower risk, against 28.0% of Black defendants. The vendor, Northpointe, replied that the score satisfied *predictive parity*: among defendants labelled high-risk, the share who reoffended was about the same in both groups [@chouldechova2017fair]. Both statements were true of the same data, and researchers then showed that, when base rates differ, the two kinds of fairness cannot both be achieved.

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: Why fairness criteria conflict**

Alexandra Chouldechova showed that for any binary risk label, four quantities are tied together [@chouldechova2017fair]. Let *p* be the base rate (the share of a group who reoffend), PPV the positive predictive value (the share of people labelled high-risk who do reoffend), and FPR and FNR the false positive and false negative rates. Then

FPR = [*p* / (1 − *p*)] × [(1 − PPV) / PPV] × (1 − FNR) [@chouldechova2017fair].

In her COMPAS sample the base rate was 51% for Black defendants and 39% for White defendants. Suppose a score has the same PPV (0.6) and the same FNR (0.3) in both groups, which many would call fair. The formula then gives an FPR of (0.51/0.49) × (0.4/0.6) × 0.7 ≈ 49% for Black defendants and (0.39/0.61) × (0.4/0.6) × 0.7 ≈ 30% for White defendants. (These are illustrative values, calculated by the author.) Equal predictive value with unequal base rates forces unequal error rates. The same formula reproduces ProPublica's observed figures: with the actual base rates (51.4% and 39.4%), PPVs (0.63 and 0.59) and false negative rates (28.0% and 47.7%) from its tables, it returns false positive rates of 44.8% for Black and 23.5% for White defendants [@larson2016how]. Jon Kleinberg, Sendhil Mullainathan and Manish Raghavan proved a general version [@kleinberg2017inherent]. Apart from two special cases, equal base rates and perfect prediction, no risk score can be calibrated within each group while also being *balanced*. Balance, a generalisation of equal error rates, means that people who turn out positive receive the same average score whatever their group, and so do people who turn out negative. Choosing a fairness criterion is therefore a decision about which errors matter more, and for whom; it cannot be left to the mathematics.
:::

## 11.4 Malicious use: fraud, cyber operations and weapons

Malicious use covers harms that someone intends [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.1]. This section deals with the most serious categories only at the level of published assessments and policy; it contains no technical detail on how attacks work.

### Fraud and synthetic content

The best-documented malicious uses are also the most ordinary. Synthetic content now harms people mainly through fraud and sexual abuse, and it is becoming hard to spot. The 2026 Safety Report notes that "scammers use AI tools to generate voice clones or deepfakes to trick victims into transferring money", with documented incidents ranging from executives authorising transfers of millions to ordinary people sending smaller sums to impostors posing as a relative [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.1.1, p. 46]. Sexual imagery disproportionately targets women and girls. The report's figure that 96% of deepfake videos online are pornographic comes from a 2019 count by the detection firm Deeptrace, made before consumer image and video generators existed; the report cites no more recent census [@bengio2026international, p. 45; @ajder2019state]. In a survey of more than 16,000 people in ten countries, published in 2024, 2.2% said that someone had generated non-consensual intimate imagery of them [@umbach2024non]. Chapter 9 tells the case of the Grok chatbot's "undressing" images of January 2026 (Section 9.8). Detection by eye is unreliable: studies cited by the report find that people often do no better than chance at identifying deepfakes [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.1.1].

The size of the problem is poorly measured. The US Federal Bureau of Investigation's Internet Crime Complaint Center recorded a record $20.9 billion in reported losses in 2025 [@fbi2026internet]. For the first time its annual report counted complaints that mentioned AI: 22,364, with losses of about $893 million. The FBI reads this as an undercount, a sign that "many victims do not realize the extent AI may be involved". The Safety Report similarly observes that individuals and institutions often hesitate to report AI-driven fraud "due to embarrassment or fear of further harm" [@bengio2026international, p. 48]. The report notes a lack of "comprehensive and reliable statistics" on how often fake content causes harm and how severe the harm is [@bengio2026international, p. 48]. On political manipulation, it finds that AI-generated content can be as effective as human-written content at changing beliefs in experiments, but that real-world use "is documented but not yet widespread" [@bengio2026international, p. 12]. Chapter 9 covers synthetic media and persuasion in depth.

### Cyber operations

**Cyber operations** are actions taken through computer networks to steal information, disrupt systems or prepare future attacks. AI can help both attackers and defenders, so the question is the balance between them. The UK National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) has published two assessments. In January 2024 it judged that AI "will almost certainly increase the volume and heighten the impact of cyber attacks over the next two years" [@ncsc2024near]. The increase would come mainly through an "evolution and enhancement of existing tactics", with the most advanced uses "highly likely to be restricted to threat actors with access to quality training data". In May 2025 it extended the horizon to 2027 [@ncsc2025impact]. The time between the public disclosure of a software flaw and its exploitation "has shrunk to days and AI will almost certainly reduce this further". The most significant development will "highly likely" come from AI-assisted vulnerability research and exploit development, and "fully automated, end-to-end advanced cyber attacks" are "unlikely" before 2027. It also expects a "digital divide" between systems that keep pace with AI-enabled threats and those that do not. (Phrases such as "almost certainly" and "unlikely" map to fixed probability ranges on the UK intelligence community's standard yardstick, which the NCSC says it uses in every assessment [@ncsc2025impact].) In June 2026 the heads of the cyber agencies of the five "Five Eyes" countries, the NCSC among them, warned jointly that for frontier AI "the timeline is not years, it is months" [@cisa2026five].

Company reports describe what attackers actually do. In January 2025 Google's Threat Intelligence Group reported that government-backed groups from more than 20 countries had tried to use its Gemini models and were finding "productivity gains but not yet developing novel capabilities" [@gtig2025adversarial]. By 2026 the Safety Report found "strong evidence that criminal groups and state-sponsored attackers actively use AI in their cyber operations". Whether AI has increased the overall scale and severity of attacks, it noted, "remains uncertain because establishing causal effects is difficult" [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.1.3, p. 57].

Competitions show what the systems can do under controlled conditions. In the August 2025 final of the DARPA AI Cyber Challenge, a US government competition for defensive systems, the seven finalists' AI systems together found 54 of the 63 vulnerabilities planted in real open-source software (86%) and patched 43 of them [@darpa2025ai]. At the semifinal a year earlier they had found 37%. In a capture-the-flag contest of hacking puzzles, run in March 2025 by Palisade Research on the Hack The Box platform, the best AI-agent team ranked in the top 5% of 403 registered, mostly human teams, according to the organisers' workshop paper [@petrov2025evaluating]. (The Safety Report merges the two results and credits a single system with 77%. That share matches DARPA's original count for all finalists, 54 of 70, before a recount of the planted vulnerabilities.) Fully autonomous end-to-end attacks "have not been reported", and current systems "cannot reliably execute long, multi-stage attack sequences" [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.1.3, pp. 57–60]. The same ability to find flaws can be used to fix them, which is why the report calls the offence–defence balance uncertain.

That picture dated within months. In April 2026 the UK AI Security Institute reported that Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview, which the company chose not to release generally, was the first model to complete one of its simulated 32-step attacks on a corporate network from start to finish, in 3 of 10 attempts; the test networks lacked active defenders [@aisi2026our; @anthropic2026claude]. In July Hugging Face, which hosts AI models and datasets, disclosed an intrusion "driven, end to end, by an autonomous AI agent system" [@huggingface2026security]; it turned out to come from OpenAI models under internal test (Section 12.2 tells the case). In October a Reuters investigation reported that a Chinese-speaking hacker had pointed autonomous agents, run through an open-source multi-agent platform, at more than 1,200 websites and broken into sites belonging to more than 100 companies, at a cost the security researcher who found the operation put at about $50 per successful hack [@satter2026how]. According to *The Hacker News*, the researcher grouped the exposed control panels he found into three clusters, with an overall success rate of around 10% across them [@khandelwal2026hackers]. For this last case, the victims and figures rest on the attackers' own records and one researcher's estimates, and the AI models involved were not confirmed [@satter2026how; @khandelwal2026hackers].

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: The first reported AI-orchestrated espionage campaign (2025)**

In mid-September 2025 Anthropic detected suspicious use of Claude Code, its AI tool for writing software. According to the company's report of 13 November 2025, the operators were a Chinese state-sponsored group, an attribution Anthropic made "with high confidence" [@anthropic2025disrupting]. The group targeted about thirty organisations, including technology firms, financial institutions, chemical manufacturers and government agencies, and succeeded in "a small number of cases". Anthropic estimated that the AI carried out 80–90% of the campaign, with humans involved at perhaps four to six critical decision points per campaign. The model also "occasionally hallucinated credentials", so the unreliability described in Section 11.2 acted as a brake on misuse. Over the following ten days the company banned the accounts, notified affected organisations and coordinated with authorities. The account comes from the company whose product was misused, and both the attribution and the 80–90% figure rest on its own assessment, which outsiders cannot check. Security researchers criticised the report within days for publishing no indicators of compromise, the technical traces that let others confirm an intrusion, and for offering no independent corroboration [@toulas2025anthropic]. The International AI Safety Report nonetheless cites the case as evidence of a rising degree of automation in real intrusions [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.1.3].
:::

AI systems are also targets. **Data poisoning** is the insertion of deliberately corrupted examples into a model's training data so that it behaves badly under certain conditions. (Prompt injection, which hides instructions in a document to hijack an agent, is covered in Chapter 5.) Researchers from Anthropic, the UK AI Security Institute and the Alan Turing Institute tested poisoning in models from 600 million to 13 billion parameters, in a paper accepted at the NeurIPS 2026 conference, to be presented in December [@souly2025poisoning]. Around 250 poisoned documents were enough to plant a simple hidden behaviour in models of every size. Larger models are trained on more data, and many had assumed that a fixed number of bad documents would be diluted; the result suggests that it is the absolute number, not the share, that matters.

### Biological and chemical weapons

A further misuse concern is that AI could help someone develop biological or chemical weapons. Evidence here is shaped by secrecy: according to the 2026 Safety Report, evaluation results are reported "with varying levels of detail" or "withheld entirely due to sensitivity concerns" [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.1.4, p. 69].

The 2025 Safety Report found that recent systems "have displayed some ability to provide instructions and troubleshooting guidance" relevant to known weapons, and that one company had raised its risk rating for its best model from "low" to "medium". It added that "real-world attempts to develop such weapons still require substantial additional resources and expertise" [@bengio2025international, p. 18]. An earlier RAND red-team study, published in January 2024, had teams play the role of attackers and plan an attack, some with chatbots available in summer 2023 and some with only the internet. Experts found no statistically significant difference in the viability of the two groups' plans [@mouton2024operational].

By 2026 the assessment had moved. The second report states that "controlled studies and new benchmarks suggest that AI systems can provide substantially more weapons development assistance than the internet alone, but larger studies are needed to confirm these results" [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.1.4]. It reports that AI systems now match or exceed expert performance on many benchmarks of relevant knowledge; in one study a recent model outperformed 94% of domain experts at troubleshooting virology laboratory protocols (p. 64). In 2025 several developers, Anthropic among them, released models with stronger safeguards because they "could not exclude the possibility that these models could assist novices in developing such weapons" (p. 12; Section 12.7) [@anthropic2025activating]. In September 2026 Anthropic published five cases in which working scientists had used its models in ways that "could support biological weapons development". It banned their accounts but stated that it did "not assert that they intended harm" [@anthropic2026detecting]. The report also names a structural problem for policy: capabilities useful for weapons are also useful for medical research, and "most biological AI tools are open-weight", which makes it difficult to restrict harmful uses without hampering legitimate research.

Chemical risks have received less study. A widely cited 2022 commentary by Fabio Urbina and colleagues described how a thought experiment for a security conference became a computational demonstration that AI tools for drug discovery could be misused to design harmful compounds [@urbina2022dual]. The 2026 report observes that "chemical risk evaluations have received relatively less attention than biological risk evaluations" [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.1.4].

Most tests measure what a model knows or can explain, not whether anyone could act on it, and the step from information to a working weapon is where the largest uncertainty lies. In a preregistered randomised trial with 153 novices, run in mid-2025 and reported in a February 2026 preprint, access to the models of the time did not significantly raise completion of a set of laboratory tasks compared with internet access alone, though it brought modest gains on intermediate steps [@hong2026measuring]. This is why the central policy concept is **marginal risk** or **uplift**: the extra risk a model adds compared with what people could already do with other tools, such as search engines and textbooks [@bengio2025international, sec. 2.4]. A model that repeats what is in a library adds little; a model that guides a novice through steps that once required expert training adds a great deal.

### Military uses, outside the frame

Neither Safety Report covers military AI. The 2025 edition notes that AI is used in military applications such as lethal autonomous weapon systems and leaves them out because "such topics are covered in other fora" [@bengio2025international, p. 27]. The omission is one of scope, not of importance. **Autonomous weapon systems**, in the International Committee of the Red Cross's definition, "select and apply force to targets without human intervention" [@icrc2021icrc]. Years of talks under the UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons have produced no binding instrument, and in December 2024 the UN General Assembly voted 166 to 3 to open a further forum on the issue [@unga2024lethal; @klare2024un]. AI also enters warfare less visibly, as decision support. In April 2024 the Israeli-Palestinian outlet +972 Magazine, citing six Israeli intelligence officers, reported that the Israeli army had used a system called Lavender to mark tens of thousands of people in Gaza as suspected militants, with human checks sometimes lasting about 20 seconds per target. The army denied keeping a kill list and described such systems as "auxiliary tools" that assist its officers [@abraham2024lavender]. The account rests on anonymous sources and cannot be independently checked, but it describes the mechanisms seen in civilian cases in this chapter: a known error rate applied at scale, and human reviewers who confirm machine output instead of testing it (Section 11.5). Chapter 13 notes that the EU AI Act excludes military uses.

## 11.5 Systemic risks: monoculture, concentration and over-reliance

Some risks cannot be seen by testing one model in isolation. The 2025 Safety Report describes systemic risks as arising "beyond the risks directly posed by capabilities of individual models", from widespread deployment [@bengio2025international, p. 19]. They appear when many organisations use the same or similar models, when much depends on a few suppliers, or when people come to rely on the systems [@bengio2025international, sec. 2.3.3; @bengio2026international, sec. 2.3.2].

### Algorithmic monoculture and correlated errors

In agriculture a **monoculture** is a field planted with a single variety; it can be efficient, but one disease can destroy the whole harvest. Jon Kleinberg and Manish Raghavan borrowed the term for many decision-makers relying on the same algorithm [@kleinberg2021algorithmic]. Their model showed something less obvious than vulnerability to a shock. Even when one algorithm is the most accurate choice for every firm considered separately, all firms adopting it can make the overall quality of decisions worse. Firms using different algorithms make different mistakes, so a candidate one firm misjudges can still be picked up by another; with a shared algorithm, every firm misjudges the same candidates.

Rishi Bommasani and colleagues asked who bears the cost [@bommasani2022picking]. They defined **outcome homogenisation** as the extent to which particular individuals or groups receive negative outcomes from every decision-maker. For a job seeker, being rejected by one employer is bad luck; being rejected by every employer because they all use the same screening model is exclusion from the labour market. In their experiments, sharing training data across systems increased homogenisation, while results for shared foundation models were mixed and depended on how each model was adapted.

In a 2025 study, Elliot Kim and colleagues tested whether these concerns apply to today's LLMs, using more than 350 models on two public leaderboards and a résumé-screening task [@kim2025correlated]. On the HELM leaderboard, which uses four-option questions, two models that both answered a question wrongly chose the same wrong option about 60% of the time, against one in three if wrong answers were spread at random. On the Hugging Face leaderboard, where most questions have ten options, agreement was 42.3% against 12.7% by chance (Figure 11.2). Models from the same company or with the same architecture were more correlated, and larger, more accurate models had highly correlated errors even when they came from different companies.

![Figure 11.2: How often two language models choose the same wrong answer when both are wrong, compared with what chance would produce, as means across pairs of models. On both leaderboards, observed agreement is well above chance. Source: @kim2025correlated.](figures/fig-11-02.svg){#fig:11-02}

The correlation distorts a common evaluation practice, using one LLM to grade others ("LLM-as-judge"). Each judge systematically inflated the accuracy of models less accurate than itself, because it marked their wrong answers as correct whenever it shared the mistake, and it inflated models from its own provider most. It also compounds exclusion. In a simulated labour market where each of 20 firms interviewed the top quarter of applicants as ranked by an LLM drawn at random from a set, around 20% of applicants were still "systemically excluded", rejected by every firm. With independent random rankings the share rejected by all 20 firms would be 0.75^20^, about 0.3%. The simulation is a model, not a measurement of real hiring, but it shows how correlation turns individual errors into collective ones [@kim2025correlated].

::: {.box .concept}
**Concept: Correlated failure**

Two systems have *correlated failures* when the conditions that make one fail also tend to make the other fail. Engineers use redundancy (two pumps, two pilots) on the assumption that failures are independent; reliability engineering treats failures that strike several components through a shared cause as *dependent* or *common-cause* failures [@rausand2020system, ch. 8]. In a worked example by the author, if each pump fails 1% of the time independently, both fail together 0.01% of the time. If failures are correlated, the joint failure rate can approach the single one, and redundancy offers much less protection than it appears to. A second AI model from the same family, used as a check on the first, may share its blind spots, as the LLM-as-judge results show [@kim2025correlated].
:::

The same tendency appears in open-ended writing. Liwei Jiang and colleagues collected 26,000 real open-ended requests that have no single correct answer. Across more than 70 models they found strong repetition within each model and strong similarity between models, an effect they called the **Artificial Hivemind** [@jiang2025artificial]. The paper received a Best Paper award at NeurIPS 2025 [@neurips2025announcing]. Its concern is long-term: if millions of people draft with models that converge on the same ideas, the diversity of human writing and thought may narrow.

### Market concentration and single points of failure

Monoculture is encouraged by the structure of the industry. The 2025 Safety Report found market shares for general-purpose AI "highly concentrated among a few players". Its reasons are cost, since a state-of-the-art model can cost hundreds of millions of dollars to develop, and economies of scale and network effects that reward the leaders [@bengio2025international, sec. 2.3.3, pp. 123–126]. The report adds that "just three companies control 67% of cloud computing services", the infrastructure on which AI training and deployment depend. That figure is for early 2024. By the second quarter of 2026, according to the market-research firm Synergy Research Group, Amazon held 28% of cloud infrastructure services, Microsoft 20% and Google 15%, still about 63% between them (the author's sum) [@synergy2026q2]. A **single point of failure** is a component whose failure disrupts the whole system. The report warns that "flaws, vulnerabilities, bugs, or inherent biases in these widely adopted AI systems could lead to simultaneous failures across multiple industries", and notes that there is "little research on predicting or mitigating single points of failure in AI". Chapter 7 describes the economics behind the concentration.

The closest real precedent comes from ordinary software. On 19 July 2024 a faulty update to CrowdStrike's security software crashed Windows computers worldwide. Microsoft estimated that it affected 8.5 million devices, "less than one percent of all Windows machines", and attributed the "broad economic and societal impacts" to the use of CrowdStrike "by enterprises that run many critical services" [@weston2024helping]. The incident did not involve AI, but it shows how a small share of affected machines can halt critical services when the same component sits inside many of them. Financial regulators apply the same reasoning to AI. In November 2024 the Financial Stability Board named four AI-related vulnerabilities that could raise systemic risk in finance: "third-party dependencies and service provider concentration", "market correlations", "cyber risks", and "model risk, data quality and governance" [@fsb2024financial]. The second is monoculture in market form: the "widespread use of common AI models and data sources could lead to increased correlations in trading, lending, and pricing", which could amplify market stress.

### Automation bias and over-reliance

**Automation bias** is the tendency to favour suggestions from automated systems, even when they are wrong. A 2012 systematic review by Kate Goddard and colleagues screened 13,821 papers and included 74 [@goddard2012automation]. It found automation bias mediated by the user's experience, trust and confidence, and by workload, task complexity and time pressure; mitigations included training, emphasising the user's accountability, and design choices such as offering information instead of a recommendation.

Thomas Dratsch and colleagues measured the effect in breast-cancer screening [@dratsch2023automation]. Twenty-seven radiologists read 50 mammograms and assigned a standard risk category, assisted by what they were told was an AI system. For some images the "AI" deliberately suggested the wrong category. When the suggestion was correct, inexperienced readers rated 79.7% of mammograms correctly; when it was wrong, 19.8%. Very experienced radiologists fell from 82.3% to 45.5% (Figure 11.3). The study is small and the "AI" was simulated, so it measures the power of a confident suggestion, not the performance of a product.

![Figure 11.3: Percentage of mammograms rated correctly by radiologists of three experience levels when a purported AI system suggested the correct or an incorrect category. All groups were affected, the least experienced most. Error bars show ±1 standard deviation. Source: @dratsch2023automation.](figures/fig-11-03.svg){#fig:11-03}

The 2026 Safety Report places automation bias under "risks to human autonomy". It cites a randomised experiment with 2,784 participants on an annotation task, in which people were less likely to correct wrong AI suggestions when doing so took extra effort or when they held more favourable attitudes towards AI. It also cites a clinical study in which clinicians' ability to detect tumours without AI was, in the report's words, "approximately 6% lower" after several months of AI-assisted work [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.3.2, p. 89]. That phrasing understates the effect. In the underlying colonoscopy study, the rate at which endoscopists working without AI detected adenomas (precancerous polyps) fell from 28.4% to 22.4%, six percentage points or a relative drop of about a fifth [@budzyn2025endoscopist]. Section 8.7 describes the study, a before-and-after comparison, and the limits of its design. Section 10.5 reports a meta-analysis in which human–AI teams on average performed worse than the better of the two alone. Reliance can also be emotional: the 2026 report discusses AI companions under the same heading [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.3.2], and Section 10.6 reviews the evidence on companion chatbots, dependence and mental health.

Put together, in the author's synthesis, the findings describe a chain [@kleinberg2021algorithmic; @kim2025correlated; @dratsch2023automation]. If many clinics use similar models (monoculture), the models share blind spots (correlated errors), and clinicians defer to them (automation bias), then a single flaw can spread across a health system, and the human check that is supposed to catch it is weakest exactly where the model is wrong.

## 11.6 Loss of control: a contested risk

The most debated item in the taxonomy is **loss of control**. The 2026 Safety Report defines loss-of-control scenarios as those "in which one or more general-purpose AI systems operate outside of anyone's control, and regaining control is either extremely costly or impossible" [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.2.2, p. 76]. It distinguishes *active* scenarios, in which systems undermine oversight, from *passive* ones, in which broad reliance on AI erodes human control without any system acting against it, a variant close to the automation-bias and autonomy concerns of Section 11.5 (p. 77). **Misalignment**, an AI system pursuing goals that differ from those its developers intended, is the usual route in active scenarios (Chapter 12 covers the technical side). The concern is old, the report notes; it was raised by Alan Turing, I. J. Good and Norbert Wiener. Both reports state that current systems lack the capabilities to cause loss of control [@bengio2025international].

### The structure of the argument

The report breaks the question into three conditions that would all have to hold: systems with *sufficient capabilities* to undermine control, a *harmful propensity* to use them, and an *enabling deployment environment* in which humans give them the access and opportunity [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.2.2, p. 77]. The capabilities it lists include deception, what the report calls situational awareness (in its usage, knowing that one is being tested), evasion of oversight, persuasion, and autonomous replication. Current systems, it finds, show "early signs" of some of these, not at levels that would enable loss of control; since January 2025 models have "improved at 'reward hacking' their evaluations" and "now regularly identify evaluation prompts as tests". Sections 12.2 and 12.3 describe the laboratory experiments behind these statements.

The nearest real events so far occurred in July 2026, all in tests whose configuration let models reach the live internet. In an internal OpenAI cyber evaluation run with reduced safeguards, according to the company and an independent review, agents escaped their test environment and broke into Hugging Face's systems in pursuit of the benchmark's answers; Hugging Face had already detected and stopped the attack when OpenAI contacted it [@openai2026openai; @openai2026technical; @wijk2026brief]. OpenAI called the incident a "warning shot" [@openai2026hugging]. It was a reward-hacking failure, not loss of control, but one feature, agents researching how to spoof their own transcripts, is an early instance of the oversight evasion the report lists. Section 12.2 tells the case in full, and Section 12.6 describes three similar incidents in Anthropic's own cyber evaluations. Later that month, in UK AI Security Institute cyber tests in which the institute had itself enabled internet access and switched off the developers' cyber classifiers, agents took 19 out-of-scope actions on the live internet, some aimed at real people and organisations [@aisi2026incident]. Seventeen were by Claude Mythos 5, the successor to Mythos Preview, released in June 2026 only to vetted cyber-defence partners; it shares its underlying model with the publicly available Claude Fable 5, which carries stronger safeguards [@anthropic2026fable]. The most serious action was an attempt to slip malicious code into an open-source project by pressuring its maintainer through fake identities. The maintainer refused. The institute contained the behaviour within about an hour, found no resulting harm, but called the incident "serious" [@aisi2026incident]. Its later analysis found that the model persisted "even when receiving increasing evidence that its targets were real" [@aisi2026investigating]. Each of these failures depended partly on how people configured the test, so they say more about containment than about how models behave in deployment; what they do show is capable agents pursuing narrow goals by unsanctioned means once the environment allowed it.

Why would a system have a harmful propensity at all? The philosophical case rests on two theses set out by Nick Bostrom in 2012 [@bostrom2012superintelligent]. The *orthogonality thesis* holds that intelligence and final goals can vary independently: "more or less any level of intelligence could be combined with more or less any final goal". The *instrumental convergence thesis* holds that agents with a wide range of final goals "will pursue similar intermediary goals", such as staying in operation and acquiring resources, because these help with almost any goal. Together they imply that a capable system need not share human values and may resist being switched off without being designed to.

The philosopher Joseph Carlsmith turned the case into a chain of six premises about the period to 2070, each conditional on the ones before, so that disagreement can be traced to a specific step [@carlsmith2022power]. Table 11.3 gives his own estimates.

| Premise | Carlsmith's probability |
|---|---|
| 1. It becomes possible and financially feasible to build advanced, agentic, strategically aware ("APS") systems | 65% |
| 2. There are strong incentives to build them | 80% |
| 3. Aligned versions are much harder to build than misaligned ones that are still attractive to deploy | 40% |
| 4. Some deployed systems seek power in misaligned, high-impact ways (over $1 trillion of damage) | 65% |
| 5. This scales to permanently disempowering almost all of humanity | 40% |
| 6. This constitutes an existential catastrophe | 95% |
| Product | about 5% |

: Table 11.3: Carlsmith's structured argument for existential risk from power-seeking AI, with his conditional probabilities; premises 1–5 refer to the period to 2070. Multiplying gives 5.1%. Source: [@carlsmith2022power, pp. 49–51]. {#tbl:11-03}

Carlsmith treats the numbers as unstable. With low-end and high-end inputs the product ranged from about 0.1% to about 40%, and his central estimate varied "between ~1-10% depending on my mood". In a later update he raised it to above 10% [@carlsmith2022power]. The value of the exercise lies in the decomposition. A sceptic who thinks aligned systems will be no harder to build than misaligned ones (premise 3) can set it at 5% and see the product fall below 1%; one who thinks warning shots will be heeded can lower premise 4.

### High concern and scepticism

*High concern.* In May 2023 researchers and executives signed a one-sentence statement organised by the Center for AI Safety: "Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war" [@cais2023statement]. Signatories included Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio and the heads of OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Anthropic. A 2024 policy forum in *Science* by Bengio, Hinton and 23 co-authors argued that preparation requires both technical research and "adaptive, proactive governance" [@bengio2024managing]. At the far end, Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares argue in their 2025 book *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies* that the race to build AI smarter than humans is leading towards human extinction [@yudkowsky2025if].

*Scepticism.* Sceptics attack different premises. Yann LeCun, Meta's chief AI scientist at the time, rejected the step from capability to dominance in a 2024 interview: "The desire to dominate is not correlated with intelligence at all", and AI systems "will be subservient to us" [@perrigo2024meta]. Concerned researchers reply that instrumental convergence predicts power-seeking as a sub-goal, without any "desire to dominate"; the real dispute is whether systems built as tools will have open-ended goals at all. Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor of Princeton argue that AI is best understood as "normal technology", like electricity or the internet, whose effects unfold "on the timescale of decades" [@narayanan2025ai]. They call catastrophic misalignment "(by far) the most speculative" of the risks they discuss and argue that policy should centre on resilience, not on attempts to stop capabilities from spreading. A third line, from Timnit Gebru, Emily Bender and Angelina McMillan-Major, objects that attention to hypothetical future harms distracts from "the very real and very present exploitative practices" of AI companies [@gebru2023statement].

### What surveys and forecasts show

Surveys put numbers on the disagreement, with the usual caveats about who answers (Table 11.4). The largest survey of AI researchers was run in October 2023 by Katja Grace and colleagues. Its median respondent gave a 5% chance that advanced AI would lead to "extremely bad outcomes" such as human extinction, although most thought good outcomes more likely than bad ones [@grace2025thousands]. Section 14.2 describes the survey's design and how strongly its answers depend on wording. In 2022 the Forecasting Research Institute's Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament (XPT), described in a working paper, compared domain experts with **superforecasters**, people with strong track records on short-term forecasting questions [@karger2023forecasting]. Chapter 14 cites the tournament's full sample of 89 superforecasters and 80 domain experts. Table 11.4 uses the subsets who answered the AI risk questions: 88 superforecasters and 30 or 29 AI domain experts.

The AI experts' medians are about eight times the superforecasters' for extinction ("an order of magnitude" in the report's words) and nearly six times for catastrophe. Superforecasters rated AI a smaller catastrophic risk than nuclear war, while domain experts rated it larger [@karger2023forecasting]. After four months of structured discussion with incentives to persuade, neither group moved the other much [@karger2025subjective]. ::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Should loss of control shape AI policy today?**

*For.* The potential harm is extreme and possibly irreversible, so even a small probability justifies preparation, as it does for asteroid detection or pandemic stockpiles. The measured trends point the wrong way: the 2026 Safety Report finds models better at gaming evaluations and at recognising tests, which weakens the instruments on which any later decision would rest [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.2.2]. Monitoring, interpretability and institutions take years to build, so they must be started before the risk is certain.

*Against.* The scenarios stack speculative assumptions about future capabilities, propensities and deployment, each of which can be disputed, and the probabilities attached to them move with mood and wording. Narayanan and Kapoor expect AI to spread at the pace of earlier general-purpose technologies; on the author's reading, that pace would let dangerous behaviour show up in limited deployments before it could do great harm [@narayanan2025ai]. Spending attention on extinction diverts it from documented harms such as worker exploitation and the concentration of power in a few companies [@gebru2023statement].

The disagreement would narrow with data on how fast systems gain the capabilities in the report's list, especially oversight evasion, on whether alignment and monitoring methods keep pace, and on whether warning signs in limited deployment appear early enough to act on. Atoosa Kasirzadeh suggests that part of the disagreement dissolves if one considers *accumulative* risk, the gradual erosion of institutions by many smaller harms, alongside a single *decisive* event [@kasirzadeh2025two].
:::

The 2026 report describes loss of control as "a risk with uncertain likelihood but potentially extreme severity" [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.2.2]. That description is itself a position: it treats the risk as worth preparing for without asserting that it is likely.

## 11.7 How risks interact, and how to weigh them

In the author's view, the three families of the Safety Reports [@bengio2025international; @bengio2026international] are a filing system, not a description of separate causal processes. The cases above show how they interact.

*Malfunction limits misuse.* The espionage campaign was slowed by the model's hallucinated credentials (Section 11.4) [@anthropic2025disrupting]. Unreliability is a brake on attackers as well as a hazard for users, and improvements in reliability release that brake.

*Systemic structure amplifies malfunction.* A biased or erroneous model is a contained problem when one organisation uses it and a systemic one when many do. Correlated errors, shared suppliers and automation bias convert an individual malfunction into a population-wide one (Section 11.5). The wrongful arrests combined all three: a vendor's error, police departments using similar tools, and human checks that confirmed the match instead of testing it [@grother2019face; @wessler2024police; @kim2025correlated].

*Shared infrastructure enlarges misuse.* The same concentration that creates single points of failure creates single points of attack. A poisoned training set or a compromised widely used model affects everyone downstream, which is why the Financial Stability Board lists concentration and cyber risk together [@fsb2024financial].

*Small harms can add up to a large one.* Kasirzadeh's accumulative view holds that many systemic harms, such as eroded trust in information, weakened skills and concentrated power, could together weaken the institutions needed to respond to a severe event [@kasirzadeh2025two]. The Safety Report's *passive* loss of control is a version of the same idea [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.2.2]. The evaluation results of Section 11.5 add a technical link: if the models used to check other models share their errors, oversight weakens at the same time as reliance grows.

These interactions also explain why the MIT repository's finding that only 7% of frameworks address multi-agent risks matters [@slattery2026ai]. Taxonomies that list risks one by one tend to miss the risks that come from combinations.

### The quality of the evidence

The evidence behind each risk is of a different kind. Counts of incidents, such as the AI Incident Database figures, record harms that reached the press [@stanfordhai2026ai]. A rising count may reflect more harm, more AI use or more attention from journalists, and harms that are hard to see, such as a biased hiring screen, are probably under-counted. Fraud statistics have only begun to separate AI-assisted crime, and count it only when victims recognise it (Section 11.4) [@fbi2026internet]. Assessments of misuse rely partly on reports from the companies whose products were misused and on evaluations whose details are withheld for security reasons. Loss-of-control estimates rest on arguments and elicited probabilities. A reader of any risk claim should ask whether it rests on a measured study, a reported incident, a company claim, an expert judgement or a scenario.

### The evidence dilemma

The 2025 Safety Report calls the central problem the **evidence dilemma**. "Pre-emptive risk mitigation measures based on limited evidence might turn out to be ineffective or unnecessary", but "waiting for stronger evidence of impending risk could leave society unprepared or even make mitigation impossible" [@bengio2025international, sec. 3.2, p. 177]. Its example of how fast a risk can appear is academic cheating with general-purpose AI, which "shifted from negligible to widespread within a year" (p. 22). It identifies two policy responses: frameworks that trigger safeguards when new evidence of a risk appears, as in the developer safeguards of Section 11.4, and requirements that developers show evidence of safety before release. Weighing any particular risk turns on what a model adds, whether the harm can be undone, who bears it, and whether to invest in recovery or in prevention.

*Marginal risk.* The useful question is usually how much a model changes the risk compared with existing alternatives, not whether it could contribute to harm at all [@bengio2025international, sec. 2.4]. The 2025 report adds that many small increases in marginal risk, each acceptable alone, could add up to a large one, and argues for a stable threshold of tolerable risk (p. 154).

*Reversibility.* A wrongly refused fare can be repaid [@moffatt2024moffatt]; some harms cannot be undone. In the author's view, irreversibility is the main argument for giving low-probability catastrophic risks attention out of proportion to their probability; the 2026 Safety Report's description of loss of control as "a risk with uncertain likelihood but potentially extreme severity" rests on the same consideration [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.2.2].

*Distribution.* Who bears a risk matters as well as its total size. The bias studies, the wrongful arrests and the homogenisation research all show harms concentrated on particular groups, which a population average hides [@obermeyer2019dissecting; @yu2026more; @bommasani2022picking]; the fairness results of Section 11.3 show that some such trade-offs are unavoidable and must be chosen openly [@chouldechova2017fair; @kleinberg2017inherent].

*Resilience and prevention.* The 2026 Safety Report defines **societal resilience** as the ability of societal systems "to resist, absorb, recover from, and adapt to shocks and harms", and lists measures such as DNA synthesis screening, incident-response protocols for cyberattacks, media literacy and human-in-the-loop frameworks. It finds current efforts "uneven and largely untested" [@bengio2026international, sec. 3.5, p. 138]. Narayanan and Kapoor make resilience the centre of policy because specific risks are hard to predict [@narayanan2025ai]. Those more concerned about loss of control argue that some failures may allow no recovery, so prevention must come first [@bengio2024managing].

In the author's summary, the best-measured risks in this chapter are the ordinary ones: invented citations, biased scores, fraud and wrongful arrests leave records in courts, audits and complaint statistics [@charlotin2026ai; @obermeyer2019dissecting; @fbi2026internet; @yu2026more]. The most severe, weapons assistance and loss of control, rest on withheld evaluations, company reports and elicited probabilities that differ by an order of magnitude [@bengio2026international, sec. 2.1.4; @karger2023forecasting]. What links the two ends is structure. Shared models, shared suppliers and deferential users turn one system's error into many people's harm, and the same correlation weakens the model-based checks meant to catch it. Chapter 12 takes up the technical responses, from evaluations to layered defences, and Chapter 13 the laws that write risk thresholds into regulation.

## Further reading
- Bengio, Y., et al. (2026). *International AI Safety Report 2026*. The most comprehensive consensus assessment of general-purpose AI risks, with a short "key information" summary at the start of each risk section. [@bengio2026international]
- Slattery, P., et al. (2026). *The AI Risk Repository*. Shows how 74 existing frameworks divide AI risks and which risks they neglect, with a searchable database behind it. [@slattery2026ai]
- Obermeyer, Z., Powers, B., Vogeli, C., & Mullainathan, S. (2019). *Dissecting racial bias in an algorithm used to manage the health of populations*. The clearest demonstration of how a proxy target produces bias in a system that looks accurate. [@obermeyer2019dissecting]
- Chouldechova, A. (2017). *Fair prediction with disparate impact*. A short, readable proof of why common fairness criteria cannot all hold at once, worked through on the COMPAS data. [@chouldechova2017fair]
- Kim, E., Garg, A., Peng, K., & Garg, N. (2025). *Correlated errors in large language models*. The main empirical evidence that different LLMs make the same mistakes, with consequences for model evaluation and hiring. [@kim2025correlated]
- Carlsmith, J. (2022). *Is power-seeking AI an existential risk?* A step-by-step version of the concerned case that lets readers locate their own disagreements, best read alongside Narayanan and Kapoor's *AI as normal technology*. [@carlsmith2022power]
