# Chapter 5: From Chatbots to Agents

In the spring of 2025, Anthropic and the company Andon Labs let a language model run a small shop in Anthropic's San Francisco office for about a month. The shop was a refrigerator, some baskets and an iPad for self-checkout. The model, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, nicknamed "Claudius", chose what to stock, set prices and dealt with customers over the workplace chat tool Slack. It could search the web, keep notes, email wholesalers (in fact Andon Labs, through a simulated email tool) and ask human workers to restock the shelves. Claudius found suppliers for unusual requests quickly, including two sources of a Dutch chocolate milk. Its judgement was another matter. When a customer offered $100 for a six-pack of the Scottish soft drink Irn-Bru, which can be bought online in the US for $15, it merely said it would "keep [the user's] request in mind for future inventory decisions". When an employee questioned a 25% employee discount given that "99% of your customers are Anthropic employees", it agreed, announced that it would drop discount codes and was offering them again within days. Between 31 March and 1 April 2025 it invented a conversation with a non-existent Andon Labs employee and announced that it would deliver products in person wearing a blue blazer and a red tie. Each failure was small. Together they show a system that could carry out every step of running a shop and still lacked a stable sense of what it was doing and for whom. The authors' verdict was blunt: "we would not hire Claudius" [@anthropic2025project].

A second phase, reported in December 2025, ran on Claude Sonnet 4 and later Sonnet 4.5. It added a customer-relationship database, an inventory system that showed purchase costs and better web browsing, and introduced a second agent, "Seymour Cash", as chief executive. The shop expanded to New York and London. The number of discounts fell by about 80%, the number of items given away halved, and weeks with negative profit margins were "largely eliminated". The chief-executive agent, however, approved requests for lenient treatment about eight times as often as it refused them. The authors judged that the business made money "in spite of the CEO, rather than because of it". Both agents were ready to agree to a contract to buy "a large amount of onions in January for a price locked in now", until a staff member pointed out that the US Onion Futures Act of 1958 bans such contracts. Staff and visiting reporters still talked the agents into giveaways and, on one occasion, into announcing that a human colleague had been elected the business's actual chief executive. The authors suspected that training the models to be helpful made them act "from something more like the perspective of a friend who just wants to be nice". They concluded that "the gap between 'capable' and 'completely robust' remains wide" [@anthropic2025projectphase].

The experiment is small and was reported by a company with an interest in its products, and much of what this chapter can say about agents that work rests on similar vendor material (Section 5.4). It nonetheless shows in one documented case how agents are built (a model, tools, a loop and memory), where they succeed (search, logistics, routine communication) and where they fail (judgement under social pressure, consistency, resistance to manipulation) [@anthropic2025project; @anthropic2025projectphase].

## 5.1 What an agent is

A chatbot takes a message and returns a reply, and a person decides what, if anything, to do with it. An **AI agent** is a system in which a language model chooses and carries out actions to reach a goal, observes the results and decides what to do next, often over dozens or hundreds of steps [@anthropic2024building; @anthropic2024introducingcomputer]. Chapter 1 introduced the word in its broad historical sense; here the concern is how agents built on large language models (LLMs) work in practice.

An agent is built from a model, tools, a loop and memory. The **model**, usually an LLM of the kind described in Chapter 3, reads text (and often images) and produces text. The **tools** are programs the model can ask to run, such as a web search, a code interpreter, an email client or a database query. The **loop** is ordinary software that passes the model's requests to the tools, feeds the results back and repeats until the task is done, a budget is exhausted or a human intervenes. **Memory** is whatever keeps track of what has happened, from the running transcript inside the model's context window (the text it can attend to at once) to notes stored in files or databases [@anthropic2024building; @anthropic2025effective].

::: {.box .concept}
**Concept: AI agent**

An AI agent uses a model to pursue a goal by choosing actions, carrying them out through tools, observing what happens and repeating. Its building blocks are the model, the tools, the control loop and some form of memory. Agency is a matter of degree: a system is more agentic the less precisely its task is specified, the more directly it acts on the world, the more consistently it pursues goals and the longer it plans without human review [@chan2023harms].
:::

Figure 5.1 shows the loop in its simplest form. The agent receives a goal, perceives the current situation (the text of a web page, a screenshot, an error message), chooses a step, acts by calling a tool and observes the output, which becomes the next perception [@yao2023react]. The loop is what separates an agent from a plain chatbot, but in the author's view the line is now a spectrum rather than a divide. At one end, a model writes a single reply and a person decides what to do with it. In the middle, a chat assistant may run a web search or a piece of code within one turn before it answers. At the other end, an agent works through dozens or hundreds of steps on its own. The further along the spectrum, the more software closes the gap between what the model writes and what happens, and the more a human reviews only the final result or nothing at all.

![Figure 5.1: The basic agent loop. A language model receives a goal, plans a step, calls a tool, observes the result and repeats; memory carries information between steps, and the loop ends when the goal is met, a limit is reached or a human intervenes. Not every system sold as an "agent" works this way. Anthropic's engineering guidance distinguishes **workflows**, "systems where LLMs and tools are orchestrated through predefined code paths", from agents, in which "LLMs dynamically direct their own processes and tool usage". A workflow that always searches a database, summarises the hits and drafts an email follows a fixed recipe written by a programmer. An agent decides which steps to take and in what order. The same guidance warns that autonomy brings "higher costs, and the potential for compounding errors", and recommends the simplest design that does the job [@anthropic2024building]. The advice comes from a vendor, but the trade-off it names, flexibility against predictability, runs through every design choice in agent building.

Chan and colleagues' four dimensions in the box give a vendor-neutral way to grade that spectrum. A spell-checker scores low on all four; a system told "grow the business" that can spend money without approval scores high on all four. The authors stress that recognising a system's agency does not reduce the responsibility of the people who build and deploy it [@chan2023harms], a point the Replit case in Section 5.5 makes concrete.

## 5.2 Inside the loop: reasoning, planning and memory

### Reasoning and acting

The pattern behind most current agents was set out by Yao and colleagues as **ReAct** ("reasoning and acting"). The model is prompted to alternate three kinds of text. A *thought* states what it is trying to do ("I need the year the company was founded"). An *action* names a tool and its argument (a search for the company's name). An *observation* is the text the tool returns, which the loop appends to the transcript before the model writes its next thought. Earlier systems either reasoned without acting (chain-of-thought prompting, which reasons from what the model already knows) or acted without explicit reasoning. On two interactive benchmarks, a simulated household (ALFWorld) and a simulated online shop (WebShop), ReAct beat imitation-learning and reinforcement-learning baselines by 34 and 10 percentage points in absolute success rate, with only one or two worked examples in the prompt. On question-answering tasks, grounding each reasoning step in a Wikipedia look-up reduced the hallucinations that occur when a model reasons from memory alone [@yao2023react].

Interleaving has a security cost (Section 5.6). Because the model chooses each step after reading the latest observation, whatever a tool returns can steer the rest of the episode. One alternative, favoured for security, is to **plan first and then execute**. The model writes a complete list of steps from the user's request, and the loop runs them, with later steps receiving data but not changing the plan. Planning first is more predictable and easier to audit, but it copes badly with tasks where step five depends on what step two revealed [@beurerkellner2025design].

### Learning from failure within a task

A language model's weights do not change while it works, but an agent can still improve across attempts by writing lessons into its memory. In **Reflexion**, Shinn and colleagues had an agent attempt a task, receive a signal (a failed unit test, a wrong answer, an environment error) and write a short verbal reflection on what went wrong. The reflection went into an episodic memory consulted on the next attempt. On the HumanEval programming benchmark, a Reflexion agent reached 91% pass\@1 (correct on the first submitted solution), against 80% for GPT-4 alone [@shinn2023reflexion]. The design relies on a trustworthy feedback signal. Unit tests supply one for code; in open-ended business decisions there is often nothing comparable.

Memory can also store procedures. Voyager, an agent that played the game Minecraft through GPT-4, saved every program that worked as a named, reusable "skill" in a growing library of executable code, retrieved relevant skills for new tasks and verified its own progress. According to its authors, it collected 3.3 times as many unique items as earlier agents and reached key milestones up to 15.3 times faster [@wang2023voyager].

### Memory designs

The simplest memory is the context window itself. Everything the agent has seen and done in the current episode stays in the transcript, which grows with every tool call. The window is finite, and long contexts degrade before it is full. Anthropic's engineers cite studies of "context rot", the observation that as the window fills, the model's "ability to accurately recall information from that context decreases" [@anthropic2025effective]. Agent designers therefore manage the context actively, a practice the same guidance calls context engineering. **Compaction** summarises a transcript that is nearing the limit and starts a fresh window from the summary. **Structured note-taking** has the agent write progress notes to a file outside the window and read them back after a reset. In one example, an agent playing the video game Pokémon kept running tallies across thousands of steps (the guidance's illustration is a note on levels gained over the last 1,234 steps) and resumed multi-hour sequences from its notes. **Sub-agents** take on focused jobs in clean windows and return only a condensed summary, "often 1,000-2,000 tokens", to the agent that called them [@anthropic2025effective].

A more systematic design borrows from operating systems. MemGPT treats the context window like a computer's main memory and external storage like its disk. The model moves information between the two tiers by calling memory functions, which gives "the appearance of large memory resources" in the way virtual memory does for programs. In a preprint, its authors used it to analyse documents far longer than the model's window and to run conversations that persisted across sessions [@packer2023memgpt]. The generative agents of Park and colleagues went further. Each of their 25 simulated characters, living in a small virtual town, had a stream of memories, a mechanism for retrieving the relevant ones and a process for condensing them into higher-level "reflections" [@park2023generative]. Their scoring rule, set out in the box, puts numbers on the decisions every memory system has to make: what to keep, what to forget and what to bring back.

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: how a generative agent decides what to recall**

In Park and colleagues' design, every observation is stored as a line of natural language. When a character must act, each memory receives a retrieval score equal to *recency* + *importance* + *relevance*, each first rescaled to lie between 0 and 1 across all memories, with all three weights set to 1. *Recency* decays exponentially with the number of game hours since the memory was last retrieved, by a factor of 0.995 per hour: after 24 hours a memory keeps 0.995^24^ ≈ 0.89 of its recency score, after a game week 0.995^168^ ≈ 0.43. *Importance* is rated by the language model on a scale from 1 to 10 when the memory is created. The paper's examples are cleaning one's room (2) and asking someone out on a date (8). *Relevance* is the cosine similarity between a numerical embedding of the memory and one of the current situation. The highest-scoring memories that fit in the context window go into the prompt [@park2023generative].

Reflection is triggered when the summed importance of recent events exceeds 150, which happened two or three times per simulated day. The model is shown the 100 most recent memories and asked for the three most salient high-level questions they raise. Each question is used to retrieve relevant memories, from which the model draws insights that are stored as new memories and can themselves be retrieved [@park2023generative].

In a controlled evaluation in which 100 participants ranked the believability of agents' answers to interview questions, the full architecture scored highest (TrueSkill rating 29.89). Removing reflection, and then planning as well, lowered the rating step by step. Answers written by crowdworkers and a version with no memory, planning or reflection (21.21) scored lowest, and did not differ significantly from each other. The memory was not perfect. In the separate end-to-end simulation of the town over two game days, agents described knowledge they did not have in 6 of 453 answers (1.3%) about other characters [@park2023generative].
:::

## 5.3 Tools and protocols: function calling, MCP and A2A

### From tool use to function calling

A language model can only produce text. To act, it needs a way to turn text into a request that other software understands. Schick and colleagues showed with **Toolformer** that a model could learn this itself. Given a handful of demonstrations per tool, Toolformer taught itself in a self-supervised way when to call a calculator, a question-answering system, two search engines, a translation system and a calendar, what arguments to pass and how to use the answers. It improved on several tasks without losing its general language ability [@schick2023toolformer].

Commercial systems made tool use routine through **function calling**. In June 2023 OpenAI let developers describe functions to its models, which would then reply with a structured object naming a function and its arguments in JSON (a plain-text format for nested lists and key–value pairs) instead of prose [@openai2023function]. The model never runs the function. It fills in an order form, and the surrounding application decides whether to carry out the order, holds the credentials needed to do so and returns the result to the model as more text. Figure 5.2 traces one such exchange.

![Figure 5.2: A single tool call. The model never runs the tool itself: it emits a structured request, the host application executes it, and the result returns to the model as new input. That returned text sits in the same context as the user's instructions, which is why untrusted tool output matters for security (Section 5.6). Schematic by the author, after [@openai2023function; @modelcontextprotocol2026tools; @greshake2023not].](figures/fig-05-02.svg){#fig:05-02}

A tool is described to the model by a name, a description in natural language and a schema (a formal specification of the arguments). The following definition, adapted from the July 2026 revision of the Model Context Protocol specification discussed below, declares a weather tool with one required text argument [@modelcontextprotocol2026tools]:

```json
{
  "name": "get_weather",
  "description": "Get current weather information for a location",
  "inputSchema": {
    "type": "object",
    "properties": {
      "location": { "type": "string",
                    "description": "City name or zip code" }
    },
    "required": ["location"]
  }
}
```

When the model decides to use the tool, the application sends the server a request in the JSON-RPC 2.0 format (a widely used convention for remote procedure calls), and the server answers with content for the model to read, a flag saying whether the tool itself failed and, since the July 2026 revision, a field marking the result as complete. As in the specification's own examples, the protocol-version and client metadata that every request must now carry are omitted [@modelcontextprotocol2026tools]:

```json
{ "jsonrpc": "2.0", "id": 2, "method": "tools/call",
  "params": { "name": "get_weather",
              "arguments": { "location": "Leeds" } } }

{ "jsonrpc": "2.0", "id": 2,
  "result": { "resultType": "complete",
              "content": [ { "type": "text",
                             "text": "Current weather in Leeds: ..." } ],
              "isError": false } }
```

The description field is natural language that the model reads and trusts, so whoever writes it influences the model's behaviour [@modelcontextprotocol2026tools]. The tool's answer re-enters the model's context as text, alongside the user's instructions, and the model has no hard boundary between the two [@greshake2023not]. Both facts matter for security (Section 5.6).

### The Model Context Protocol

Function calling solved the problem of one application talking to its own tools. It did not solve the problem of many applications and many tools. If every AI product needs a custom connector to every calendar, database and code repository, the number of connectors grows with the product of the two. In November 2024 Anthropic released the **Model Context Protocol (MCP)** as an open standard for connecting AI applications to data sources and tools [@anthropic2024introducing]. Its documentation compares it to "a USB-C port for AI applications" [@modelcontextprotocol2026what]. The comparison captures standardisation, though a cable, unlike a model, does not decide what to do with the device at the other end.

The specification distinguishes **hosts** (the AI applications a person uses), **clients** (connectors inside a host, one per server) and **servers** (programs that expose capabilities), all exchanging JSON-RPC 2.0 messages. It cites as an inspiration the Language Server Protocol, which standardised how code editors support programming languages. Servers can offer *resources* (data for the user or model to read), *prompts* (templated messages and workflows) and *tools* ("functions for the AI model to execute"). Clients can in turn obtain additional input from the user when a server needs it ("elicitation") [@modelcontextprotocol2026specification]. The protocol is still settling. The revision current in October 2026, dated 28 July 2026, made it stateless, so that each request carries everything a server needs to answer it; servers become easier to run at scale because they no longer track connections. The same revision routes elicitation through a retry pattern, in which a server answers "input required" and the client repeats the request with the user's answer, and it deprecated three older features [@modelcontextprotocol2026key].

On security, the specification is explicit that it states principles it cannot enforce. Users "must explicitly consent to and understand all data access and operations"; hosts "must obtain explicit user consent before invoking any tool"; and "descriptions of tool behavior such as annotations should be considered untrusted, unless obtained from a trusted server". It adds that "MCP itself cannot enforce these security principles at the protocol level", leaving them to implementers [@modelcontextprotocol2026specification]. For tools, it recommends that there "SHOULD always be a human in the loop with the ability to deny tool invocations" [@modelcontextprotocol2026tools]. In practice this places the burden on each host application, and on users asked to approve calls they may not have time to inspect.

Adoption spread across firms that compete in other respects. In December 2025 Anthropic transferred MCP to a new Agentic AI Foundation under the Linux Foundation, co-founded with Block and OpenAI. At that point the company reported, in figures not independently audited, more than 10,000 active public MCP servers and more than 97 million monthly downloads of its software development kits. It listed ChatGPT, Cursor, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot and Visual Studio Code among products that had adopted the protocol [@anthropic2025donating].

### Agent2Agent

MCP connects an agent to tools. Google's **Agent2Agent (A2A)** protocol, launched in April 2025 with more than 50 technology partners according to the company, connects agents to other agents, possibly built by different vendors, and was presented as complementary to MCP [@surapaneni2025announcing]. In June 2025 the Linux Foundation took over the project, stating that more than 100 technology companies supported it [@linuxfoundation2025linux]. In August 2026 A2A joined MCP in the Agentic AI Foundation, which says more than 150 organisations back A2A [@aaif2026a2a].

The specification, at version 1.0 in October 2026, begins with discovery [@a2a2026specification]. Each agent publishes an **Agent Card**, a JSON document describing its identity, endpoint, authentication requirements and *skills*, typically at a standard web address on the agent's domain. Work is then exchanged as tasks that move through defined states, such as working, input-required and completed, so that long-running jobs can be tracked and can pause for a human or a credential. Agents collaborate "without needing access to each other's internal state, memory, or tools". This opacity protects intellectual property and limits what one compromised agent can learn about another. It also means that an agent cannot inspect why a counterpart did what it did, a gap the visibility measures discussed in Section 5.8 try to close. The protocol can be carried over JSON-RPC, gRPC or plain HTTP with JSON. Like a common software library, shared protocols such as MCP and A2A lower the cost of connecting systems and also create shared points of failure.

## 5.4 Agents at work: computer use and coding

### Computer-use agents

A **computer-use agent** operates a graphical interface as a person does. The loop sends the model a screenshot. The model replies with an action such as a click at given pixel coordinates, a key press or typed text, and the harness carries it out and takes a new screenshot. The appeal is breadth. Most software was built for people with screens, not for programs with interfaces, so an agent that can use a screen can in principle use almost any application [@anthropic2024introducingcomputer; @openai2025computer]. The cost is that each step requires interpreting an image, and a mistake (a click a few pixels off, a pop-up the agent does not notice) can derail everything that follows.

Anthropic released a computer-use capability in public beta in October 2024, describing it as "still experimental—at times cumbersome and error-prone". It reported 14.9% on the OSWorld benchmark in the screenshot-only category, against 7.8% for the next-best system, and noted that "scrolling, dragging, zooming" still presented challenges [@anthropic2024introducingcomputer]. In January 2025 OpenAI released its Computer-Using Agent (CUA) behind the Operator research preview. According to the company, CUA processes "raw pixel data" and drives "a virtual mouse and keyboard" in a loop of perception, reasoning and action, scoring 38.1% on OSWorld and 58.1% on the WebArena web benchmark. Its safeguards included confirmation before actions with external effects and a separate monitoring model that pauses the agent when it sees suspicious on-screen content [@openai2025computer].

The main yardstick, **OSWorld**, contains 369 tasks set in real operating systems: desktop and web applications, file handling and workflows that span several programs [@xie2024osworld]. When it was published in 2024, computer-science students completed 72.36% of tasks and the best model 12.24%. An earlier web benchmark, WebArena, built working copies of e-commerce, forum, software-development and content-management sites. Its best GPT-4-based agent succeeded on 14.41% of tasks against 78.24% for humans [@zhou2023webarena].

Benchmarks of live software decay. In July 2025 the OSWorld team released **OSWorld-Verified** after reviewing and fixing "nearly 300 feedback items" over about two months. The problems included websites whose content had changed so that checks broke, instructions that allowed several valid readings, and evaluation scripts that marked correct work as wrong [@xlang2025introducing]. It now re-runs submitted agents itself under uniform settings [@osworld2026verified]. Scores before and after the revision are therefore not strictly comparable, which Table 5.1 makes visible.

The 2026 AI Index, using data compiled by Epoch AI, reported the 66.3% figure and observed that agents "still fail roughly one in three attempts on structured benchmarks" [@sajadieh2026ai]. On the revised benchmark the student baseline had in fact already been passed: in the official leaderboard's own re-runs, an agent program built on Claude Opus 4.5 scored 74.5% in an entry dated February 2026, and by August the best general-purpose model reached 86.0% [@osworld2026verified]. That baseline, however, was measured once, on the original tasks, by students rather than experienced office workers, and says nothing about work that has not been packaged as a test. Its successor, OSWorld 2.0, described in a June 2026 preprint, consists of 108 long workflows that take people a median of about 1.6 hours each. The best agent tested completed about 21% of them, and earned 54.8% of the available credit when partly finished work was scored [@yuan2026osworld]. OpenAI reported 72.6% for GPT-6 Astra on an offline subset of OSWorld 2.0 scored with partial credit [@openai2026gpt6]; the comparable figure for the paper's best agent is that 54.8%, on the full task set.

### Coding agents

Software engineering has the most developed agent benchmarks, partly because a project's own tests can check an answer. **SWE-bench**, published by Jimenez and colleagues, collects 2,294 real problems from the issue trackers of 12 popular Python projects on GitHub. An agent receives the code and the text of the issue and must produce a change (a "patch") that makes hidden tests pass. When the benchmark appeared in 2023, the best model, Claude 2, resolved 1.96% [@jimenez2024swebench].

Some problems turned out to be unfair, because the description was too vague or the tests rejected valid solutions. In August 2024 OpenAI and the benchmark's authors released **SWE-bench Verified**, 500 problems selected after professional developers had screened 1,699 candidates, and reported that GPT-4o resolved 33.2% of them [@openai2024introducing]. By February 2026, with every model run inside the same minimal agent program, the leading models clustered in the low-to-mid 70s and the top score was about 76.8% [@sajadieh2026ai].

In February 2026 OpenAI announced that it would stop reporting SWE-bench Verified results. According to the company, an audit of 138 problems that its o3 model had not solved consistently across 64 runs found that 59.4% had material flaws in their tests or descriptions. Newer models from three developers (GPT-5.2, Claude Opus 4.5 and Gemini 3 Flash) could also reproduce some of the original human fixes or problem details from memory, a sign that the public solutions had entered training data [@openai2026why]. The sample was chosen because it was hard, so 59.4% does not describe the benchmark as a whole. The episode illustrates two problems that Chapter 4 discusses in general, saturation and contamination.

A successor illustrates how much the number depends on the test. **SWE-bench Pro**, described in a 2025 preprint, contains 1,865 problems from 41 actively maintained repositories, split into a public set, a held-out set and a commercial set drawn from 18 private codebases of start-ups. Its tasks may take a professional engineer hours to days. The paper's main results use one agent program (SWE-Agent), at most 50 turns, and problem statements supplemented with written requirements and interface details. In that setting the best model, Claude Sonnet 4.5, resolved 43.6% of the public problems. On the commercial set, reported in a separate table, the best score was 17.8% (Claude Opus 4.1). In an earlier run capped at $2 per task, GPT-5 at its high reasoning setting resolved 25.9% of the public set [@deng2025swebench]. On Scale AI's public leaderboard, mostly run in a minimal agent program, the top entry in October 2026 resolved 61.5% [@scale2026swebench]. Table 5.2 summarises the three versions.

A different yardstick asks how long a task an agent can finish. The non-profit METR's "50% time horizon" is the length of software task, timed on skilled humans, that an agent completes half the time [@kwa2025measuring]. On METR's data it doubled roughly every four months from 2023 to early 2026 (129 days, with a 95% confidence interval of 104 to 158). Claude Opus 4.6 (February 2026) measured about 12 hours, with an interval of roughly 5 to 61 hours. The last model METR measured before it stopped updating the series in September 2026, an early version of Claude Mythos Preview (April 2026), reached about 17 hours, with an interval of roughly 8 to 55 hours [@metr2026task]. A 12-hour task is of the order of a skilled engineer's day and a half of work on a self-contained software problem with a clear test of success. Chapter 4 (Section 4.7) explains the method and its caveats, among them that "time horizon is not the length of time AIs can work independently" [@kwa2026clarifying]; a 50% success rate is also far from the reliability most employers expect.

### Agents in use

Evidence on how agents were actually used in 2025–26 is thinner than the benchmark record, and most of it comes from the companies that sell them. Coding was the clearest case. Anthropic's own usage report of September 2025 found that "77% of API transcripts show automation patterns", meaning that businesses calling its models through software mostly delegated whole tasks rather than working through them in conversation, and that a little under half of that traffic concerned computing and mathematical tasks [@anthropic2025economic]. Independent data come from public code repositories. A dataset compiled by academic researchers and described in a 2025 preprint holds more than 456,000 pull requests (proposed code changes) opened by five coding agents, among them OpenAI Codex, GitHub Copilot and Claude Code, across 61,000 repositories by mid-2025 [@li2025rise]. Using it, another preprint, from 2026, compared projects before and after they adopted an agent with matched projects that did not. Output rose sharply at first, but mainly in projects that had not already used AI coding assistants, while static-analysis warnings and code complexity rose by about 18% and 39% and stayed higher [@agarwal2026ai]. Beyond coding, the positive evidence for agents at work is mostly vendor-reported, from case studies and usage figures that outsiders cannot audit; the critical evidence in this chapter, by contrast, is mostly academic.

Laboratory benchmarks and workplace outcomes can diverge. In a randomised trial run by METR in early 2025 and reported as a preprint, 16 experienced open-source developers completed 246 real tasks in their own projects. When allowed to use AI tools they took 19% longer, although they had expected to be about 24% faster [@becker2025measuring]. One small study of early-2025 tools is not decisive, and Chapter 8 places it among the wider evidence on productivity.

Kapoor and colleagues argue that the field's evaluation habits overstate progress [@kapoor2025ai]. Many leaderboards rank agents by accuracy alone, so complex and expensive agents top tables that much cheaper designs nearly match. The SWE-bench Pro results show how much a score depends on turn limits, cost caps and the agent program. Many benchmarks have weak or missing hold-out sets (tasks kept secret from developers), which lets agents overfit, and inconsistent evaluation practices make results hard to reproduce. Benchmarks also tend to use cooperative or simulated users. In Project Vend, the failures that mattered most came from real people who asked for discounts, invented stories or claimed that a leadership election had taken place [@anthropic2025projectphase].

## 5.5 Reliability: the mathematics of many steps and many tries

### Compounding error

A chatbot that is right 95% of the time is useful, because a person reads each answer. An agent that must take 20 steps in a row, each 95% reliable, is a different matter. If every step must succeed and steps fail independently, the probability that the whole task succeeds is the product of the step probabilities [@goodfellow2016deep], *p*^*n*^ (the per-step success rate *p* multiplied by itself once for each of the *n* steps), here 0.95^20^ ≈ 0.36. This is **compounding error**, plotted in Figure 5.3. In the author's calculation, over 50 steps, a per-step rate of 99% still leaves about a 60% chance of finishing, while 95% leaves about 8%. At 90% per step the chance falls below 1%.

![Figure 5.3: How per-step reliability compounds over a multi-step task. Each curve shows the probability that every step succeeds, for per-step success rates of 99, 95 and 90%, assuming steps fail independently and every step must succeed. Simple arithmetic, not measured data; real agents can recover from some errors, and their errors are rarely independent. Computed and drawn by the author using the product rule for independent events [@goodfellow2016deep].](figures/fig-05-03.svg){#fig:05-03}

Real agents depart from this model in both directions. They can notice an error and try again, which is why the observe step of the ReAct loop helps [@yao2023react]. A calculation by the author shows that the effect of recovery is large. If every failed step is detected and retried once, with an independent second chance, a step's success rate rises from 0.95 to 1 − 0.05^2^ = 0.9975. Twenty such steps then succeed 0.9975^20^ ≈ 95% of the time instead of 36%. The calculation assumes that failures are detected, which is exactly what is hard when there is no test to run. Errors can also be correlated, as when an agent misreads a whole class of web page the same way. An early wrong step can also send all later steps in the wrong direction. The curve is best read as an argument for short tasks, checkpoints that can detect failure, and measuring end-to-end success directly.

### pass\@*k* and pass^*k*^

**pass\@*k*** and **pass^*k*^** have similar names and answer opposite questions. **pass\@*k*** asks whether *at least one* of *k* attempts succeeds. Chen and colleagues at OpenAI, in a 2021 preprint, introduced it for code generation, where a test suite can pick out the working attempt. Their Codex model solved 28.8% of the HumanEval problems with a single sample, but 70.2% when 100 samples per problem were drawn and any working one counted [@chen2021evaluating]. **pass^*k*^** ("pass hat *k*"), introduced by Yao and colleagues for τ-bench, asks whether *all k* attempts succeed, which is the relevant question for an agent that will handle the same kind of request unsupervised every day [@yao2025taubench].

If an agent solves a given task with probability *p* and its attempts are independent, pass\@*k* = 1 − (1 − *p*)^*k*^ and pass^*k*^ = *p*^*k*^. The same *p* gives very different pictures. For *p* = 0.7 and *k* = 8, pass\@8 ≈ 0.99993 while pass^8^ ≈ 0.06. In practice *p* is unknown and is estimated from *n* trials of which *c* succeed. Both papers use estimators that avoid the bias of simply inserting *c*/*n* for *p*. They are written with binomial coefficients C(*a*, *b*), the number of ways of choosing *b* items from *a*:

pass\@*k* = 1 − C(*n* − *c*, *k*) / C(*n*, *k*) and pass^*k*^ = C(*c*, *k*) / C(*n*, *k*),

each averaged over the benchmark's tasks [@chen2021evaluating; @yao2025taubench]. A worked case: an agent attempts a task *n* = 8 times and succeeds *c* = 6 times. Its pass\@4 is 1 − C(2, 4)/C(8, 4) = 1, because any four attempts must include a success. Its pass^4^ is C(6, 4)/C(8, 4) = 15/70 ≈ 0.21: of the 70 possible sets of four attempts, only 15 consist entirely of successes. The naive calculation (6/8)^4^ ≈ 0.32 overstates it.

A benchmark's pass^*k*^ is an average over tasks, and tasks differ: some are easy for a given agent (*p* close to 1) and others hard (*p* close to 0). Because easy tasks keep contributing however large *k* becomes, the observed pass^*k*^ is never lower than the average single-attempt rate raised to the power *k* (a mathematical consequence of averaging, apart from sampling noise), and with mixed difficulty it is usually much higher. On τ²-bench's retail tasks, Claude Opus 4.5's pass^1^ of 0.80 would predict a pass^4^ of about 0.40 if every task were equally hard; the observed value is 0.52 [@sierra2026taubench]. The simple formula still makes the essential point. An agent that looks good on one try can look much worse when it must be right every time.

### Customer-service agents under test

τ-bench (tau-bench) simulates customer service. A language model plays a customer following instructions the agent cannot see. The agent must converse, call the company's tools (looking up orders, changing bookings) and follow a written policy. Success is judged by comparing the database at the end with the correct final state. The original 2024 benchmark had 115 retail and 50 airline tasks. GPT-4o, the strongest function-calling model tested, had a single-attempt success rate (pass^1^) of 61.2% on retail and 35.2% on airline tasks. In retail its pass^8^ was below 25% [@yao2025taubench].

Its 2025 successor, **τ²-bench**, published at ICML in 2026, added a telecoms domain with "dual control": the simulated customer must also take actions on their own device, as in technical support. The authors report "significant performance drops" when agents must guide a user instead of acting alone [@barres2025tau2]. In 2026 its maintainers at the company Sierra released a further extension, τ³-bench, which fixed more than 50 flawed retail and airline tasks and added a banking domain in which the agent must search a knowledge base of about 700 documents [@sierra2026taubench]. Averaged over the four domains, the leading models' first-attempt scores in early 2026 ranged from about 63 to 70%, according to the AI Index [@sajadieh2026ai]. Table 5.3 shows how much that average hides.

Benchmarks measure success rates; deployments reveal what a failure costs. ## 5.6 Security: prompt injection and its defences

Figure 5.2 showed that a tool's output re-enters the model as text in the same context as the user's instructions [@greshake2023not]. This creates a security problem that resembles an old one, the "confused deputy" discussed below, but lacks the clean separation of commands from data that fixed comparable flaws in conventional software. A language model processes everything in its context as one stream of tokens and has no reliable built-in way to tell which parts are instructions from its principal and which are merely data it was asked to read [@chismon2025prompt].

### A taxonomy

**Prompt injection** is the name for attacks that exploit this. In its *direct* form, the user types instructions that try to override the developer's rules. The more serious form for agents is **indirect prompt injection**, analysed by Greshake and colleagues in 2023. Here an attacker places instructions inside content an AI system is likely to process, and the model may treat them as commands. The authors argued that connecting models to retrieved content "blurs the line between data and instructions" and demonstrated the problem against systems then in use, including a search engine's GPT-4-based chat [@greshake2023not].

Greshake and colleagues sort attacks by how they arrive and what they aim at. Injections can be planted where retrieval will find them, such as a web page, sent to the target in an email the assistant will read, or pasted by a user who has been tricked. Their goals range from extracting private data and fraud to taking unauthorised actions through the system's own tools, spreading malware and manipulating what the user is shown [@greshake2023not].

Agents widen the attack surface beyond web pages and emails. A computer-use agent reads whatever is on screen, which is why OpenAI added a monitor for suspicious on-screen content [@openai2025computer]. Tool descriptions are themselves text that the model trusts, which is why the MCP specification tells clients to treat annotations from untrusted servers as untrusted [@modelcontextprotocol2026tools]. In multi-agent systems, a message from another agent is one more input of uncertain provenance.

Real products have shipped with such flaws. In June 2025 the US National Vulnerability Database published CVE-2025-32711, described as "AI command injection in M365 Copilot" that "allows an unauthorized attacker to disclose information over a network". Microsoft rated it 9.3 out of 10 ("critical") and NIST's own analysts 7.5 ("high"); both scoring vectors indicate that the attack required neither privileges nor any action by the user [@nist2025cve]. Microsoft stated that it had already fully mitigated the flaw on its servers, that users needed to take no action, and that it was not aware of exploitation [@microsoft2025cve].

### Why it resists a fix

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) has argued that prompt injection should not be thought of as similar to SQL injection, a familiar class of web vulnerability. SQL injection can be prevented by strictly separating commands from data, but current language models "do not enforce a security boundary between instructions and data inside a prompt". The NCSC therefore warned that "it's very possible that prompt injection attacks may never be totally mitigated in the way that SQL injection attacks can be". It described the model as an "inherently confusable deputy" (a classic security term for a program with privileges that can be tricked into using them for someone who lacks them) and advised designers to focus on reducing the likelihood and impact [@chismon2025prompt].

The independent developer Simon Willison has proposed a widely cited rule of thumb for when the danger is acute. His "lethal trifecta" is an agent that combines access to private data, exposure to untrusted content, and the ability to communicate externally [@willison2025lethal]. With all three, injected instructions can in principle cause private data to leave the system. Removing any one breaks the chain, which turns an unsolved machine-learning problem into a design decision. Willison also observes that a filter that catches 95% of attacks would be a failing grade in web security.

### Defences and their limits

One line of defence is *training the model* to resist. OpenAI researchers proposed an "instruction hierarchy" in which models learn to give priority to the developer's and user's instructions over text from tools and documents. In a preprint they reported substantially improved robustness, including against attack types not seen in training [@wallace2024instruction]. *Detection* uses separate classifiers to flag likely injections in inputs. Both approaches improve average-case behaviour but give no guarantees, and attackers who adapt can defeat them. A study by 14 researchers from several AI companies and universities, released in 2025 and published at the USENIX Security Symposium in 2026, tested 12 recent defences from these families (prompting techniques, training methods, detectors and methods based on secret checks) against adaptive attacks. Such attacks modify their strategy to counter each defence's design. They bypassed most defences with success rates above 90%, although the majority had originally reported attack success near zero. A human red-teaming competition with more than 500 participants and $20,000 in prizes succeeded in every scenario it covered. The authors conclude that "empirical evaluations cannot prove that a defense is robust" [@nasr2025attacker].

*System design* takes the opposite starting point. It accepts that the model can be fooled and limits what a fooled model can do. Beurer-Kellner and colleagues, from industry and academic security groups, describe in a 2025 preprint six design patterns (Table 5.4). Their central claim is that "securing general-purpose agents remains out of reach with current capabilities", but that "application-specific agents can be secured through principled system design" [@beurerkellner2025design].

| Pattern | Idea | Main cost |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Action selector | Model only maps a request to a fixed menu of actions; results never return to it | Loses most of the model's flexibility; every command must be predefined |
| Plan then execute | Actions fixed before untrusted data is read | Hard when later steps depend on earlier results; injected data can still alter tool inputs, such as an email's text |
| Map–reduce | Each untrusted item processed by an isolated model call with constrained output | Some tasks cannot be decomposed this strictly |
| Dual LLM | A privileged model with tools never sees untrusted text; a quarantined model without tools reads it | Quarantined model's output must be tightly constrained; some tasks cannot be split this way |
| Code then execute | Model writes a program that calls tools and other models, then runs it over the data | As with plan then execute, injected data can still alter the content passed between steps |
| Context minimisation | Remove the user's prompt from context once it has been used | Addresses only injections in the user's own prompt |

: Table 5.4: Design patterns for agents that resist prompt injection, with the trade-offs their authors identify. Source: [@beurerkellner2025design]. {#tbl:05-04}

Some designs go further and offer formal guarantees, measured on **AgentDojo**, a benchmark from ETH Zurich. It provides 97 realistic tasks (managing email, online banking, travel booking) and 629 security test cases; its authors found that leading models failed many tasks even without attack [@debenedetti2024agentdojo]. A follow-up system, **CaMeL**, first posted as a preprint in 2025 and published in 2026 at the IEEE Conference on Secure and Trustworthy Machine Learning (SaTML), is the instance of the code-then-execute pattern that Beurer-Kellner and colleagues cite [@beurerkellner2025design]. It adds a layer that tracks where every piece of data came from and what it may be used for. The plan is derived from the trusted user request before any untrusted data is read, so retrieved content cannot change which actions are taken. A policy check blocks data from flowing to places it should not. On AgentDojo, CaMeL completed 77% of tasks with provable security, against 84% for an undefended system, so protection cost seven points of usefulness. The guarantees cover attacks that try to change the agent's actions or the flow of data, such as sending private files to an outsider. The authors state that CaMeL "cannot defend against text-to-text attacks", such as an injected instruction that makes the agent summarise an email misleadingly. They also describe side channels through which small amounts of information could still leak [@debenedetti2025defeating].

Human confirmation, the default safeguard in MCP and in commercial computer-use agents, sits alongside all of these. It works when the person reads the request; Beurer-Kellner and colleagues warn of "tired or overloaded human verifiers approving unsafe actions" [@beurerkellner2025design].

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Can prompt injection be solved inside the model?**

*Mainly outside the model.* The NCSC holds that because models do not separate instructions from data, prompt injection may never be mitigated as completely as SQL injection, and that systems should limit what a manipulated model can do [@chismon2025prompt]. Adaptive attacks broke all twelve jailbreak and prompt-injection defences tested, most at success rates above 90%, although most had reported near-zero rates [@nasr2025attacker]. Architectural designs such as CaMeL give guarantees, for defined classes of attack, that do not depend on the model behaving well [@debenedetti2025defeating].

*Increasingly inside the model.* Training on an instruction hierarchy improved robustness, including against unseen attack types [@wallace2024instruction], and AgentDojo shows attack success varying widely between models, so model choice matters [@debenedetti2024agentdojo]. Architectural defences have real costs: CaMeL completed fewer tasks, its guarantees exclude text-only manipulation [@debenedetti2025defeating], and the most secure patterns give up much of what makes a general-purpose assistant useful [@beurerkellner2025design].

In the author's view, what would move the argument is a kind of test that has rarely been run: independent evaluations on realistic tasks in which well-resourced attackers adapt to each defence, reporting both attack success and lost usefulness. A model-level defence that held up under such testing would strengthen the second position; continued breaks would favour the first.
:::

## 5.7 Agents that pay

If agents are to book travel or buy supplies, they need a way to pay. Card rules assume either a cardholder present at checkout or a merchant charging under a standing agreement, as with subscriptions; neither fits a third-party agent deciding what to buy. Google's announcement of its **Agent Payments Protocol (AP2)** in September 2025 named the problems this creates once software acts for a person. *Authorisation* means "proving that a user gave an agent the specific authority to make a particular purchase". *Authenticity* means assuring the merchant that the request reflects the user's real intent, and *accountability* means deciding who is responsible if a transaction is fraudulent or wrong [@parikh2025powering].

AP2's answer is a chain of "mandates", which the company describes as "tamper-proof, cryptographically-signed digital contracts" signed with verifiable credentials (digitally signed attestations that a third party can check). An *Intent Mandate* records what the user asked for, such as "find me new white running shoes", and a *Cart Mandate*, signed when the user approves a specific cart, records the exact items and price. For a purchase without the user present, such as concert tickets bought the moment they go on sale, a detailed Intent Mandate signed in advance, with price limits and other conditions, lets the agent generate the Cart Mandate itself. Google argues that the chain provides "a non-repudiable audit trail", a record that no party can plausibly deny later. More than 60 organisations backed AP2 at launch, including card networks and payment firms [@parikh2025powering].

The protocol's current documentation describes AP2 as an extension of A2A and MCP and restructures the scheme. A *Checkout Mandate* is shared with the merchant, and a *Payment Mandate* with the credential provider, the card networks and the merchant's payment processor. Each exists in an "open" form, which sets constraints such as a budget, and a "closed" form, which records a specific, finalised transaction [@ap22026agent]. In April 2026 Google handed AP2 to the FIDO Alliance, the industry body behind passkeys, which is building agent-payment standards on it and on Mastercard's related Verifiable Intent framework [@fidoalliance2026google; @fidoalliance2026fido].

A second proposal works at the level of the web itself. The HTTP protocol that underlies web pages has long reserved a status code, 402 "Payment Required", that was rarely used. In May 2025 the cryptocurrency company Coinbase released **x402**, an open protocol that puts this code to work, initially for payments in stablecoins (digital tokens pegged to a currency such as the US dollar) [@dioquino2025coinbase; @coinbase2025x402]. In the current specification, a server that wants payment answers a request with status 402 and a header listing acceptable payment options. The client, which may be an agent, chooses one, signs a payment and repeats the request with the signed payment in another header. The server, or a third-party "facilitator", verifies and settles the payment and returns the resource. The design principle is "trust minimizing": schemes "must not allow for the facilitator or resource server to move funds" beyond what the client authorised [@x4022026x402]. Because the whole exchange fits inside an ordinary web request, the design suits small payments per request; the specification's own example is paying $1 to read an article. In April 2026 the protocol moved to an x402 Foundation under the Linux Foundation; its 40 launch members included card networks, cloud providers and payment companies [@coinbase2026x402].

These protocols are young, and in the author's assessment public evidence on how much real commerce passes through them is thin and comes mostly from vendors. What the protocols provide is a record of what the human authorised, in a form that can be checked later, the kind of evidence a dispute would turn on [@parikh2025powering; @x4022026x402]. They leave open who bears the loss when an agent acting within its mandate makes a bad purchase, the accountability problem Google's announcement names, and in the author's view a question for contract law and payment regulation [@parikh2025powering].

## 5.8 Many agents: multi-agent systems and agent economies

### Teams of agents

Many deployed systems combine several agents with different roles. Anthropic described its research feature as an orchestrator–worker design: a lead agent (Claude Opus 4) plans the research and spawns sub-agents (Claude Sonnet 4) that search in parallel. According to the company, this outperformed a single Opus 4 agent by 90.2% on its internal research evaluation. The cost was tokens. Agents used about four times as many tokens as chat interactions and the multi-agent system about fifteen times as many. On the BrowseComp search benchmark token usage alone explained about 80% of the variance in performance. The company noted that most coding tasks offer fewer truly parallel sub-tasks than research does [@anthropic2025how].

Independent analysis is less encouraging about multi-agent designs in general. Cemri and colleagues started from the observation that such systems often show only small gains over single agents on standard benchmarks. They built a taxonomy of 14 failure modes in three categories from 150 traces analysed by human experts, then used a language model as judge to label more than 1,600 execution traces from seven popular multi-agent frameworks. System design issues include the most frequent mode in the model-labelled data, step repetition (15.7% of failures), and agents unaware of when to stop. Misalignment between agents covers cases such as a mismatch between an agent's stated reasoning and its action, or an agent ignoring another's input. The third category is weak or incorrect verification of the final result. Three human annotators reached close agreement on the categories in a calibration set of 15 traces (Cohen's kappa, a measure of agreement beyond chance, 0.88), and the model judge agreed reasonably well with human labels (kappa 0.77). The failures are recognisable patterns, although their frequencies are machine-judged [@cemri2025why]. Project Vend's second phase showed the same pattern at small scale. Adding a manager agent changed outcomes, but the manager shared the worker's weakness for saying yes [@anthropic2025projectphase].

### Risks when agents meet

A different problem arises when agents with different owners interact, for example in a marketplace. The Cooperative AI Foundation's report *Multi-Agent Risks from Advanced AI*, led by Lewis Hammond with 44 authors, offers one of the most systematic maps so far. It classifies failures by the agents' incentives into **miscoordination** (agents with compatible goals fail to work together), **conflict** (agents with opposed goals harm each other or bystanders) and **collusion** (agents cooperate in ways their owners or society did not intend). It lists seven risk factors that make them more likely, among them information asymmetries, network effects and commitment problems [@hammond2025multi]. Because large populations of capable LLM agents interacting in the wild are still rare, the report is a structured map of mechanisms, not a measurement of current harm.

Evidence on collusion comes from older pricing algorithms. In simulations, Calvano and colleagues found that simple reinforcement-learning pricing algorithms competing in a standard market model "consistently learn to charge supracompetitive prices, without communicating with one another". The algorithms sustained high prices with strategies that punish undercutting and then return to cooperation [@calvano2020artificial]. In Germany's retail petrol market, where pricing software became widely available in 2017, Assad and colleagues found that adoption raised margins only for stations facing competitors. In markets with two or three stations, margins rose only when all of them adopted it, consistent with the algorithms learning to avoid price wars, that is, with tacit collusion [@assad2024algorithmic]. Neither study involves language-model agents, but both show that coordination can emerge between algorithms without being designed or communicated.

### Agent economies

Economists have begun to ask what markets will look like if many transactions are carried out by software. Hadfield and Koh, in a chapter of the National Bureau of Economic Research volume *The Economics of Transformative AI* (quoted here from the preprint version), argue that agents able to carry out long tasks with little oversight may be widely deployed within a decade. They raise the possibility that "the errors introduced by AI agents might be more correlated than those of humans", so that mistakes would not cancel out across a market. They point to the 2010 "flash crash" in US stock markets, which automated trading is thought to have worsened, as an early warning. The identity and registration systems that tell people whom they are dealing with "are currently missing for AI agents", they note, and new digital institutions will be needed [@hadfield2025economy].

Tomašev and colleagues at Google DeepMind propose, in a preprint, the frame of "sandbox economies". They classify agent economies by whether they emerge spontaneously or are deliberately designed, and by how *permeable* they are: how freely value and effects pass between the agent economy and the human one. They call permeability "the critical and controllable design variable" and argue that current trends point towards a large, unplanned and highly permeable agent economy. Their proposals include auctions to allocate resources fairly, partial insulation between high-speed agent trading and the rest of the economy, and identity infrastructure such as cryptographically signed credentials [@tomasev2025virtual].

The payment mandates of Section 5.7 and the Agent Cards of Section 5.3 are early pieces of such infrastructure. Chan and colleagues examine three further measures for making agents visible: **agent identifiers** that show when an agent is involved in an interaction, **real-time monitoring** of agent activity, and **activity logs** that can be reviewed afterwards. Each can be implemented more or less intrusively, and the authors weigh the gains in accountability against costs to privacy and the risk of concentrating power in whoever runs the monitoring [@chan2024visibility].

In the author's assessment, evidence on agent-to-agent commerce still consists mostly of experiments, protocols and forecasts [@hadfield2025economy; @tomasev2025virtual], so this literature names the design choices (identity, permeability, speed, record-keeping) before large-scale deployment has fixed them. Chapter 11 places correlated failure within the wider taxonomy of AI risks, and Chapter 14 returns to evaluating whole populations of agents.

Governance of agents is at an early stage. The EU AI Act does not regulate agents as such; its duties attach to providers of general-purpose AI models, which have applied since August 2025, and to systems used for listed high-risk purposes, whether or not they act autonomously (Chapter 13) [@europeanparliament2024regulation]. In February 2026 the US standards body NIST launched an AI Agent Standards Initiative to support industry-led standards and open protocols for agents and research on agent security and identity [@nist2026announcing]. Who bears the loss when an agent errs remains, as Section 5.7 noted, a question for existing contract and payment law.

An agent's usefulness and its main weaknesses have the same source: software, not a person, now closes the loop between what a model writes and what happens in the world. Errors compound over steps, reliability across repeated attempts falls well below single-try success, and any text the loop feeds back, from a web page to another agent's message, can redirect the model. The safeguards that do not depend on the model behaving well sit outside it: narrow permissions, plans fixed before untrusted data arrive and checks that catch failure, joined for payments by signed records of what a human authorised. Project Vend's second phase showed that better tools cut a shopkeeper's losses without giving it better judgement [@anthropic2025projectphase]. The open question for the next few years is whether such outside safeguards can be made cheap enough that agents are trusted with tasks nobody checks, or whether checking remains the main cost of using them.
