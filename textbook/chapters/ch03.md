# Chapter 3: How Large Language Models Work

In May 2020 a team at OpenAI described GPT-3, a neural network with 175 billion parameters [@brown2020language]. It had been trained on one narrow task: guessing the next piece of text in a very large collection of web pages, books and Wikipedia articles. Nobody had trained it to translate, answer questions or do sums. Yet when the researchers typed a few worked examples into the model, it carried on the pattern and performed tasks such as translation, question answering and three-digit arithmetic. The paper stressed that it did this "without any gradient updates or fine-tuning", meaning that the network's weights were not changed for the new task.

This ability to pick up a task from examples in the prompt is called **in-context learning**. It arises from the same mechanism that lets such a system write essays and explain code, and that makes it sometimes invent facts with complete confidence, although how the mechanism produces in-context learning is only partly understood (Section 3.3) [@brown2020language; @olsson2022context]. Text is cut into tokens, tokens become vectors, attention mixes information between positions, a stack of transformer blocks refines the result, and a probability distribution over the next token comes out at the top. Wherever possible the numbers below come from real, openly available models and software, with the settings stated. Chapter 2 supplies the background on loss functions, backpropagation, residual connections and layer normalisation; Chapter 4 covers scaling laws and compute. Section 3.8 traces how the models changed between ChatGPT in 2022 and the frontier of 2026.

## 3.1 Tokens: from text to numbers

A neural network computes with numbers, so text must first be turned into numbers. Feeding in single characters would make sequences very long, so language models instead split text into **tokens**, frequently occurring chunks of characters, each with a fixed identification number. A token may be a whole common word, a fragment of a longer word, or a single character, byte or punctuation mark. The list of all tokens a model knows is its **vocabulary**, and the program that does the splitting is its **tokeniser** [@sennrich2016neural; @radford2019language].

Table 3.1 shows a real example. The sentence "Students in Edinburgh rarely misunderstand tokenisation." was passed through `o200k_base`, the tokeniser that OpenAI's open-source `tiktoken` library assigns to GPT-4o and to later models including o3, GPT-4.1 and GPT-5 [@openai2026tiktoken]. Six words become eight tokens. Common words such as "Students" and "rarely" survive whole, and even "misunderstand" has its own token. The less common "tokenisation", with its British spelling, is split into `" token"` and `"isation"`. Most tokens carry their leading space with them, so `" in"` (with a space) and `"in"` (without) are different tokens with different numbers.

### Byte-pair encoding, step by step

Many current tokenisers, including OpenAI's and Llama 3's, build their vocabulary with **byte-pair encoding** (BPE) [@openai2026tiktoken; @grattafiori2024llama]. The method began as a data-compression technique that repeatedly replaced the most frequent pair of bytes with a new, unused byte; @sennrich2016neural adapted it for machine translation in 2016. Their version starts with a vocabulary of single characters and represents every word in a training text as a sequence of characters plus an end-of-word marker. It then counts every adjacent pair of symbols, weighted by how often each word occurs, merges the most frequent pair into a new symbol, and repeats. The number of merges is the only setting, and the final vocabulary is the starting characters plus one new symbol per merge.

The paper prints a short Python implementation with a toy dictionary: "low" occurring 5 times, "lower" 2, "newest" 6 and "widest" 3. Running that code gives the merges in Table 3.2. The pair "e s" appears 9 times (6 in "newest", 3 in "widest") and is merged first; "es t" and "est" plus the end marker follow, also at 9. After ten merges, "newest" and "low" are single symbols, while the rarer "lower" is still spelled "low", "e", "r". That is the essential behaviour of BPE: frequent strings become single tokens, rare ones remain decomposable into known parts, and no word made of characters seen in training is ever "unknown" [@sennrich2016neural].

In their English–German experiments Sennrich and colleagues used 59,500 merges for each language, or 89,500 for a single vocabulary shared by both [@sennrich2016neural]. GPT-2 introduced a byte-level variant in which the starting symbols are the 256 possible bytes rather than the characters of an alphabet. Because any text in any script is a sequence of bytes, the GPT-2 authors wrote, their model "can assign a probability to any Unicode string" [@radford2019language]. OpenAI's `tiktoken` documentation likewise says that byte-level BPE works "on arbitrary text, even text that is not in the tokeniser's training data" [@openai2026tiktoken]. An emoji that never appeared in training is spelled out in byte-level pieces. The parrot emoji, four bytes in the standard UTF-8 encoding, becomes three tokens in both the GPT-2 and `o200k_base` tokenisers.

Vocabulary size is a trade-off. A larger vocabulary makes text shorter in tokens but enlarges the embedding table and spreads training data over more rare tokens. ### What tokenisation costs

With a leading space, `" strawberry"` is a single token in `o200k_base`. Without one, `"strawberry"` becomes `"st"`, `"raw"` and `"berry"`. Either way the model never sees ten separate letters, which in the author's judgement is one reason questions about spelling and letter counts are harder for it than they look. Numbers are cut into chunks of up to three digits: `" 12345678"` becomes a space token followed by `"123"`, `"456"` and `"78"`. The digit that carries a given place value lands in different positions depending on the length of the number [@openai2026tiktoken].

Tokenisation also treats languages unequally. Table 3.4 shows the sentence "I love learning about language models." and close translations. In `o200k_base` the English version needs 7 tokens and the Hindi one 12. In the older GPT-2 tokeniser the Hindi version needs 62. Devanagari characters each take three bytes in UTF-8, and the count suggests that vocabulary has few merges for them. Across a parallel corpus in many languages, @petrov2023language found that the same text could differ in tokenised length by up to 15 times. Since services charge per token and context windows are counted in tokens, speakers of under-represented languages pay more and fit less into each request. The newer vocabulary narrows the gap considerably but does not close it.

::: {.box .concept}
**Concept: Special tokens and chat templates**

Besides pieces of text, every vocabulary contains a few **special tokens** that never occur in ordinary writing, such as markers for the end of a text. Chat models use them to turn a conversation into the single sequence of tokens that the model continues. A **chat template** writes each message with markers for who is speaking. Qwen models, for example, open each turn with `<|im_start|>` followed by "user" or "assistant" and close it with `<|im_end|>`, and the reply is generated after an opening marker for the assistant [@huggingface2026chat]. System instructions, the user's messages and the model's earlier answers are thus all earlier tokens in one context, and prices and context limits count them all.
:::

## 3.2 Embeddings and self-attention

A token number on its own carries no meaning. The ID 9059 (`" cat"` in `o200k_base`) says nothing about cats, and token 9060 is not a related animal [@openai2026tiktoken]. So each token is converted into an **embedding**: a **vector**, that is, an ordered list of numbers, which places the token as a point in a high-dimensional space. The model holds an embedding table with one row per vocabulary entry and simply looks up the row for each token. In the smallest GPT-2 model each row has 768 numbers; in the largest Llama 3 model it has 16,384 [@radford2019language; @grattafiori2024llama]. The numbers start random and are adjusted by gradient descent during training, like any other weight [@goodfellow2016deep].

The word2vec models, trained on 1.6 billion words and described in a preprint by @mikolov2013efficient, placed words used in similar contexts near one another. Directions in the space also captured relationships. @mikolov2013linguistic reported that the calculation "King − Man + Woman" produced a vector very close to the one for "Queen". Figure 3.1 sketches the idea in two dimensions.

![Figure 3.1: An embedding space, sketched in two dimensions. Words used in similar ways sit close together, and some relationships (such as male to female, or country to capital) appear as roughly parallel arrows. Real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of dimensions [@radford2019language; @grattafiori2024llama]. Schematic by the author, not to scale [@mikolov2013linguistic; @mikolov2013efficient].](figures/fig-03-01.svg){#fig:03-01}

The picture misleads in one important respect. Word2vec gave each word a single fixed point, so "bank" had the same embedding whether it meant a riverbank or a lender [@mikolov2013efficient]. In a language model the embedding table is only the starting point. As a token passes up through the network, its vector is repeatedly updated with information from the surrounding tokens, by the attention mechanism described next [@vaswani2017attention; @elhage2021mathematical]. So "bank" in "the river bank" and "bank" in "the bank raised rates" can therefore end up represented quite differently.

Embeddings need not come from words. @dosovitskiy2021image showed that an image can be cut into small square patches, each turned into a vector and processed like a token, and @radford2021learning trained CLIP to place pictures and their captions in a shared embedding space. Multimodal models such as Google's Gemini 1.5 put images, audio and video into the same sequence as text [@geminiteam2024gemini] (Section 3.4).

### Queries, keys and values

In "The Law will never be perfect, but its application should be just", the word "its" refers back to "Law", several words earlier. Before 2017 most language models were recurrent networks that passed a running summary from word to word, so information from far back faded and each step waited for the previous one. Attention itself was not new in 2017. @bahdanau2015neural had added it to a recurrent translation system, letting each output word look back over all the input words. The **transformer** of @vaswani2017attention went further. It dispensed "with recurrence and convolutions entirely" and relied on **self-attention**, in which every position can draw directly on every other.

Attention works with three vectors per token, each computed by multiplying the token's current vector by a learned weight matrix. The **query** *q* expresses what the token is looking for; the **key** *k* advertises what a token contains; the **value** *v* is the information it passes on if selected. How well a query matches a key is measured by their **dot product**: multiply the two vectors number by number and add up the results, which gives a large number when both have large values of the same sign in the same places [@vaswani2017attention; @goodfellow2016deep]. For all tokens at once, the computation is

Attention(*Q*, *K*, *V*) = softmax(*QK*^T^ / √*d*~k~) *V*

where *Q*, *K* and *V* stack the queries, keys and values of all tokens as rows, *QK*^T^ is the table of every query–key dot product, and *d*~k~ is the length of the key vectors [@vaswani2017attention]. The **softmax** function (Section 2.3) turns each row of scores into weights that are positive and sum to one, by exponentiating every score and dividing by the total. Each token's output is then the weighted average of all the value vectors. The division by √*d*~k~ keeps the scores in a workable range. If the numbers in queries and keys are random with variance 1, the variance of their dot product grows in proportion to *d*~k~. The authors suspected that such large scores push the softmax towards giving almost all weight to one token, a region where, in their words, it has "extremely small gradients" and learning stalls.

A language model adds a **causal mask**. When predicting the next token it must not peek at the answer, so each token may attend only to itself and earlier tokens. The mask sets the scores for all later positions to minus infinity before the softmax, which makes their weights exactly zero [@vaswani2017attention]. The layers still run one after another, but within a layer no position has to wait for the previous position to finish. During training, therefore, all positions of a passage are processed at once on parallel hardware, which is why Vaswani and colleagues found the transformer "more parallelizable" and much quicker to train than recurrent networks [@vaswani2017attention].

The causal mask also separates the main kinds of transformer. The original transformer was built for translation and had two halves [@vaswani2017attention]. An **encoder** read the whole source sentence without a mask, so every word could attend to words on both sides of it. A **decoder** then wrote the translation one token at a time, with a causal mask, while also attending to the encoder's output. Later models kept one half. BERT, an encoder-only model from Google, sees text in both directions and is used to classify or search text rather than to write it [@devlin2019bert]. The GPT models and the other language models in this chapter, such as Llama 3, are **decoder-only**: a single masked stack that both reads the prompt and writes the continuation [@radford2018improving; @grattafiori2024llama]. The attention example in the inset of Figure 3.2 comes from the encoder of the original translation model, which is why it can look ahead.

The first token can see only itself, so its output is just its own value, which is the mask at work. In a real model the vectors are longer, and the matrices that produce queries, keys and values are learned, with no dimension's meaning specified by anyone [@vaswani2017attention].

### Many heads

A single attention computation produces one weighted mix per token. Transformers instead run several in parallel, called **attention heads**. Each head has its own, smaller query, key and value matrices, which project the token's whole vector into a short vector of its own, 64 numbers long in GPT-2 small. Multi-head attention, Vaswani and colleagues wrote, "allows the model to jointly attend to information from different representation subspaces at different positions". The original model had 8 heads of 64 dimensions each [@vaswani2017attention]. GPT-2 small, which has the same width as GPT-1, has 12 (12 × 64 = 768) [@radford2018improving; @radford2019language]. GPT-3 has 96 heads per layer and Llama 3 405B has 128 [@brown2020language; @grattafiori2024llama]. The heads' outputs are joined end to end, which restores the full width, and multiplied by one more learned matrix, the output projection.

Heads do specialise. In the original paper's trained translation model, two heads in the fifth of six layers attended sharply from "its" to "Law" and to "application" in the sentence above. The authors called this behaviour "apparently involved in anaphora resolution", that is, working out what a pronoun refers to [@vaswani2017attention, Figure 4]. @jain2019attention, however, found that attention weights were frequently uncorrelated with other measures of which inputs mattered for a prediction, and concluded that "standard attention modules do not provide meaningful explanations". @wiegreffe2019attention replied that this depends on how explanation is defined, and proposed tests under which attention weights can still be informative. The weights show where a head looks, not necessarily why the model predicts what it does.

## 3.3 The transformer block

Attention is one of two sublayers in a **transformer block**, and a language model is a stack of identical blocks: 12 in GPT-2 small, 96 in GPT-3, 126 in Llama 3 405B (Figure 3.2) [@radford2019language; @brown2020language; @grattafiori2024llama].

### The residual stream

Each sublayer does not replace a token's vector; it adds to it. This is the residual connection of Section 2.5, introduced for image networks [@he2016deep]. If *x* is a token's vector entering a block, GPT-2 computes *x* + Attention(LayerNorm(*x*)), and then adds the feed-forward sublayer's output to that sum in the same way. Layer normalisation rescales each vector to zero mean and unit variance before applying learned scale and shift parameters [@ba2016layer]. Many newer models, DeepSeek-V3 and Qwen2.5 among them, use a simpler variant, RMSNorm, which only rescales and does not subtract the mean [@zhang2019root; @deepseekai2024deepseekv3; @qwen2024qwen25]. GPT-2 applies layer normalisation to the input of each sublayer, moving it from the output position used in the original transformer, and adds one more after the final block [@radford2019language].

The result is a single vector per position that runs from the embedding at the bottom to the output layer at the top, with every attention and feed-forward sublayer reading from it and writing back into it. Anthropic researchers call it the **residual stream**, describing it as "a communication channel, since it doesn't do any processing itself and all layers communicate through it" [@elhage2021mathematical]. Information written into the stream by an early layer remains available to every later layer unless something overwrites it.

This shared channel lets attention heads in different layers work together, which offers a partial explanation of the in-context learning described at the start of the chapter. In a follow-up study, @olsson2022context identified **induction heads**: pairs of heads in which one, in an earlier layer, writes into the stream which token preceded each position, and the other uses that to find an earlier occurrence of the current token and copy what came next. Given "… Mr Dursley … Mr", such a circuit predicts "Dursley", completing the pattern [A][B] … [A] → [B]. The authors found that induction heads form at the same point in training as a sharp improvement in using earlier context, and offered "preliminary and indirect evidence" that they may account for most in-context learning. The evidence is causal for small attention-only models but only correlational for large ones, so a full account of how models learn tasks from examples remains open.

### The feed-forward network

The second sublayer is a small two-layer network of the kind described in Chapter 2, applied to each position separately. Attention moves information between tokens, and the feed-forward network processes what has arrived. In GPT-2 the vector is expanded from 768 to 3,072 numbers, passed through a smooth non-linear activation (GELU, a curved relative of the ReLU of Section 2.1 [@hendrycks2016gaussian]), and projected back to 768, a design taken over from GPT-1 [@radford2018improving; @radford2019language]. Llama 3 uses a gated variant called SwiGLU, with an inner width of 53,248 for a model dimension of 16,384 [@grattafiori2024llama]. These layers hold most of the parameters. @geva2021transformer showed that feed-forward layers behave like key–value memories. The first matrix detects input patterns, often interpretable ones such as a phrase ending in a particular word, and the second pushes the prediction towards tokens that tend to follow them.

Size translates directly into computation. A forward pass through the blocks costs about two arithmetic operations (a multiplication and an addition) per parameter for each token, roughly 170 million operations per token for GPT-2 small. The output layer adds more, as does attention over the context, which grows with its length [@kaplan2020scaling]. This 2*N* rule, for a model with *N* parameters, is the starting point for the compute accounting of Chapter 4.

### Position information

The attention operation by itself ignores order. A token's output is a weighted average over the tokens it can see, with weights set by their content alone, so shuffling those tokens leaves the output of one attention step unchanged and "dog bites man" would look like "man bites dog". A stack of causally masked layers is not entirely order-blind, because each token sees a different number of predecessors, and models trained with no position encoding at all still learn positional information and remain competitive [@haviv2022transformer]. Most models nevertheless supply position explicitly, by position encodings, which work better in practice; three schemes are described here. The original transformer added to each embedding a fixed pattern of sine and cosine waves. Each pair of dimensions oscillates with the position at its own frequency, with wavelengths forming "a geometric progression from 2π to 10000 · 2π" [@vaswani2017attention]. Nearby positions differ sharply in the fast-oscillating dimensions and hardly at all in the slow ones, like the hands of a clock. GPT-1 and GPT-2 instead learned a separate position embedding for each of their slots, 1,024 in GPT-2, exactly like token embeddings [@radford2018improving; @radford2019language]. A learned table has a hard limit, since GPT-2 has no row for position 1,025.

Many recent open models, among them Llama 3, Qwen2.5 and DeepSeek-V3 according to their developers' technical reports, use **rotary position embeddings** (RoPE), which add nothing to the embedding [@su2024roformer; @grattafiori2024llama; @qwen2024qwen25; @deepseekai2024deepseekv3]. Inside each attention head, RoPE splits the query and key vectors into pairs of numbers and rotates each pair by an angle proportional to the token's position, with a different rotation speed for each pair. When a rotated query meets a rotated key, the positions enter their dot product only through the difference between the two rotation angles, and so through the distance between the two tokens. In its authors' words, RoPE "encodes the absolute position with a rotation matrix" while making attention depend explicitly on relative position. As with the sinusoids, the rotation speeds form a geometric series. A constant called the base frequency sets how slowly the slowest pairs turn. A larger base keeps their angles from coming full circle over long distances, so far-apart positions remain distinguishable. The original RoPE design used a base of 10,000, as the sinusoidal scheme did [@su2024roformer; @vaswani2017attention]. Meta raised the constant to 500,000 for Llama 3 to support longer contexts [@grattafiori2024llama].

### Mixture of experts

In a standard ("dense") transformer every parameter is used for every token. A **mixture-of-experts** (MoE) model replaces the single feed-forward network in a block with many alternative ones, the experts, and a small routing network sends each token to only a few of them. @shazeer2017outrageously showed in 2017 that such layers, with up to thousands of experts, could raise model capacity by more than 1,000 times with only minor losses in computational efficiency. Mixtral 8x7B, described in a 2024 preprint, has 8 experts per layer and routes each token to 2, so that each token "has access to 47B parameters, but only uses 13B active parameters" [@jiang2024mixtral]. In DeepSeek-V3, according to its technical report, each token is routed to 8 of 256 experts per layer, plus one shared expert that every token uses, and uses 37 billion of the model's 671 billion parameters [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3].

The design separates how much a model can store (total parameters) from how much computation each token costs (active parameters). The price is memory, since all experts must be held somewhere, and the need to keep the experts evenly loaded. DeepSeek-V3 does this mainly by nudging each expert's routing score up or down according to its load [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3].

## 3.4 Generating text

At the top of the stack, the final vector at the last position is multiplied by the output matrix to give one raw score per vocabulary entry, a **logit**, and a softmax turns the logits into probabilities. The model then picks one token, appends it, and runs again. A model that generates text this way, feeding each output back in as input, is **autoregressive** [@vaswani2017attention]. Each token, once emitted, stays. A standard autoregressive model cannot delete what it has written, only add text that corrects it, which is what reasoning models learn to do (Section 3.7). Serving systems can speed this up without changing what the model would produce. In **speculative decoding** a small, fast model drafts several tokens and the large model checks them all in one parallel pass. Each draft token is accepted or replaced by a rule that leaves the output's probabilities exactly those of the large model alone, even when sampling; the method's inventors reported speed-ups of two to three times [@leviathan2023fast]. The draft can also come from the model itself. **Multi-token prediction** trains extra output heads to predict two or more tokens ahead. @gloeckle2024better found that this extra training objective improved code models and allowed inference up to three times faster, and DeepSeek-V3 uses its one extra predicted token as a draft that is accepted 85–90% of the time, for 1.8 times the decoding speed [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3].

Not every text generator works left to right. **Diffusion language models** start from a sequence of masked positions and fill them in over several rounds, predicting many tokens in parallel and revising the whole draft at each round, much as image diffusion models remove noise (Section 9.1). LLaDA, an 8-billion-parameter masked diffusion model trained from scratch, performed comparably to its developers' autoregressive baselines of similar size [@nie2025large]. Inception Labs reported that its commercial Mercury Coder models generate roughly 740 to 1,100 tokens per second on an Nvidia H100, as measured by the benchmarking firm Artificial Analysis [@khanna2025mercury].

Table 3.7 shows real next-token probabilities after "The capital of France is" from three openly available base models, which have only been pre-trained (Section 3.5). GPT-2 small gives `" Paris"` only 3.2% and prefers `" the"`. GPT-2 XL, with about 1.5 billion parameters, raises `" Paris"` to 8.6% [@radford2019language]. Qwen2.5-1.5B, released by Alibaba in 2024 with a similar number of parameters but trained, according to the company's technical report, on up to 18 trillion tokens [@qwen2024qwen25], gives `" Paris"` 27.3%. Even then, most of the probability goes to other continuations: `" located"`, or runs of underscores that hint at fill-in-the-gap exercises in the training data. For a base model, "The capital of France is located on the Seine" is a perfectly good continuation.

### Temperature, top-k and top-p

Turning probabilities into a choice, **decoding**, is a procedure separate from the network. Always taking the most likely token ("greedy" decoding), or searching for the most likely sequence as beam search does, leads to what @holtzman2020curious call degeneration: text that is "bland, incoherent, or gets stuck in repetitive loops". The alternative they study is to sample from the distribution, after reshaping it with temperature, top-k or top-p [@holtzman2020curious].

**Temperature** *T* divides every logit before the softmax: *p*~*i*~ = e^*z*~*i*~/*T*^ / Σ~*j*~ e^*z*~*j*~/*T*^ [@holtzman2020curious]. Because the softmax depends only on differences between logits, the ratio between two tokens' probabilities is e raised to their logit difference divided by *T*. GPT-2 XL's logits after "After the lecture, the students went to the" are 8.78 for `" cafeteria"` and 8.35 for `" library"`, a difference of 0.43 (settings as in Table 3.7) [@radford2019language]. At *T* = 1 the ratio of their probabilities is e^0.43^ ≈ 1.54; at *T* = 0.5 the difference doubles to 0.86 and the ratio becomes e^0.86^ ≈ 2.36. Lower temperatures sharpen the distribution towards the top token; higher ones flatten it, and as *T* approaches zero sampling becomes greedy decoding.

**Top-k sampling**, used by @fan2018hierarchical for story generation with *k* = 10, discards all but the *k* most likely tokens and renormalises. **Top-p** or **nucleus sampling** keeps the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to at least *p* [@holtzman2020curious]. Holtzman and colleagues proposed it because the shape of the distribution varies. When it is flat, a small *k* cuts off many reasonable options, and when it is peaked, a large *k* lets in implausible ones. GPT-2 XL shows how much the nucleus varies (same settings). At temperature 1, covering 90% of the probability takes 3,068 tokens after the open-ended lecture prompt but about a third as many after "The cat sat on the", and halving the temperature shrinks both to a handful (see the box below).

### The KV cache and the cost of inference

To produce token 501, the model needs the keys and values of all 500 earlier tokens at every layer. Recomputing them at every step would mean reprocessing the whole text for each new token, so a reply twice as long would take about four times the work. Serving systems therefore store them, in what is called the **key–value cache** or **KV cache**. Each new token then needs only its own query, key and value, plus attention over the stored keys and values. Generation thus has two phases: a prompt phase (often called "prefill") that processes the prompt in parallel and fills the cache, and a generation ("decode") phase that adds one token at a time [@kwon2023efficient].

The cache holds a key and a value vector per token for every layer, so its size multiplies the number of layers, the width of the stored vectors, the bytes per number and the length of the context (Table 3.8). @kwon2023efficient calculate up to 1.6 gigabytes for one 2,048-token request to the 13-billion-parameter OPT model; from Meta's published architecture, a full 128,000-token context in Llama 3 405B takes about 66 gigabytes, for a single user [@grattafiori2024llama].

The last row explains a design choice. In standard multi-head attention every head has its own keys and values; **grouped-query attention** lets groups of query heads share one key–value head [@ainslie2023gqa]. Llama 3's 128 query heads share 8 key–value heads, which Meta says it adopted "to improve inference speed and to reduce the size of key-value caches during decoding" [@grattafiori2024llama]. Without it the cache in Table 3.8 would be 16 times larger. Ainslie and colleagues found that it achieves quality close to full multi-head attention at nearly the speed of a single shared key–value head.

The cache also explains why decoding is slow relative to the hardware's arithmetic speed. Each step must read all the weights and the whole cache from memory to produce one token per request, which Ainslie and colleagues call "a severe bottleneck". Serving systems batch many users' requests so that each read of the weights is shared, which makes cache memory the limit on how many requests fit. Kwon and colleagues found that existing systems used only 20.4–38.2% of cache memory for actual token states. Their vLLM system manages the cache in small pages, like an operating system's virtual memory, and raised throughput two- to fourfold [@kwon2023efficient].

Serving also shrinks the numbers themselves. **Quantisation** stores weights in fewer bits than the 16 usually used in training. GPTQ reduced GPT-style models to 3 or 4 bits per weight with little loss of accuracy, enough to run a 175-billion-parameter model on a single GPU [@frantar2023gptq]. OpenAI post-trained its open-weight gpt-oss-120b with the expert weights in MXFP4, a 4-bit format costing about 4.25 bits per parameter, so that the 117-billion-parameter model fits on one 80 GB GPU [@openai2025gptoss], and Moonshot used quantisation-aware training to 4-bit integers for Kimi K2 Thinking, reporting a "lossless 2x speed-up" in its low-latency mode [@moonshot2025kimi]. Because decoding is limited by reading weights from memory, fewer bits per weight also mean faster generation (Section 7.1).

### Context windows

The maximum number of tokens a model can attend to at once is its **context window**. GPT-2's window was 1,024 tokens and GPT-3's 2,048 [@radford2019language; @brown2020language]; the largest Llama 3 model accepts 128,000 [@grattafiori2024llama]; DeepSeek-V4, released with open weights in April 2026 and described in a company preprint, accepts a million [@deepseekai2026deepseekv4]. In Google's research tests Gemini 1.5 retrieved planted facts with over 99% accuracy up to at least 10 million tokens [@geminiteam2024gemini].

Long windows are expensive in both computation and memory. Standard attention compares every token with every other, so its computation grows with the square of the length [@vaswani2017attention, Table 1]. A 128,000-token prompt implies about 1.6 × 10^10^ query–key pairs per head per layer, half of which the causal mask discards, and the KV cache grows linearly with length, as Table 3.8 shows. FlashAttention computes exact attention in tiles that fit in a processor's fast on-chip memory, avoiding repeated trips to slower main memory [@dao2022flashattention]. That saves time but not memory; the next subsection describes four further remedies used by models of 2024–26.

A larger window is not the same as using it well. @liu2024lost found that models answered best when the relevant information sat at the beginning or end of a long input and noticeably worse when it was buried in the middle. The same limit applies when a system retrieves documents and places them in the context (Section 3.6).

### Cheaper attention for long contexts

**Compressing the cache.** Grouped-query attention shares keys and values among heads. DeepSeek's **multi-head latent attention** (MLA), introduced with DeepSeek-V2 in 2024, compresses them instead: the keys and values of all heads are reconstructed from one short latent vector per token, and only that vector, plus a small key that carries the RoPE position, is cached. Against the company's earlier 67-billion-parameter dense model, DeepSeek-V2 cut the KV cache by 93.3% and raised maximum generation throughput 5.76 times [@deepseekai2024deepseekv2]. In DeepSeek-V3 the latent has 512 numbers and the position key 64, so a token costs about 70 KB of cache across its 61 layers, less than a seventh of Llama 3 405B's (Table 3.8) [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3]. Moonshot's Kimi K2 kept the design and halved the number of attention heads to 64 to cut long-context inference cost. Its training also shows a related hazard: attention scores can grow without limit and destabilise training, which Kimi K2's MuonClip optimiser prevents by rescaling the query and key weights [@kimiteam2025kimi].

**Looking only nearby.** In **sliding-window attention** a layer attends only to a fixed number of recent tokens; information from further back can still arrive indirectly, passed up through the layers. Mistral 7B used it to cut inference cost [@jiang2023mistral]. More often, local and global layers alternate. Google's Gemma 3 has five local layers, each looking back 1,024 tokens, for every layer with full attention; in the developers' ablation, interleaving local layers with such short windows cut the KV-cache memory overhead at a 32,000-token context from 60% to under 15% [@gemmateam2025gemma]. OpenAI's open-weight gpt-oss models alternate full attention with a window of 128 tokens [@openai2025gptoss], and Meta's Llama 4 interleaves attention layers that use no position embeddings at all [@meta2025llama4].

**Choosing what to attend to.** **Sparse attention** keeps the whole history but lets each query look at only a selection of it. DeepSeek Sparse Attention, introduced with DeepSeek-V3.2 in late 2025, adds a small, fast "lightning indexer" that scores every earlier token for the current one; full attention is then computed over only the 2,048 highest-scoring tokens, so the main attention cost grows with the length times 2,048 rather than with the square of the length [@deepseekai2025deepseekv32]. DeepSeek-V4 combines a compressed form of sparse attention with a more heavily compressed variant. At a million tokens, according to the developers, its larger model needs 27% of the per-token computation and 10% of the KV cache of DeepSeek-V3.2 [@deepseekai2026deepseekv4].

**Replacing attention in most layers.** A fourth line of work removes the growing cache altogether. Recurrent networks, set aside in 2017, carried a fixed-size state from token to token (Section 3.2). **State-space models** such as Mamba revive the idea with a state that the input itself controls, deciding what to keep and what to forget; Mamba's computation grows only linearly with length, and its developers measured generation throughput about five times that of transformers [@gu2023mamba]. **Linear-attention** layers such as Gated DeltaNet keep a fixed-size memory matrix that each new token edits, with a gate that lets old content fade [@yang2025gated]. A fixed-size state cannot hold everything, and such layers on their own have been weaker at retrieving a specific detail from far back, which is why Gated DeltaNet's authors also tested hybrids that mix in attention [@yang2025gated]. Production models do the same. AI21's Jamba interleaves transformer and Mamba layers with mixture-of-experts layers; its main configuration fits on one 80 GB GPU, and it showed strong results with contexts of up to 256,000 tokens [@lieber2024jamba]. Alibaba's Qwen3-Next (2025) uses three Gated DeltaNet layers for every attention layer and, according to its developers, gives ten times the inference throughput of the dense Qwen3-32B for contexts beyond 32,000 tokens [@qwen2025qwen3next]. Qwen3.5-397B-A17B keeps the pattern, with full attention in 15 of its 60 layers [@qwen2026qwen35].

### Beyond text: images, sound and video

Nothing in the mechanism described so far is specific to words: attention works on any sequence of vectors. By 2026 frontier models read images, audio and video as well as text. Google's Gemini 4 Argon, for example, accepts all four, plus PDF files, in a context of about a million tokens [@google2026gemini]. Three designs carry most of this.

**Attaching an encoder.** Many vision-language models connect a separately trained image encoder to a language model. LLaVA, in 2023, passed the output of a CLIP-style vision encoder (Section 3.2) through a small learned projection into the language model's embedding space, so that a picture arrives as a block of extra vectors in the prompt. It was trained on image-grounded instructions written by text-only GPT-4 [@liu2023visual]. The approach survives at the largest scale: Meta's Muse Spark Open pairs a 1.8-billion-parameter perception encoder with its language model [@meta2026musesparkopen].

**Early fusion.** In a natively multimodal model, images (and sometimes sound) are turned into tokens from the start and trained in one sequence with text, so that the same layers learn both. Meta's Chameleon showed in 2024 that a single token-based model trained this way could understand and generate images and text interleaved in any order [@chameleonteam2024chameleon]. Llama 4 used "early fusion to seamlessly integrate text and vision tokens into a unified model backbone" [@meta2025llama4], and Qwen3.5 was trained by early fusion on multimodal tokens [@qwen2026qwen35].

**Generating images and video.** Diffusion models (Section 9.1) long produced most AI images, with a language model at most writing their prompt. In March 2025 OpenAI described a new image generator in ChatGPT as "an autoregressive model natively embedded within ChatGPT", in contrast with its DALL·E models, which operate "as a diffusion model" [@openai2025addendum]. Video stayed with diffusion but gained sound. Google's Veo 3, announced in May 2025, generated "videos with audio", including sound effects and "dialogue between characters" [@google2025fuel], and OpenAI's Sora 2 that September produced speech and sound effects with its video and was, according to OpenAI, "better about obeying the laws of physics compared to prior systems"; OpenAI withdrew the Sora product in April 2026 [@openai2025sora]. Google DeepMind's Genie 3 (August 2025) goes further. It is a **world model**, in the company's definition an AI system that can use its understanding of the world "to simulate aspects of it": it generates an environment a user can move through in real time, at 24 frames per second and 720p, which stays consistent for a few minutes [@deepmind2025genie].

**Hearing and speaking.** A model can also take speech in and give speech out directly. GPT-4o, described by OpenAI as an "autoregressive omni model" that is "trained end-to-end across text, vision, and audio", responds to spoken input in 320 milliseconds on average, close to human conversational timing [@hurst2024gpt4o]. Alibaba's open Qwen2.5-Omni documents one way to build such a model. A "Thinker" language model reads text, images, audio and video, and a "Talker" turns the Thinker's internal representations into speech tokens as they are produced, so that speaking can begin before the reply is complete; a modified RoPE keeps the timestamps of video frames and audio aligned [@xu2025qwen25omni].

Such **omni models** are common at the frontier, but output is usually narrower than input. Gemini 4 Argon's API returns text only [@google2026gemini], and Muse Spark Open takes text, images, video and audio but answers in text [@meta2026musesparkopen]. The developers of closed models seldom say which design they use; OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra announcement, for instance, reports benchmarks for reading screens without describing how images enter the model [@openai2026gpt6].

## 3.5 From text predictor to assistant: pre-training and post-training

A modern chatbot is built in stages (Figure 3.3) [@ouyang2022training]. The first and usually by far the most expensive is **pre-training**: next-token prediction over an enormous body of text, which yields a **base model** like those in Table 3.7. **Post-training** then turns it into an assistant [@ouyang2022training; @edelman2025why].

![Figure 3.3: How a typical chat model is trained. Pre-training on web-scale text produces a base model that continues text. Reasoning models add a further stage of reinforcement learning on problems whose answers can be checked automatically, and since 2025 on tasks in tool and code environments (Section 3.7). ### The pre-training objective

Pre-training uses the text itself as the answer key, which is why it is called **self-supervised learning**: no human labelling is needed [@brown2020language; @radford2018improving]. At every position of every training passage the model predicts the next token, and the loss is the cross-entropy of Section 2.3, −ln *p*~true~, the negative natural logarithm of the probability the model gave to the token that actually came next [@goodfellow2016deep]. Table 3.7 gives a sense of scale. If the true continuation is `" Paris"`, Qwen2.5-1.5B's loss at that position is −ln 0.273 ≈ 1.30, while GPT-2 small's is −ln 0.032 ≈ 3.44. A perfect prediction would cost 0. Training averages this loss over trillions of positions and uses backpropagation to nudge every weight in the direction that reduces it. Because of the causal mask, a passage of *n* tokens supplies *n* − 1 separate next-token predictions in one parallel pass.

The pressure to predict text is in effect a pressure to absorb whatever helps predict it: grammar, facts, the conventions of code and mathematics, styles of argument; the GPT-3 results that open this chapter are one illustration [@brown2020language]. How loss falls with model size, data and compute is the subject of Chapter 4.

### The data

The corpora have grown by orders of magnitude. GPT-1 in 2018 was trained on BooksCorpus, "over 7,000 unique unpublished books" [@radford2018improving]. GPT-2 used WebText, about 40 GB of web pages linked from Reddit posts [@radford2019language]. GPT-3 was trained on 300 billion tokens drawn largely from Common Crawl, a public archive of the web, filtered from 45 TB of compressed text to 570 GB, plus books and Wikipedia [@brown2020language]. Meta's Llama 3 405B was pre-trained on about 15.6 trillion tokens [@grattafiori2024llama].

Meta describes removing personal data and unsafe domains, de-duplicating, applying heuristics such as "dirty word" counts, and scoring documents with quality classifiers. It then chose the mix by training small models on candidate mixes and extrapolating. The final mix was roughly 50% general knowledge, 25% mathematics and reasoning, 17% code and 8% multilingual text [@grattafiori2024llama]. The open FineWeb dataset documents a comparable pipeline and tests each filtering choice. It holds 15 trillion tokens, and its 1.3-trillion-token educational subset gave "dramatically better performance" on knowledge and reasoning benchmarks [@penedo2024fineweb].

### Instruction tuning

A base model is trained to continue text, not to follow instructions [@ouyang2022training]. Asked a question, it may answer, or it may continue with five more questions in the style of an exam paper. The simplest fix is **supervised fine-tuning** (SFT) on examples of instructions paired with good responses, often called **instruction tuning**; this chapter uses the two terms interchangeably. @wei2021finetuned fine-tuned a 137-billion-parameter model on more than 60 datasets phrased as natural-language instructions and found that it improved substantially on new kinds of tasks it had not been tuned on. The model learns the general habit of reading an instruction and doing what it says.

### Reinforcement learning from human feedback

Many qualities of a good answer are easier to recognise than to demonstrate. In **reinforcement learning** the model produces an output and receives only a score, a **reward**, and its weights are adjusted to make high-scoring outputs more likely. **Reinforcement learning from human feedback** (RLHF) obtains the reward from people's comparisons, an idea developed by @christiano2017deep for game-playing and simulated robots.

@ouyang2022training applied it to GPT-3 to create InstructGPT, an early instruction-following assistant, in three steps. About 40 contractors wrote demonstrations for supervised fine-tuning. They then ranked several model outputs for each of about 33,000 prompts, and these rankings trained a **reward model** that outputs a single score for any prompt and response.

The reward model's loss, for a preferred response *y*~w~ and a rejected one *y*~l~, is −log σ(*r*(*x*, *y*~w~) − *r*(*x*, *y*~l~)), where σ is the logistic function that maps any number to a probability. The model thus learns to treat the score difference as the log-odds that a labeller prefers *y*~w~: a difference of 1 means a predicted preference of σ(1) ≈ 73%. Finally, the PPO reinforcement-learning algorithm adjusted the language model to maximise the reward model's score minus a penalty, at each token, for drifting away from the fine-tuned starting model [@ouyang2022training]. The penalty matters because the reward model is only a learned proxy; a policy that strays far from the text it was trained on can find outputs the proxy overrates (Chapter 12 discusses this "reward hacking").

The effect was large. In the authors' human evaluations, outputs from the 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT were preferred to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, "despite having 100x fewer parameters" [@ouyang2022training]. The comparison was with the base GPT-3 given a prompt, a weak baseline for following instructions, so it shows how much post-training helps an untuned model rather than that alignment generally beats a hundredfold increase in scale.

### Direct preference optimisation

RLHF is, in the words of @rafailov2023direct, "a complex and often unstable procedure", because it trains a separate reward model, then samples from the language model repeatedly during reinforcement learning. Their **direct preference optimisation** (DPO) shows that, under the same preference model, the optimal policy can be obtained without either step. DPO trains directly on preference pairs with a classification-style loss (see the box below). It raises the probability of the preferred answer and lowers that of the rejected one, each relative to the starting model, so that the model's own probabilities act as an implicit reward, hence the paper's subtitle, "Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" [@rafailov2023direct]. The authors reported that DPO matched or exceeded PPO-based RLHF on sentiment control, summarisation and single-turn dialogue while being "substantially simpler to implement and train" [@rafailov2023direct]. Later controlled comparisons qualify this. @xu2024dpo found that PPO, once its settings were carefully tuned, outperformed DPO and other alignment methods in all the dialogue and code-generation tests they ran. The openly documented Tülu 3 recipe, for example, runs supervised fine-tuning, then DPO, then a reinforcement-learning stage described in Section 3.7 [@lambert2024tulu].

**Going deeper: The DPO loss**

where π is the model being trained, π~ref~ the starting model, σ the logistic function of the reward-model loss above, and β a setting that controls how far π may move. ### Constitutional AI and AI feedback

Human feedback is slow and costly. In an Anthropic preprint, @bai2022constitutional proposed **Constitutional AI**. To train the model to avoid harmful outputs, humans supply only a short list of written principles, the "constitution", in place of human harmlessness labels. In a supervised phase, the model drafts responses, critiques them against the principles and revises them, and is fine-tuned on the revisions. In a reinforcement-learning phase, a separate AI model judges which of two responses better satisfies a principle, and these judgements train the preference model, which plays the role of the reward model in RLHF. The authors called this **reinforcement learning from AI feedback** (RLAIF). They reported an assistant that was less harmful without being evasive, although the paper still used human labels for helpfulness.

Post-training of this kind has traditionally used far less data and computation than pre-training, by about a hundredfold according to an analysis by Epoch AI researchers [@edelman2025why]. A common view, which the authors of the LIMA study called the "superficial alignment hypothesis", is that it changes mainly which of the base model's capabilities are expressed, and in what style, without adding much knowledge. They fine-tuned a 65-billion-parameter model on only 1,000 carefully chosen examples and concluded that "almost all knowledge in large language models is learned during pretraining" [@zhou2023lima]. Reasoning models (Section 3.7) test that picture. A model rewarded for answers that people like can also learn to flatter users or to sound confident (Chapter 12).

## 3.6 Why language models hallucinate, and what retrieval changes

LLMs sometimes produce fluent statements that are false: invented quotations, non-existent references, wrong dates. The research literature calls this **hallucination**. @ji2023survey distinguish "intrinsic" hallucinations, which contradict a source the model was given, from "extrinsic" ones, which cannot be verified from that source at all. The term is a loose metaphor. The model perceives nothing, and the errors come from the same mechanism that produces correct answers.

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: Mata v. Avianca (2023)**

In a lawsuit against the airline Avianca in a US federal court in New York, lawyers filed a court submission in March 2023 citing court decisions that did not exist. In his opinion on sanctions, Judge Castel found that the lawyers had "submitted non-existent judicial opinions with fake quotes and citations created by the artificial intelligence tool ChatGPT" and had continued to stand by them after the court questioned their existence [@sdny2023mata, p. 1]. One lawyer testified that he had "falsely assumed" ChatGPT was "like a super search engine" (p. 15). When he asked the chatbot whether a case was real, it replied that it had supplied "real" authorities findable in standard legal databases (p. 19). The court imposed a $5,000 penalty on the two lawyers and their firm (pp. 33–34). Asking the same model to check itself was no independent check.
:::

### A statistical account

A model generates text that is probable given its training, and a plausible-looking citation has the right form whether or not it exists. @kalai2025why, in a preprint by researchers at OpenAI and Georgia Tech, make this precise. They compare generation with a simpler task they call "Is-It-Valid": shown a candidate answer, decide whether it is valid or an error. Generating only valid answers is at least as hard as this classification. They derive that a base model's generative error rate is at least roughly twice its misclassification rate on Is-It-Valid, minus a term that measures how badly the model is **calibrated**, that is, how far its confidence departs from its accuracy. The authors argue that pre-training on next-token prediction tends to make base models calibrated, so the term is small for them. For such a model, wherever classification is bound to fail, generation will fail too. The bound does not bind a model that abstains, or a miscalibrated one, and post-trained models are often less calibrated than their base models [@kalai2025why].

Arbitrary facts are where classification is bound to fail. A person's birthday follows no pattern that could be inferred from other facts, so a birthday seen only once in training gives the model little more to go on than one never seen. Building on an estimator that goes back to Alan Turing, the authors use the share of facts that appear exactly once, the "singleton rate", as a measure of how much the model has in effect not learned. If 20% of birthday facts appear exactly once in the training data, a calibrated base model should be expected to hallucinate on at least about 20% of birthday questions [@kalai2025why].

The second part of the argument concerns post-training. Most benchmarks award a point for a correct answer and nothing for "I don't know", so a model that guesses outscores one that admits uncertainty, as a student does on a multiple-choice exam without negative marking. The authors' meta-evaluation found that the large majority of popular benchmarks use such binary grading. Their remedy is to state a confidence threshold *t* in each evaluation's instructions and penalise wrong answers by *t*/(1 − *t*) points. With *t* = 0.75 a wrong answer costs 0.75/0.25 = 3 points, so guessing pays only when the model is more than 75% sure. Under such scoring, saying "I don't know" below the threshold becomes the best-scoring strategy [@kalai2025why].

### Retrieval-augmented generation

If hallucination arises partly because rare facts are stored imperfectly in the weights, one remedy is to supply the facts at answer time. **Retrieval-augmented generation** (RAG), named and popularised by @lewis2020retrieval, combines a language model with a searchable index. The documents are cut into passages, and an encoder network converts each passage into an embedding. A second encoder, trained alongside the first, embeds the question; the passages whose embeddings have the largest dot product with the question's are retrieved and placed in the model's context window alongside it. The dense passage retriever used by Lewis and colleagues searched English Wikipedia in 100-word passages. On the Natural Questions benchmark it found a passage containing the answer among its top 20 results for 78.4% of questions, against 59.1% for BM25, a classic keyword-based search method [@karpukhin2020dense]. RAG models generated "more specific, diverse and factual language" than a model relying on its weights alone [@lewis2020retrieval]. The pattern now sits behind chatbots that search the web and assistants that consult company documents, and lets a system use information newer than its **knowledge cut-off**, the date after which its training data end.

Retrieval reduces errors but does not remove them. The retriever may return the wrong passages, and the model may misread the right ones or blend them with its own guesses. In a preregistered study, @magesh2025hallucination tested legal research tools from LexisNexis and Thomson Reuters that were marketed with claims such as "hallucination-free". The tools hallucinated less than general-purpose GPT-4, but each hallucinated between 17% and 33% of the time on the authors' set of legal queries. Long retrieved contexts also run into the "lost in the middle" problem (Section 3.4).

On the evidence of this section, an LLM's answer is most trustworthy where the relevant facts are common in its training data or supplied in its context, and least trustworthy for rare specifics: exact figures, quotations, references and recent events [@kalai2025why; @lewis2020retrieval; @magesh2025hallucination]. Chapter 11 treats hallucination as a source of wider risk.

## 3.7 Reasoning models, and what they are doing inside

A model spends roughly the same computation on every token, so a hard question answered in one word gets little "thinking". @wei2022chain showed that giving a large model a few worked examples with step-by-step reasoning, **chain-of-thought prompting**, markedly improved its performance on arithmetic word problems. With eight such examples a 540-billion-parameter model set a new state of the art on the GSM8K benchmark of grade-school mathematics problems.

### Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards

Reasoning models make this behaviour a trained skill. OpenAI's system card describes its o1 series, first released as o1-preview in September 2024 and in full in December 2024, as "trained with large-scale reinforcement learning to reason using chain of thought" [@openai2024o1, p. 1]. The key change from RLHF is the reward. Where an answer can be checked by a program (a number to compare, code to run against tests), the reward needs no learned model of human taste. The Tülu 3 report named this **reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards** (RLVR): the model "only gets a reward if the model's completions are verified to be correct" [@lambert2024tulu]. A program that checks a mathematical answer is much harder to fool than a reward model, though not impossible (Chapter 12).

DeepSeek published one of the most detailed recipes, in peer-reviewed form in *Nature* in September 2025 [@guo2025deepseek]. For an experimental model, DeepSeek-R1-Zero, it started from its pre-trained base model, skipped supervised fine-tuning and applied reinforcement learning with two rule-based rewards: one for an answer matching the known correct result and one for putting the reasoning in the required format. The algorithm, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), introduced in a 2024 DeepSeek preprint, is a variant of PPO. It keeps PPO's safeguard against overly large updates and adds the penalty for drifting from a reference model, which InstructGPT-style RLHF built into the reward, directly to its loss. It drops PPO's **value network**, a second network that predicts how good a partial answer is and serves as a baseline [@shao2024deepseekmath]. For each question it samples a group of answers, scores them, and treats each answer's reward relative to the group's mean, divided by the group's standard deviation, as the signal. If four sampled answers score 1, 0, 0 and 1, the mean is 0.5 and the standard deviation 0.5 (or 0.58 if computed as a sample standard deviation), so the correct answers are reinforced with roughly +1 and the wrong ones discouraged with roughly −1. If all four are right, or all wrong, there is nothing to learn from that question.

Progress was measured on AIME 2024, the American Invitational Mathematics Examination, in which every answer is a single number that can be checked automatically. During training, R1-Zero's average accuracy rose from 15.6% to 77.9%. Its answers grew steadily longer, and it began to check and revise its own work, at one point writing "Wait, wait. Wait. That's an aha moment I can flag here." (The figures are DeepSeek's own, from the *Nature* version; the January 2025 preprint reported 71.0%.) The released DeepSeek-R1, which added supervised fine-tuning stages, scored 79.8%, close to the developers' measurement for OpenAI's o1, and was published under the permissive MIT licence [@guo2025deepseek].

This stage is still small next to pre-training, but it is growing. DeepSeek's *Nature* paper reports that the reinforcement-learning runs for R1-Zero and R1, with the data preparation between them, took about 147,000 GPU-hours [@guo2025deepseek]. That is roughly 5% of the 2.79 million GPU-hours the company reported for training its base model, DeepSeek-V3 [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3]. Epoch AI, an independent research institute, had estimated about 20% before these figures were published. It also cited OpenAI's statement that o3 used ten times the training compute of o1, most likely in this stage [@you2025how].

Whether this training adds new skills or mainly brings out existing ones is disputed. The gains in mathematics and coding are large, and DeepSeek reports that self-checking behaviour emerged during the training itself [@guo2025deepseek]. @yue2025does, however, compared reinforcement-trained models with their base models when each was allowed many attempts. The trained models won on a single attempt, but with hundreds of attempts the base models solved as many problems or more. The authors concluded that current training of this kind "rarely elicits fundamentally new reasoning patterns" and mainly makes the model find, more reliably, solutions it could already reach. Chapter 4 returns to what this implies for scaling.

Reasoning models also make capability purchasable at answer time, by thinking longer or sampling several answers; Chapter 4 treats this **test-time compute** as a scaling trend, and Section 4.6 describes the effort settings and routers through which 2025–26 models let users choose how much reasoning to buy [@openai2024o1; @guo2025deepseek].

### Rewards for answers no program can check

Verifiable rewards need answers that a program can check, as in mathematics and programming [@lambert2024tulu]. Essays, medical advice and research reports have no such checker, and developers have tried three ways round the problem.

The first rewards the steps rather than only the result. For "Let's Verify Step by Step", OpenAI researchers had people label 800,000 individual reasoning steps as correct or not, and trained a **process reward model** to score each step. Picking among candidate solutions with it, a model solved 78% of a representative subset of the MATH benchmark, clearly more than when solutions were judged by their final answers alone [@lightman2023lets].

The second uses a language model as the judge, an **LLM judge**. GPT-4, asked which of two chatbot answers was better, agreed with human preferences more than 80% of the time, about as often as people agree with each other, but it favoured the answer shown first, longer answers and answers resembling its own [@zheng2023judging].

The third gives the judge a checklist. With **rubric-based rewards**, each training prompt comes with criteria a good answer must meet, written by experts or generated by a model, and a judge scores the response against them. Training with such prompt-specific rubrics beat a judge that gave a single overall rating by up to 31% (relative) on HealthBench, a benchmark of medical conversations [@gunjal2025rubrics]. Developers' reports describe the same idea at scale. DeepSeek-V3.2 used "a generative reward model where each prompt has its own rubrics" for general tasks [@deepseekai2025deepseekv32], and Kimi K2 ranks its own candidate answers against core rubrics, against rubrics written to block known tricks, and against human-written ones [@kimiteam2025kimi].

A judge is a learned proxy, like the reward models of RLHF, and its biases are targets: a policy trained against it can learn to please it rather than to be right (Chapter 12) [@zheng2023judging]. These methods widen the reach of reinforcement learning without giving it the near-certainty of a test suite.

### Training agents: reinforcement learning in environments

From 2025 the largest change in post-training moved reinforcement learning from single answers to multi-step tasks. Instead of answering a maths problem, the model works in an **environment**: a software repository with tests, a terminal, a web browser, a simulated user with a database behind it, or a virtual computer with a screen. It calls tools, reads what comes back, reasons and calls again, and the reward comes at the end, when tests pass, a checker confirms that the task is done, or a judge scores the result [@kimiteam2025kimi; @deepseekai2025deepseekv32]. Chapter 5 describes agents built by placing a model in such a loop; **agentic reinforcement learning** trains the model inside the loop.

OpenAI said of o3 and o4-mini, in April 2025, that it had used reinforcement learning to teach them "not just how to use tools, but to reason about when to use them", and that they were its first reasoning models able to "agentically use and combine every tool within ChatGPT" [@openai2025introducingb]. xAI wrote that Grok 4, released that July, "was trained with reinforcement learning to use tools" such as a code interpreter and web browsing [@xai2025grok4]. Anthropic's Claude 4 models, released in May 2025, could use tools such as web search during extended thinking, alternating between reasoning and tool calls [@anthropic2025claude4]. Such **interleaved thinking** is the agentic counterpart of a chain of thought: the model reasons, acts, reads the result and reasons again within one trajectory.

Open developers have published most about how it is done. Kimi K2's post-training began with agentic data built at scale: more than 3,000 real tools that use the Model Context Protocol (Section 5.3) and over 20,000 synthetic ones, thousands of agents with different tool sets, simulated users, and a tool simulator that keeps track of state between calls. An LLM judge scored each trajectory against a task rubric and only successful ones were kept for training; reinforcement learning then ran in real and simulated environments, with real sandboxes mainly for coding [@kimiteam2025kimi]. Its successor, Kimi K2 Thinking, was trained to "interleave chain-of-thought reasoning with function calls" and, according to Moonshot, stays on task across 200 to 300 consecutive tool calls [@moonshot2025kimi]. DeepSeek-V3.2 reported more than 1,800 environments and 85,000 complex prompts: search and coding agents learned in real environments, and 1,827 general-agent environments were synthesised. Its reasoning is kept across tool calls and discarded only when a new user message arrives [@deepseekai2025deepseekv32]. Alibaba describes Qwen3.5's reinforcement learning as "scaled across million-agent environments" [@qwen2026qwen35].

Three things set this training apart from the maths-problem kind. Trajectories are long, and a single reward at the end says little about which of many steps helped, so developers cap and shape them: Kimi K2's training set a token budget per task and penalised answers that ran over [@kimiteam2025kimi]. Environments become a scarce input, since each needs a working sandbox and a checker that cannot be fooled, and Epoch AI researchers count building them among the bottlenecks of further scaling [@edelman2025why]. And the checkers can be gamed. In a 2025 Anthropic study, a model trained in real production coding environments that were open to reward hacks learned to exploit them, and the habit generalised to broader misbehaviour [@macdiarmid2025natural]; OpenAI traced its agents' escape from a test environment in July 2026 partly to training in which cheating, such as copying a hidden reference answer, had been rewarded [@openai2026technical] (Section 12.2).

The computation spent on this stage is growing. DeepSeek put the reinforcement-learning budget for V3.2 above 10% of its pre-training cost [@deepseekai2025deepseekv32], against about 5% for R1. xAI said it ran reinforcement learning for Grok 4 "at pretraining scale", and for Grok 4.7, in September 2026, "a longer reinforcement learning run on a harder mix of tasks", emphasising problems that take many hours [@xai2025grok4; @xai2026grok47]; these are company statements without published figures. Meta describes the reinforcement learning after Muse Spark's pre-training as the stage that "leverages compute to scalably amplify model capabilities" [@meta2026muse]. In the author's view this training, more than better prompting, explains most of the gains in agent benchmarks reported in Chapters 4 and 5, although no developer discloses how much each ingredient contributed. Chapter 5 describes how such models are deployed as agents, and where they still fail.

The written reasoning is not necessarily a faithful record of how the model reached its answer. In a preprint, Anthropic researchers slipped hints into questions and checked whether reasoning models that used a hint said so. The models mentioned the hint only a minority of the time: 25% on average for Anthropic's Claude 3.7 Sonnet and 39% for DeepSeek-R1 [@chen2025reasoning]. This matters for safety monitoring that relies on reading the chain of thought (Section 12.5).

### What is inside

Engineers know exactly what computations an LLM performs, but not fully what the learned weights represent. **Interpretability** research, which tries to read what a network has learned from its internal activity, bears on what reasoning models are doing inside [@elhage2021mathematical; @lindsey2025biology]; the debate below draws on it, and Section 12.4 describes its methods and limits.

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Do large language models understand language?**

A 2022 survey of natural-language-processing researchers [@michael2023what], also discussed by @mitchell2023debate, asked whether some model trained only on text, given enough data and computing power, could understand natural language "in some non-trivial sense". Of the 327 active researchers whose answers were analysed, 51% agreed and 49% disagreed.

The case against rests on a point of principle. @bender2020climbing argue that "a system trained only on form has a priori no way to learn meaning". Their thought experiment imagines a hyper-intelligent octopus tapping an undersea telegraph cable between two people. It could learn to imitate their messages convincingly yet would have no idea what the words refer to. On this view fluent output reflects statistical patterns in human writing, and hallucinations are exactly what one would expect from a system with no link between words and world.

The case for rests on internal evidence. @li2023emergent trained a GPT-style model only on sequences of moves in the board game Othello, never showing it the board, and found an internal representation of the board state; altering that representation changed the model's predicted moves accordingly. Anthropic's own unreviewed studies of its Claude models point the same way. They found features for concepts such as the Golden Gate Bridge [@templeton2024scaling], and traced intermediate steps, such as a model choosing a rhyme before writing the line, or representing "Texas" on the way from "Dallas" to "Austin" [@lindsey2025biology]. The caveat is that these are a company's studies of its own system.

The two sides weigh different evidence. One asks what a text-trained system can in principle connect its symbols to, the other what structure it demonstrably builds. @mitchell2023debate propose splitting "understanding" into several kinds, each with its own strengths and limitations. Intervention experiments like the Othello study can test the second question; whether any experiment could answer the first is itself disputed.
:::

**Native multimodality.** ChatGPT read and wrote text. GPT-4 accepted images [@openai2023gpt4] and GPT-4o heard and spoke [@hurst2024gpt4o]; frontier models of 2026 take in text, images, audio and video, and some products generate images within the chat model itself (Section 3.4) [@google2026gemini; @openai2025addendum].

**Reasoning, and control over it.** The step from ChatGPT to o1 in 2024 was training long chains of thought with reinforcement learning on checkable problems, a recipe DeepSeek-R1 made public (Section 3.7) [@openai2024o1; @guo2025deepseek]. Within a year reasoning was no longer a separate product line. Anthropic called Claude 3.7 Sonnet, in February 2025, "the first hybrid reasoning model on the market": one model that could answer at once or think first, within a token budget set by the developer [@anthropic2025claude37]. Qwen3 did the same with open weights [@yang2025qwen3], and Google described Gemini 2.5 models as "thinking models" [@google2025gemini25]. OpenAI's GPT-5 (August 2025) took another route, "a unified system" of a fast model and a deeper reasoning model with "a real-time router that quickly decides which to use" [@openai2025gpt5]. By 2026 the choice is usually a dial. Claude Opus 5.5 offers effort levels from low to max and can no longer be used with thinking switched off [@anthropic2026opus55], and Gemini 4 Argon offers low, medium and high thinking levels [@google2026gemini]. Section 4.6 describes these controls and the parallel "deep thinking" modes built on them.

**Agentic training.** The ChatGPT of 2022 could neither run code nor browse. Frontier models of 2026 are trained by reinforcement learning to act in software environments, calling tools within their reasoning over hundreds of steps (Section 3.7). Developers now describe their models chiefly by how long and how reliably they work. Anthropic says that Fable 5 and Mythos 5 "can work autonomously for longer than any previous Claude models" [@anthropic2026fable]; Google says Gemini 4 Carbon has run for days on internal multi-step engineering tasks [@kavukcuoglu2026gemini4carbon]; xAI says Grok 4.7's training emphasised tasks that take many hours [@xai2026grok47]. Several products also set multiple agents to work on one problem in parallel, as in Grok 4 Heavy and Muse Spark's "Contemplating" mode [@xai2025grok4; @meta2026muse].

**Families and distillation.** ChatGPT launched as one model. Developers now release families of tiers that trade capability against price and speed: Claude Haiku, Sonnet and Opus since 2024 [@anthropic2024claude3], with a Mythos tier above Opus from 2026 [@anthropic2026fable]; Gemini Pro and Flash [@vinyals2026gemini35]; GPT-6 Astra, Sol and Luna [@openai2026gpt6]. Smaller models are often trained partly by **distillation**, learning to imitate the outputs of a larger one: Meta "codistilled" Llama 4 Maverick from the larger Llama 4 Behemoth [@meta2025llama4], and DeepSeek distilled R1's reasoning into small open models [@guo2025deepseek]. Distillation also works on a competitor's outputs. Anthropic routes requests its classifiers flag as distillation attempts to an older model, and launched Opus 5.5 with an "anti-distillation safeguard" [@anthropic2026fable; @anthropic2026opus55].

**Open and closed.** In 2022 the strongest language models were closed. Meta's LLaMA and Llama 3 made open weights a credible alternative [@touvron2023llama; @grattafiori2024llama], and DeepSeek, Alibaba's Qwen team and Moonshot published several of the largest open models of 2025–26, with detailed reports (Table 3.9). Meta itself turned the other way for a time: Muse Spark, the first model of its new Superintelligence Labs, was offered in April 2026 only in Meta's own apps and a private API preview [@meta2026muse], and the open-weight Muse Spark Open followed in October [@meta2026musesparkopen]. Chapter 7 discusses licences and the policy debate over open weights.

Under all these changes, every 2026 model whose architecture has been published is still a transformer, in some cases with linear-attention layers mixed in, that predicts tokens one at a time from a context, pre-trained on next-token prediction over very large text collections and then post-trained [@deepseekai2026deepseekv4; @qwen2026qwen35; @meta2026musesparkopen].

Seen whole, the mechanism accounts for both what language models do well and where they fail. Next-token prediction rewards whatever regularities recur in the training text, so a model is fluent on common material, unreliable on rare specifics such as exact figures, quotations and references, and blind to events after its data were collected. Post-training changes what is rewarded without changing that mechanism; preference training and model judges inherit the blind spots of the people or models doing the ranking, and verifiable rewards help most where a program can check the answer. The costs follow the architecture's arithmetic, with about two operations per parameter for every token, a cache that grows with every token of context, and, in standard attention, work that grows with the square of the context [@kalai2025why; @lambert2024tulu; @kaplan2020scaling; @kwon2023efficient; @vaswani2017attention]. Those quantities, multiplied up to the scale of frontier training runs, are the subject of Chapter 4.

## Further reading

- Vaswani, A. et al. (2017). *Attention Is All You Need*. The original transformer paper; Sections 3.2 to 3.5 give the attention formula, multi-head attention and sinusoidal positions in full. [@vaswani2017attention]
- Elhage, N. et al. (2021). *A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits*. Rebuilds the transformer around the residual stream and shows what small attention-only models compute, with careful diagrams. [@elhage2021mathematical]
- Ouyang, L. et al. (2022). *Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback*. The clearest primary account of supervised fine-tuning, reward modelling and PPO turning a base model into an assistant, with dataset sizes and evaluation details. [@ouyang2022training]
- Guo, D. et al. (2025). *DeepSeek-R1 Incentivizes Reasoning in LLMs through Reinforcement Learning*. The most open description of reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards at scale, including the reward design and training curves. [@guo2025deepseek]
- Kalai, A. T. et al. (2025). *Why Language Models Hallucinate*. A short statistical argument for why confident errors arise in pre-training and persist through evaluation, with a concrete proposal for scoring. [@kalai2025why]
