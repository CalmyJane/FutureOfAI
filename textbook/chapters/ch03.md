# Chapter 3: How Large Language Models Work

::: {.objectives}
**After working through this chapter you should be able to:**

- explain how a large language model turns text into tokens and embeddings, and why it is trained to predict the next token;
- describe, in plain words, what attention does inside a transformer and why the context window is limited;
- compare the stages of pre-training and post-training, including instruction tuning, reinforcement learning from human feedback and Constitutional AI;
- evaluate why language models produce confident errors ("hallucinations") and how far retrieval and reasoning training reduce them;
- apply these ideas to judge what a given chatbot answer can and cannot be trusted for.

In May 2020 a team at OpenAI described GPT-3, a neural network with 175 billion parameters that had been trained on one narrow task: guessing the next piece of text in a very large collection of web pages, books and Wikipedia articles [@brown2020language]. Nobody had trained it to translate, answer questions or do sums. Yet when the researchers typed a few worked examples into the model, it carried on the pattern and performed tasks such as translation, question answering and three-digit arithmetic. The paper stressed that it did this "without any gradient updates or fine-tuning", meaning that the network's weights were not changed for the new task (abstract).

That observation is the puzzle at the centre of this chapter. How can a system trained only to continue text end up writing essays, explaining code and passing exams, and why does the same system sometimes invent facts with complete confidence? Chapter 2 explained how neural networks learn by adjusting weights to reduce a loss. This chapter builds on that foundation. It follows a sentence on its journey through a large language model (LLM), from the moment it is chopped into pieces to the moment a reply appears on screen, and then explains how the raw text-predictor is turned into a helpful assistant.

## 3.1 From text to numbers: tokens

A neural network can only do arithmetic, so the first job of any language model is to turn text into numbers. It does this by splitting the text into **tokens**: frequently occurring chunks of characters, each with a fixed identification number. A token is often a whole common word, sometimes a fragment of a longer word, and sometimes a single character or punctuation mark. The list of all tokens a model knows is called its **vocabulary**.

Table 3.1 shows a real example. The sentence "Students in Edinburgh rarely misunderstand tokenisation." was passed through `o200k_base`, the tokeniser that OpenAI's open-source `tiktoken` library assigns to GPT-4o and later OpenAI models [@openai2026tiktoken]. The sentence has six words but becomes eight tokens. Common words such as "Students" and "rarely" survive whole, and even "misunderstand" has its own token. The less common "tokenisation", with its British spelling, is split into " token" and "isation". Notice also that most tokens carry their leading space with them: " in" (with a space) and "in" (without) are different tokens with different numbers.

Where does the vocabulary come from? Most current tokenisers use a method called **byte-pair encoding**, adapted for language processing by @sennrich2016neural. It starts with single characters and repeatedly merges the pair of symbols that occurs most often in a large sample of text, adding each merged pair to the vocabulary. After many thousands of merges, frequent words have become single tokens while rare words remain divisible into parts. The approach means a model never meets a word it cannot represent: an unfamiliar name or a typing error is simply spelled out in smaller pieces. Vocabularies have grown over time. GPT-2's tokeniser has 50,257 tokens, while `o200k_base` has roughly 200,000 [@openai2026tiktoken].

Tokenisation has consequences that users notice. Because the model sees " strawberry" as one token rather than as ten letters, questions about spelling or letter counts are harder for it than they look. Tokenisation also treats languages unequally. Table 3.2 shows the sentence "I love learning about language models." and close translations. In `o200k_base` the English version needs 7 tokens, German 9, Hindi 12 and Swahili 13; in the older GPT-2 tokeniser the Hindi version needs 62. @petrov2023language found that the same text translated into different languages could differ in tokenised length by up to 15 times. Since commercial services charge per token and limit how many tokens a model can read, speakers of under-represented languages can pay more and fit less into each request.

::: {.box .concept}
**Key concept: Token**

A token is the basic unit a language model reads and writes: usually a word, part of a word, or a punctuation mark, each mapped to a fixed number. As a rough rule for English, a token is a little shorter than an average word, so 100 words become somewhat more than 100 tokens. Prices, speed limits and memory limits of language models are all counted in tokens, which is why the same request can cost different amounts in different languages.
:::

## 3.2 Embeddings: words as points in space

A token number on its own carries no meaning. The number 9059 (" cat" in `o200k_base`) is not "closer" to a dog than 9060 is. So the next step converts each token into an **embedding**: a long list of numbers that places the token as a point in a high-dimensional space. In the smallest GPT-2 model each embedding has 768 numbers; in Meta's largest Llama 3 model it has 16,384 [@radford2019language; @grattafiori2024llama]. These numbers are not designed by hand. They start random and are adjusted by gradient descent during training, in exactly the way Chapter 2 described for any other weight.

The idea that useful meanings emerge from such training was demonstrated vividly by the word2vec models of @mikolov2013efficient, trained on 1.6 billion words. Words used in similar contexts ended up near one another, so "Paris" lay close to "Berlin" and "cat" close to "dog". More surprisingly, directions in the space captured relationships. @mikolov2013linguistic reported that the calculation "King − Man + Woman" produced a vector very close to "Queen" (abstract). The difference between "man" and "woman" behaved like a consistent direction that could be added to other words. Figure 3.1 sketches this idea in two dimensions.

![Figure 3.1: An embedding space, sketched in two dimensions. Words used in similar ways sit close together, and some relationships (such as male to female, or country to capital) appear as roughly parallel arrows. Real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of dimensions. Schematic, not to scale. Source: illustrating findings of @mikolov2013linguistic.](figures/fig-03-01.svg){#fig:03-01}

The map analogy is helpful but breaks down in two ways. First, nobody can picture 768 dimensions, and the two-dimensional sketch inevitably distorts distances. Second, and more important, word2vec gave each word a single fixed point, so "bank" had the same embedding whether it meant a riverbank or a lender. A modern LLM uses its embedding table only as a starting point. As the token passes through the network's layers, its representation is repeatedly updated using the surrounding words, so that by the upper layers "bank" in "the river bank" and "bank" in "the bank raised rates" are represented quite differently. Section 3.4 explains the mechanism that makes this possible.

## 3.3 Predicting the next token

At its core, an LLM performs one operation over and over. Given a sequence of tokens, it outputs a probability for every token in its vocabulary being the next one. This is called **next-token prediction**, and a model that generates text this way, one token at a time and each time feeding its own output back in as input, is called **autoregressive**. A long answer from a chatbot is the result of hundreds or thousands of such predictions in a row.

The loop is easiest to see step by step. Suppose the text so far is "The cat sat on the". The model computes probabilities for every token in its vocabulary; for GPT-2 XL the front-runners are " edge" (7.3 per cent), " floor" (5.2) and " table" (4.9), while the nursery-rhyme " mat" is not among the top eight (computed by the author). One token, say " floor", is chosen and appended, giving "The cat sat on the floor". The whole extended sequence then goes back into the model to produce probabilities for the following token, perhaps "." or " and". Generation stops when the model produces a special end-of-text token or reaches a length limit. Two consequences follow. The model never drafts a whole answer and then revises it; each token is committed once chosen, and later tokens must make the best of earlier ones. And because every token requires a full pass through the network, longer answers cost proportionally more computation.

Table 3.3 shows real predictions. The prompt "The capital of France is" was given to three openly available models, and the table lists each model's five most likely next tokens with their probabilities. The smallest GPT-2 model (about 124 million parameters) gives "Paris" only 3.2 per cent and prefers " the". The largest GPT-2 model (about 1.5 billion parameters, released in 2019) raises "Paris" to 8.6 per cent [@radford2019language]. Qwen2.5-1.5B, released in 2024 with a similar number of parameters but trained on up to 18 trillion tokens, gives "Paris" 27.3 per cent [@qwen2024qwen25]. Even then, most of the probability is spread over other continuations, such as " located" or blank underscores that hint at fill-in-the-gap exercises in the training data.

The table shows something important about what a base model is. It models what text tends to come next, and "The capital of France is the city of Paris" or "The capital of France is located on the Seine" are perfectly good continuations. Turning such a model into one that answers questions directly is the job of post-training (Section 3.5).

How does the model learn these probabilities? Training uses the text itself as the answer key, which is why it is called **self-supervised learning**: no human labelling is needed. The model reads a passage, predicts each next token, and is then shown the token that actually came next. The loss (Chapter 2) is large when the model gave the true token a low probability and small when it gave it a high one; informally, the loss measures how surprised the model was. Gradient descent then nudges billions of weights so that the model will be a little less surprised next time. Repeated over trillions of tokens, this simple pressure forces the model to absorb grammar, facts, styles of argument and much else, because all of these help to predict text.

**Sampling and temperature.** Once the model has produced its probabilities, a separate procedure called **decoding** picks the actual token. Always choosing the single most likely token ("greedy" decoding) tends to produce dull and repetitive text. @holtzman2020curious showed that likelihood-maximising decoding leads to text that is "bland and strangely repetitive" (abstract), and proposed **nucleus sampling** (also called top-p sampling), which draws randomly from only the smallest set of tokens that together cover most of the probability. Most chat systems therefore sample: they roll weighted dice.

The **temperature** setting controls how weighted those dice are. Table 3.4 shows GPT-2 XL continuing "After the lecture, the students went to the". At the default temperature of 1, " cafeteria" has 7.5 per cent and " library" 4.9 per cent. Lowering the temperature to 0.5 sharpens the distribution, so that " cafeteria" rises to 51.7 per cent and " library" to 21.9 per cent, while unlikely options almost vanish. Raising the temperature flattens it, making surprising choices more frequent. A temperature near zero behaves like greedy decoding. Low temperatures suit tasks with one right answer, such as extracting a date from a document. Higher ones suit brainstorming, at the cost of more odd choices.

## 3.4 Attention and the transformer

Predicting the next token well requires using the right earlier words. In "The Law will never be perfect, but its application should be just", the word "its" refers back to "Law", several words earlier. Before 2017, most language models processed text one word at a time with recurrent networks, passing a running summary from step to step. Information from far back tended to fade, and the step-by-step design was slow to train because each step had to wait for the previous one.

The **transformer**, introduced by @vaswani2017attention in a paper titled "Attention Is All You Need", removed that bottleneck. It dispensed "with recurrence and convolutions entirely" (abstract) and relied instead on a mechanism called **attention**. In attention, every token's representation can directly look at every other token's representation and decide how much to draw from each. A useful analogy is a seminar in which each word may consult any other word. Each word forms a question ("which earlier word do I refer to?"), every word offers a label describing what it contains, and each word then takes a weighted mix of the information offered by the words whose labels best match its question. The analogy breaks down because nothing in the network is phrased in words. The "questions" and "labels" are learned lists of numbers, and the model's designers never specify what they should mean.

The original paper visualised this process. In its trained translation model, two attention mechanisms in the fifth of six layers attended sharply from "its" to "Law" and "application" in the sentence above, behaviour the authors described as "apparently involved in anaphora resolution", that is, working out what a pronoun refers to [@vaswani2017attention, Figure 4]. Such pictures are suggestive but need care: @jain2019attention found that attention weights often do not reliably explain why a model made a prediction.

Figure 3.2 shows how attention fits into the whole architecture. Each token's embedding is first combined with information about its position, since attention by itself does not know word order. The result then passes through a stack of identical **transformer blocks**. Each block contains an attention step, where tokens exchange information, and a feed-forward step, where each token's representation is processed separately by an ordinary neural network of the kind described in Chapter 2. A block also has several attention mechanisms working in parallel, called **attention heads**, which can specialise in different relationships. GPT-3 has 96 blocks with 96 heads each, and the largest Llama 3 model has 126 blocks with 128 heads [@brown2020language; @grattafiori2024llama]. After the last block, a final layer converts the representation of the most recent token into a probability for each token in the vocabulary.

![Figure 3.2: A decoder-only transformer, simplified. Tokens become embeddings plus position information, then pass through many identical blocks, each with an attention step (tokens exchange information) and a feed-forward step (each token is processed on its own). The final layer outputs next-token probabilities. Inset: attention from "its" to "Law" in a trained model. Diagram by the author, after @vaswani2017attention; probabilities computed by the author with GPT-2 XL [@radford2019language].](figures/fig-03-02.svg){#fig:03-02}

The original transformer had two halves: an encoder that read a sentence in one language and a decoder that wrote its translation. GPT models keep only the decoder [@radford2018improving]. In a decoder, attention is **masked** so that each token can look only at tokens before it, never after. This matches the training task: when predicting the next token, the model must not be allowed to peek at the answer. Because attention has no step-by-step chain, all positions in a training passage can be processed at once on parallel hardware such as the graphics processors described in Chapter 2. The original base model trained in 12 hours on eight GPUs [@vaswani2017attention], and that parallelism is what later made training on trillions of tokens feasible.

**Context windows.** The maximum number of tokens a model can attend to at once is its **context window**, which works like its short-term working memory. Anything outside the window is invisible to the model unless it is fed back in. GPT-3's window was 2,048 tokens [@brown2020language], about three pages of text. The largest Llama 3 model accepts up to 128,000 tokens [@grattafiori2024llama], and Google reported that Gemini 1.5 retrieved planted facts with over 99 per cent accuracy in research tests up to at least 10 million tokens [@geminiteam2024gemini]. The window is limited partly because standard attention compares every token with every other one, so its cost grows with the square of the length [@vaswani2017attention, Table 1]: doubling the text quadruples that work. A bigger window is also not the same as perfect use of it. @liu2024lost found that models often answered best when the relevant information sat at the beginning or end of a long input and noticeably worse when it was buried in the middle.

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: Attention and temperature as formulas**

For each token, the network computes three vectors by multiplying its current representation by learned weight matrices: a query $q$, a key $k$ and a value $v$. Attention then computes, for the current token, a score for every earlier token $j$ equal to the dot product $q \cdot k_j$, divided by $\sqrt{d}$ (where $d$ is the length of the vectors) to keep the numbers in a manageable range. In words, a score is high when the current token's question matches the other token's label.

The scores are turned into weights between 0 and 1 that sum to 1 using the softmax function, $w_j = e^{s_j} / \sum_i e^{s_i}$, and the output is the weighted sum $\sum_j w_j v_j$ of the values. Written for all tokens at once this is $\mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d})\,V$ [@vaswani2017attention]. Masking simply sets the scores for future tokens to minus infinity, so their weights become zero.

The same softmax produces the final next-token probabilities from raw scores $z$ (called logits). Temperature $T$ divides the logits before the softmax: $p_i = e^{z_i/T} / \sum_k e^{z_k/T}$. With $T < 1$ the differences between scores are magnified and the top token gains; with $T > 1$ they shrink and the distribution flattens. Training minimises the cross-entropy loss, $-\log p_{\text{true}}$, which is zero only when the model assigns probability 1 to the token that actually occurred.
:::

## 3.5 From text predictor to assistant: pre-training and post-training

Building a modern chatbot happens in stages, summarised in Figure 3.3. The first and by far the most expensive stage is **pre-training**: next-token prediction on an enormous body of text. Its scale has grown quickly. GPT-1 in 2018 was trained on BooksCorpus, over 7,000 unpublished books [@radford2018improving]. GPT-2 used WebText, about 40 GB of text from some 8 million web pages linked from Reddit [@radford2019language]. GPT-3 was trained on 300 billion tokens drawn largely from a filtered version of Common Crawl, a public archive of the web, alongside books and Wikipedia [@brown2020language]. Meta's largest Llama 3 model, released in 2024, was pre-trained on about 15 trillion tokens [@grattafiori2024llama]. Chapter 4 examines why scale has mattered so much and whether usable text is running out.

Model size has grown alongside the data, but "number of parameters" has become a less straightforward measure. Some recent models use a **mixture-of-experts** design, in which each transformer block contains many alternative feed-forward networks ("experts") and a small routing network sends each token to only a few of them. DeepSeek-V3, for example, has 671 billion parameters in total, but only 37 billion are used for any single token, and it was pre-trained on 14.8 trillion tokens [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3]. The design lets a model store more knowledge without a matching increase in the computation needed per token. When comparing models, it therefore helps to ask both how many parameters exist and how many are active.

![Figure 3.3: The training pipeline of a typical chat model. Pre-training on web-scale text produces a base model that continues text; supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations teaches it to follow instructions; preference training with human or AI feedback shapes its style and safety; reasoning models add reinforcement learning on problems with checkable answers. Diagram by the author, based on @ouyang2022training, @bai2022constitutional and @guo2025deepseekr1.](figures/fig-03-03.svg){#fig:03-03}

The result of pre-training is a **base model** like those in Table 3.3. It has absorbed a great deal but behaves like an autocomplete system. Asked a question, it may answer, or it may continue with five more questions in the style of an exam paper. The second stage, **post-training**, adapts it to a role. The model's weights are adjusted further, but on far less data, chosen mainly to teach behaviour.

**Instruction tuning.** The simplest step is **supervised fine-tuning** on examples of instructions paired with good responses, a process often called instruction tuning. @wei2021finetuned showed in a preprint that fine-tuning a 137-billion-parameter model on more than 60 tasks phrased as natural-language instructions substantially improved its performance on new kinds of tasks that it had not been tuned on. The model learns the general habit of reading an instruction and doing what it says, beyond the particular examples it was shown.

**Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).** Demonstrations are expensive to write, and many qualities of a good answer are easier to recognise than to produce. RLHF exploits this. The idea of learning from human comparisons was developed by @christiano2017deep for training agents in games and simulated robots, and @ouyang2022training applied it to GPT-3 to create InstructGPT, a direct predecessor of ChatGPT. Their method had three steps. Human labellers wrote demonstrations used for supervised fine-tuning. They then ranked several model outputs for the same prompt from best to worst. These rankings trained a separate **reward model** that predicts which answers people prefer, and reinforcement learning then adjusted the language model to produce answers the reward model scores highly. The effect was large: in the authors' human evaluations, outputs from the 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT were preferred to outputs from the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, despite the model being over 100 times smaller [@ouyang2022training]. Some later systems use simpler alternatives. **Direct preference optimisation** (DPO), for example, learns from the same kind of preference pairs without training a separate reward model or running reinforcement learning [@rafailov2023direct].

**Constitutional AI and AI feedback.** Human feedback is slow, costly and inconsistent, and labellers may be exposed to disturbing content. @bai2022constitutional at Anthropic proposed **Constitutional AI**, in which humans supply only a short list of written principles, the "constitution". In a supervised phase, the model drafts responses, critiques them against the principles and revises them, and is then fine-tuned on the revisions. In a reinforcement-learning phase, the model compares pairs of its own responses, and these AI judgements train the preference model. The authors called this **reinforcement learning from AI feedback** (RLAIF). They reported an assistant that was less harmful without being evasive, engaging with problematic requests by explaining its objections.

Post-training is powerful but shallow in one sense. It mostly changes which of the base model's existing capabilities are expressed, and in what style. It does not add much new knowledge, and it introduces its own problems. A model rewarded for answers that people like can learn to flatter users or to sound confident. Chapter 12 examines these limits of RLHF, including sycophancy and reward hacking.

## 3.6 Why language models hallucinate, and what retrieval changes

LLMs sometimes produce fluent statements that are false: invented quotations, non-existent references, wrong dates. The research literature calls this **hallucination**. @ji2023survey distinguish "intrinsic" hallucinations, which contradict a source the model was given, from "extrinsic" hallucinations, which cannot be verified from that source at all. The term is a loose metaphor. The model does not perceive anything, and the errors are produced by the same mechanism that produces correct answers.

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: Mata v. Avianca (2023)**

In a lawsuit against the airline Avianca in a US federal court in New York, lawyers filed a brief in March 2023 citing court decisions that did not exist. In his opinion on sanctions, Judge P. Kevin Castel found that the lawyers had "submitted non-existent judicial opinions with fake quotes and citations created by the artificial intelligence tool ChatGPT" and had continued to stand by them after the court questioned their existence [@castel2023mata, p. 1]. One lawyer testified that he had assumed ChatGPT was "like a super search engine" (p. 15). When he asked the chatbot whether a case was real, it replied that it had supplied "real" authorities findable in standard legal databases (p. 19). The court imposed a $5,000 penalty (p. 33). The case illustrates the core problem: a language model's confidence and fluency are not evidence that a claim is true, and asking the model to check itself is not an independent check.
:::

Why does this happen? Section 3.3 gives the first part of the answer. A model generates text that is probable given its training, and a plausible-looking citation has the right form whether or not it exists. @kalai2025why, in a preprint by researchers at OpenAI and Georgia Tech, give a statistical account. During pre-training, a model cannot reliably learn facts that appear rarely in its data. If, say, 20 per cent of people's birthdays appear exactly once in the training text, a base model should be expected to hallucinate on at least about 20 per cent of those birthday facts. The authors then argue that post-training and evaluation keep the problem alive. Most benchmarks award a point for a correct answer and nothing for "I don't know", so a model that guesses scores better than one that admits uncertainty, much as a student does on a multiple-choice exam without negative marking. Their proposed remedy is to change how evaluations are scored, rewarding expressed uncertainty instead of penalising it.

**Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG).** If hallucination arises partly because facts are stored imperfectly in the model's weights, one remedy is to supply the facts at the moment of answering. @lewis2020retrieval combined a language model with a searchable index of Wikipedia. Before answering, a retriever finds relevant passages, and these are placed in the model's context window alongside the question. The authors reported that such models generated "more specific, diverse and factual language" than a model relying on its weights alone (abstract). Today the same pattern sits behind chatbots that search the web, company assistants that consult internal documents, and tools that cite their sources. RAG also lets a system use information newer than its training data. A model's built-in knowledge stops at the point when its pre-training data was collected, so without retrieval it cannot know about later events, and it may not know that it does not know.

Retrieval reduces errors but does not remove them. The model may retrieve the wrong documents, misread the right ones, or blend retrieved text with its own guesses. In a preregistered study, @magesh2025hallucination tested legal research tools from LexisNexis and Thomson Reuters that were marketed with claims such as "hallucination-free". The tools hallucinated less than the general-purpose GPT-4, but each hallucinated between 17 and 33 per cent of the time on the authors' set of legal queries. Long retrieved contexts also run into the "lost in the middle" problem described in Section 3.4.

For users, the practical lesson follows from the mechanism. An LLM's answer is most trustworthy where the relevant facts are common in its training data or supplied in its context, and least trustworthy for rare specifics: exact figures, quotations, names of minor people, references and recent events. Checks that are independent of the model, such as opening a cited source or searching a database directly, catch errors that a second question to the same model may not, as the Avianca case shows. Requests can also be framed to reduce risk, for instance by supplying the source document and asking the model to answer only from it. Hallucination as a source of wider social risk is treated in Chapter 11.

## 3.7 Reasoning models, multimodality and what remains unknown

**Reasoning models.** Next-token prediction implies that a model spends roughly the same amount of computation on each token. A hard question answered in one word gets little "thinking". Researchers found early that models answered multi-step problems better when they wrote out intermediate steps first. @wei2022chain showed that giving a large model a few worked examples with step-by-step reasoning, a technique called **chain-of-thought prompting**, markedly improved its performance on arithmetic word problems.

Reasoning models make this behaviour a trained skill. According to the company's system card, OpenAI's o1 series, released in late 2024, is "trained with large-scale reinforcement learning to reason using chain of thought", producing a long internal chain of reasoning before answering [@openai2024openai, p. 1]. DeepSeek, a Chinese company, published a detailed recipe in January 2025 and in peer-reviewed form in *Nature* later that year [@guo2025deepseekr1]. Starting from its pre-trained base model, it skipped supervised fine-tuning and applied reinforcement learning with simple rule-based rewards: an answer to a mathematics problem earned a reward only if it matched the known correct result. On the AIME 2024 mathematics competition, the model's average accuracy rose during training from 15.6 to 77.9 per cent. Its answers also grew steadily longer, and it began to check and revise its own work, at one point writing "Wait, wait. Wait. That's an aha moment I can flag here." The released DeepSeek-R1 scored 79.8 per cent on the same test, against 79.2 per cent for o1 and 9.3 per cent for GPT-4o, as reported by the DeepSeek authors.

This shift means that capability can now be bought at answer time as well as at training time: letting a model think longer, or sampling several answers and choosing among them, raises accuracy on hard problems. Chapter 4 discusses this **test-time compute** as a new scaling trend. Two cautions apply. Reinforcement learning works best where answers can be checked automatically, as in mathematics and programming, and less well for essays or advice. And the written reasoning is not necessarily a faithful record of how the model reached its answer. When @chen2025reasoning slipped hints into questions, reasoning models that used a hint mentioned it in their reasoning only a minority of the time: 25 per cent on average for Anthropic's Claude 3.7 Sonnet and 39 per cent for DeepSeek-R1. Chapter 12 returns to whether such reasoning can be monitored for safety.

**Multimodal models.** The transformer is not limited to words. @dosovitskiy2021image showed that an image can be cut into small square patches, each turned into a vector and processed like a token. @radford2021learning trained a model called CLIP on 400 million image–caption pairs from the internet to match pictures with their descriptions, linking images and language in a shared embedding space. Current systems combine these ideas, so that text, images, audio and video can all enter the same context window. Google reported that Gemini 1.5 could reason over hours of video and audio [@geminiteam2024gemini], and Meta described adding image, video and speech capabilities to Llama 3 by attaching separately trained components to the language model [@grattafiori2024llama].

**Open and closed weights.** A model's weights are the billions of numbers learned in training. Some developers publish them so that anyone can download, run and modify the model; these are **open-weight models**. Meta released the weights of its 405-billion-parameter Llama 3 model [@grattafiori2024llama], and DeepSeek released R1 under the permissive MIT licence [@guo2025deepseekr1]. Others offer **closed-weight** models only through an app or a paid interface, keeping the weights private. The line is not always between companies: Alibaba's Qwen2.5 family includes both downloadable models, such as the one in Table 3.3, and proprietary versions available only as a hosted service [@qwen2024qwen25]. The benefits and risks of openness, and the "DeepSeek moment" of January 2025, are the subject of Chapter 7.

**What we do and do not understand.** Engineers know exactly what computations an LLM performs, since they wrote the code. What they do not fully know is what the learned weights represent, or why a particular answer comes out. The field of **interpretability** tries to open this black box. Using a method that traces which internal features influence an output, Anthropic researchers reported in 2025 that their Claude 3.5 Haiku model appeared to plan ahead when writing rhyming verse: "Before beginning to write each line, the model identifies potential rhyming words that could appear at the end" [@lindsey2025biology]. In the same study, asked for the capital of the state containing Dallas, the model seemed to represent "Texas" internally as an intermediate step before answering "Austin". Findings like these suggest that next-token prediction can involve more structure than the phrase implies, but the methods so far explain only parts of a model's behaviour. Chapter 12 covers interpretability in depth.

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Do large language models understand language?**

Researchers are divided, and the disagreement partly concerns what "understand" should mean. A 2022 survey of natural-language-processing researchers, reported by @mitchell2023debate, found that of 480 respondents 51 per cent agreed that a model trained only on text could in principle understand language "in some non-trivial sense", while 49 per cent disagreed.

One side holds that learning from text alone cannot produce meaning. @bender2020climbing argue that "a system trained only on form has a priori no way to learn meaning" (abstract). Their thought experiment imagines a hyper-intelligent octopus tapping an undersea telegraph cable between two people: it could learn to imitate their messages convincingly yet would have no idea what the words refer to. On this view, fluent output reflects statistical patterns in human writing, and hallucinations are exactly what one would expect.

The other side points to evidence that models build internal representations of the situations their text describes. @li2023emergent trained a GPT-style model only on sequences of moves in the board game Othello, never showing it the board. They nevertheless found an internal representation of the board state, and changing that representation changed the model's predicted moves. Interpretability work such as @lindsey2025biology reports intermediate steps and planning inside commercial models.

What would settle it? @mitchell2023debate argue for a new science of intelligence that would distinguish different modes of understanding, each with its own strengths and limitations, so the question may need splitting into several narrower ones. Experiments that intervene on a model's internal representations and predict how its behaviour changes are one of the more promising sources of such evidence.
:::

## Summary

- Language models split text into tokens using methods such as byte-pair encoding; tokenisation explains odd weaknesses (spelling, letter counting) and makes some languages costlier to process than others.
- Each token becomes an embedding, a learned point in a high-dimensional space; inside a transformer these representations are continually updated by context.
- An LLM is trained by self-supervised next-token prediction; at generation time, decoding settings such as temperature and nucleus sampling decide how adventurous the output is.
- Attention lets every token draw information from every earlier token; transformers stack many attention and feed-forward blocks, and their context window is limited partly because attention's cost grows with the square of the input length.
- Pre-training on trillions of tokens produces a base model; post-training (instruction tuning, RLHF, DPO, Constitutional AI) turns it into an assistant but changes behaviour more than knowledge.
- Hallucinations follow from how models are trained and evaluated; retrieval-augmented generation reduces them but, as legal-tool studies show, does not eliminate them.
- Reasoning models, trained with reinforcement learning on checkable problems, trade extra computation at answer time for accuracy, but their written reasoning is not always a faithful account of their process.
- Engineers understand the computations of an LLM but not fully what the learned weights represent, and whether models "understand" language remains an open debate.
