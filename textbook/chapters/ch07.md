The government of Alberta, a Canadian province, set out to attract data centres. Requests to connect new data centres to its grid soon reached 16 gigawatts (GW), more than the province's entire peak electricity demand, and in 2025 the grid operator imposed a temporary cap of 1.2 GW to protect the reliability of the system [@iea2026key, sec. 3]. A gigawatt is a measure of power: roughly the output of one large power station. The episode shows something that is easy to miss when AI is experienced as text on a screen. Every answer from a chatbot is computed on physical machines, in buildings that draw electricity from real grids, using chips made in a handful of factories.

This chapter is about that physical and industrial base. It starts with the chips themselves and the supply chain that produces them, then turns to the export controls that have made chips an instrument of foreign policy. It then examines the electricity and water used by data centres, the cost of training large models and the concentration of the industry in a few firms. The second half of the chapter turns to open-weight models, whose release is one of the main counterweights to that concentration, and closes with the ways governments try to steer AI through its hardware.

## 7.1 Why AI runs on special chips

Chapter 2 explained that training a neural network consists mostly of multiplying large tables of numbers (matrices) together, millions or billions of times. A conventional central processing unit (CPU), the general-purpose chip in a laptop, is designed to carry out a long sequence of varied instructions quickly, one after another. Matrix multiplication is different: it consists of a vast number of simple, independent multiplications that can be done at the same time. A graphics processing unit (GPU) contains thousands of small arithmetic units that work in parallel, which is why chips first designed to draw video-game graphics turned out to suit neural networks.

An analogy helps. A CPU is like a small team of expert chefs who can cook any dish in sequence; a GPU is like a large canteen kitchen where hundreds of assistants each chop one vegetable at the same moment. The canteen wins when the job is a mountain of identical chopping. The analogy breaks down because a GPU's "assistants" are also fed by very fast memory, and that memory, more than raw arithmetic, often limits speed in practice.

GPUs are one kind of **AI accelerator**, a chip specialised for the arithmetic of machine learning. Others were designed from the start for neural networks. Google's Tensor Processing Unit (TPU), deployed in its data centres since 2015, was reported in 2017 to be on average about 15 to 30 times faster than the contemporary GPUs and CPUs it was compared with on Google's own inference workloads, and 30 to 80 times more efficient per watt [@jouppi2017indatacenter]. Those figures come from Google's own engineers, on its own workloads, and later GPU generations narrowed the gap, but the paper illustrates why large companies have invested in custom silicon.

Accelerators do not work alone. The data they process sits in **high-bandwidth memory (HBM)**, stacks of memory chips placed close to the processor so that numbers can move in and out quickly; the leading HBM makers are SK Hynix and Samsung of South Korea and Micron of the United States [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1]. Thousands of accelerators must also exchange data constantly during training, which requires high-speed networking between servers. The 2026 AI Index estimates that worldwide AI computing capacity grew about 3.3 times a year from 2022 to 2025, reaching roughly 17.1 million "H100-equivalents" (a unit based on one of Nvidia's main data-centre chips) [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1]. Nvidia chips accounted for over 60% of that capacity, with Google and Amazon supplying much of the rest and Huawei holding a small but growing share. Because the estimate is inferred from revenue data and analyst reports, the AI Index itself describes it as indicating trends rather than exact counts.

The commercial weight of this demand is visible in Nvidia's accounts. In its fiscal year ending January 2026, the company reported revenue of $215.9 billion, of which sales to the data-centre market contributed $193.7 billion, up from $47.5 billion two years earlier [@nvidia2026form]. Hardware efficiency has also improved: leading chips deliver roughly ten times more computation per watt than those of a decade ago. Models have grown faster than efficiency, however, so the total power needed to train frontier systems has kept rising [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1].

## 7.2 The semiconductor supply chain and its chokepoints

A chip passes through several specialised industries before it reaches a data centre, and each stage is dominated by very few firms. Figure 7.1 shows the stack, from the machines that make chips at the bottom to the applications people use at the top.

![Figure 7.1: The AI supply chain as a stack. Each layer depends on the one below, and several layers are dominated by one or a few firms (named examples are illustrative, not exhaustive). Chokepoints such as extreme-ultraviolet lithography and leading-edge fabrication are where governments have focused export controls. Diagram by the author, based on @sajadieh2026ai and @asml2026annual.](figures/fig-07-01.svg){#fig:07-01}

At the base are the **lithography** machines that print circuit patterns onto silicon wafers using light. The most advanced chips require extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) lithography, and ASML, a Dutch company, states in its 2025 annual report that it is "currently the world's only manufacturer of EUV lithography systems" [@asml2026annual]. Above that sits **fabrication**, the manufacture of the chips in plants called fabs. Firms such as Nvidia and AMD design chips but do not make them; they send their designs to foundries, chiefly the Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC) and Samsung. According to the 2026 AI Index, TSMC "fabricates virtually every leading AI chip", including Nvidia's Blackwell GPUs and AMD's MI300X, which makes it a single point of dependency for the whole industry [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1]. A TSMC expansion in the United States began operating in 2025, but the AI Index still describes the global AI hardware supply chain as dependent on one foundry in Taiwan.

After fabrication, chips are **packaged**: the processor and its HBM stacks are joined on a single base so that they can communicate quickly. Packaging and testing are done by specialist companies such as ASE Group in Taiwan and Amkor Technology in the United States [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1]. Nvidia's own filings list TSMC and Samsung as its wafer suppliers and SK Hynix, Micron and Samsung as its memory suppliers [@nvidia2026form]. Every layer has, in the AI Index's words, "high barriers to entry", requiring decades of expertise, specialised equipment and large capital investment.

Concentration creates fragility. The IEA reported in April 2026 that a shortage of HBM had developed over the previous six months and was expected to last "through at least the end of 2027" [@iea2026key, Executive Summary]. The same report described how a conflict in the Middle East in 2026 disrupted supplies of helium from Qatar, which had supplied roughly a third of the world's helium. Helium is used to cool and clean chip-making equipment and has, according to the IEA, no substitute with equivalent properties [@iea2026key, Box 1.1]. A disruption far from any data centre can thus slow the supply of AI hardware.

::: {.box .concept}
**Key concept: Chokepoint**

A chokepoint is a stage in a supply chain that very few suppliers control and that others cannot easily replace. In AI hardware, the clearest examples are EUV lithography (one supplier) and leading-edge chip fabrication (mainly one foundry). Chokepoints give whoever controls them leverage, which is why export controls target them, and they create shared vulnerability, because a single fire, shortage or conflict can delay production worldwide.
:::

## 7.3 Export controls and the geopolitics of compute

Because the supply chain is concentrated, and because the most important firms are based in the United States or in allied countries, the US government has been able to use **export controls** to limit which countries can obtain advanced AI chips and chip-making tools. An export control is a legal requirement to obtain a government licence before selling certain goods abroad. The US rules are written by the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS). Figure 7.2 summarises the main steps.

![Figure 7.2: Main US export-control measures on advanced AI chips and related items, October 2022 to July 2026. The direction of policy changed in 2025: rules continued to restrict China, while the global licensing framework of January 2025 was abandoned before it took full effect and replaced by country-by-country arrangements. Source: @bis2022implementation; @bis2023implementation; @bis2024foreignproduced; @bis2025framework; @bis2025department; @nvidia2025form; @bis2026revision; @bis2026enhanced.](figures/fig-07-02.svg){#fig:07-02}

The first major rule, issued on 7 October 2022, imposed licence requirements on advanced computing chips, on computers containing them and on certain chip-manufacturing equipment, mainly aimed at China [@bis2022implementation]. Chip designers responded by offering China-specific products designed to comply with the rules, as Nvidia's filings describe [@nvidia2026form]. An update in October 2023 revised the controls to "more effectively achieve" their aims [@bis2023implementation]. Nvidia told investors that the revised rule required licences for exports to China and several other countries of chips including the A100, A800, H100 and H800, judged by "total processing performance and/or performance density" [@nvidia2023form]. In December 2024 BIS added further controls on chip-making equipment and, for the first time, on certain high-bandwidth memory [@bis2024foreignproduced].

In January 2025 the outgoing Biden administration published a broader **Framework for Artificial Intelligence Diffusion**. It extended licence requirements for advanced chips to most of the world, sorting countries into tiers with different levels of access, and added a new control on the weights of certain advanced closed-weight AI models [@bis2025framework]. Before its compliance date of 15 May 2025, the new administration announced on 13 May 2025 that it would rescind the rule and instructed officials not to enforce it, arguing that it "would have stifled American innovation" and would have downgraded dozens of countries "to second-tier status" [@bis2025department]. A formal replacement was promised; in July 2026 the worldwide licence requirement still appeared in the regulations, but BIS stated that it was enforcing it only for certain destinations, mainly countries of concern such as China [@bis2026enhanced]. On the same day BIS warned companies about the risks of using certain Huawei Ascend chips and about the use of US chips to train Chinese models.

Policy towards China itself moved in both directions. In April 2025 the US government informed Nvidia that its H20 chip, a product designed for the Chinese market, now required an export licence; the company expected charges of up to about $5.5 billion on inventory and commitments [@nvidia2025form]. In January 2026 BIS changed its review policy for exports to China of the more powerful H200 and equivalent chips from a "presumption of denial" to "case-by-case review", subject to conditions such as independent testing in the United States and certification that US supply would not be reduced [@bis2026revision]. Nvidia reported, for its part, that the Chinese government had discouraged customers from buying its data-centre products, including those designed to comply with US rules [@nvidia2026form]. Meanwhile, the United States offered favourable access to some partners: a July 2026 rule gave the United Arab Emirates' government and approved companies licence-free access to advanced computing items [@bis2026enhanced].

Whether these controls achieve their aims is contested. Supporters point to evidence that Chinese developers are short of compute: the IEA describes China's model developers as "compute-constrained due to a lack of access to high-end chips" [@iea2026key, Box 10.1]. Critics note that constraints can spur workarounds. DeepSeek trained its V3 model on a cluster of 2,048 Nvidia H800 GPUs, a chip that could be exported to China without a licence until the October 2023 update [@deepseekai2024deepseek; @nvidia2023form]. The IEA observes that Chinese developers responded to scarcity with a strong focus on techniques that reduce computation [@iea2026key, Box 10.1]. The 2026 AI Index reports that the performance gap between the best US and Chinese models "has effectively closed", with the lead changing hands several times since early 2025 [@sajadieh2026ai, Report highlights]. Neither observation settles the question, because nobody can observe what Chinese labs would have achieved without the controls.

## 7.4 Data centres, electricity and water

A **data centre** is a building that houses servers, networking equipment, power supplies and cooling systems. Not all data centres serve AI: most of their electricity still goes to ordinary cloud computing, streaming, storage and business software. The most systematic estimates of their energy use come from the IEA, an intergovernmental energy agency, which published a dedicated special report, *Energy and AI*, with global and regional projections, in April 2025 [@iea2025energy] and an update, *Key Questions on Energy and AI*, in April 2026 [@iea2026key].

According to the 2026 update, global data-centre electricity consumption grew by 17% in 2025, reaching 485 terawatt-hours (TWh) [@iea2026key, Executive Summary]. A terawatt-hour is a billion kilowatt-hours; for scale, 485 TWh was about 1.5% of the world's electricity use. Consumption by data centres that focus on AI grew faster, by about 50%. The IEA's central projection, its Base Case, sees total data-centre consumption roughly doubling to about 950 TWh by 2030 (945 TWh in the report's data tables), around 3% of global electricity demand, with AI-focused data centres tripling their use [@iea2026key, Executive Summary and Annex A].

Figure 7.3 shows these figures together with the IEA's alternative scenarios. The IEA uses four "cases" because the future depends on uncertain factors: how fast AI is adopted, how quickly chips and models become more efficient, and whether bottlenecks in grids, turbines and chips ease. The Lift-Off Case assumes stronger adoption and resolved bottlenecks; the High Efficiency Case assumes the same demand as the Base Case but faster efficiency gains; the Headwinds Case assumes slower adoption and investment [@iea2026key, sec. 1]. The spread for 2030, from 833 to 1,008 TWh, is a reminder that these are projections under assumptions, not forecasts of a single outcome.

![Figure 7.3: Global data-centre electricity consumption, 2023–2025 (IEA estimates), and 2030 under four IEA cases. Data centres used about 1.5% of the world's electricity in 2025; the Base Case reaches about 3% by 2030. Source: @iea2026key, Annex A, Table A.1 (data published April 2026).](figures/fig-07-03.svg){#fig:07-03}

Three features of the IEA's analysis are worth stressing. First, the global share is modest, but the local share can be large. The IEA notes that while data centres used 1.5% of global electricity in 2025, they accounted for as much as 20–30% of demand in the states, metropolitan areas or regions where they cluster [@iea2026key, sec. 3]. Ireland is the clearest national case: official statistics show that data centres consumed 23% of the country's metered electricity in 2025, up from 5% in 2015 [@cso2026data]. Second, the growth is concentrated geographically. In the IEA's tables the United States accounts for 183 of the world's 416 TWh in 2024 and 426 of 945 TWh in the 2030 Base Case; China rises from 102 to 277 TWh and Europe from 68 to 114 TWh [@iea2026key, Table A.4]. Figure 7.4 compares these regions.

![Figure 7.4: Data-centre electricity consumption by region, 2024 and 2030 (IEA Base Case). The United States and China account for most of the projected growth. "Rest of world" is the world total minus the three named regions. Source: @iea2026key, Annex A, Table A.4 (data published April 2026).](figures/fig-07-04.svg){#fig:07-04}

Third, efficiency per task and total demand are moving in opposite directions. The IEA estimates that the energy used per AI task has fallen "by at least an order of magnitude annually in recent years", so that a simple text query now typically uses less electricity than running a television for the same length of time [@iea2026key, Executive Summary]. Yet new uses such as video generation, reasoning models and agents can consume "hundreds or thousands of times more energy per query" than simple text generation. Total demand therefore depends on the race between efficiency and uptake. Physical density is rising too: the power density of AI servers increased elevenfold between 2020 and 2025, and by 2027 a single server rack the size of a large refrigerator could have peak demand equal to that of 65 households.

The balance between training and use is also shifting. Training a model is a one-off cost, while answering users' queries (inference) continues for as long as the model is in service. The 2026 AI Index notes that once a model is deployed at scale, the cumulative energy needed to serve queries "can exceed the one-time cost of training within months" [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1]. It cites estimates that a short GPT-4o query uses about 0.42 watt-hours, compared with about 0.3 watt-hours for a conventional web search: small for one person, large when multiplied by hundreds of millions of queries a day. Such per-query figures are estimates by outside researchers, because most developers do not publish them, and they vary widely between models and prompt lengths. Seen against the whole economy, the IEA expects data centres to account for slightly less than 10% of the growth in global electricity demand to 2030, although some regions are much more affected [@iea2026key, sec. 1].

Estimates made before the IEA's work illustrate how difficult projection is. In 2023 Alex de Vries, a PhD candidate at the Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, estimated from expected AI server production that by 2027 AI could add 85 to 134 TWh a year to worldwide electricity consumption, and that if every Google search used AI it might need about as much electricity as Ireland (about 29 TWh a year) [@devries2023growing]. These were scenario calculations in a short commentary, not forecasts, and they helped prompt the more detailed modelling that followed.

Where the electricity comes from matters for emissions. The IEA reports that the tech sector signed around 40% of all corporate renewable power-purchase agreements worldwide in 2025, and that agreements between data-centre operators and planned small modular nuclear reactors grew from about 25 GW to 45 GW during 2025, although the first such reactors are not expected before about 2030 [@iea2026key, Executive Summary]. In the United States, where grid connections are slow, some developers are building gas-fired generation on site; the IEA estimates that 15–27 GW of such capacity might power data centres by 2030.

**Water** is the less visible resource. Data centres use water mainly for cooling, often by evaporation, and indirectly through the water used to generate their electricity. Two terms are important: **withdrawal** is water taken from a source, while **consumption** is the part that is not returned, mostly because it evaporates. @li2025making estimated that training GPT-3 in Microsoft's US data centres could have directly evaporated about 700,000 litres of fresh water, and projected that global AI demand might account for 4.2 to 6.6 billion cubic metres of water withdrawal in 2027. The authors stress that such estimates depend heavily on location, season and the electricity mix. The 2026 AI Index reports estimates that a year of GPT-4o inference consumes about 1.3 to 1.6 million cubic metres of water, at the high end exceeding the annual drinking-water needs of 1.2 million people [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1].

Company reports provide measured totals, although for whole companies rather than for AI alone. Google reported consuming about 31 billion litres of water across its own data centres and offices in 2024, an increase of 28% on 2023, and said that 72% of its freshwater withdrawals came from sources at low risk of depletion [@google2025environmental]. It also reported choosing air cooling, which uses little water, for a new site at Waltham Cross in England because local water sources were under stress. These are company claims, but they show that the water question is mostly local: the same litre matters more in a drought-prone basin than beside a large river.

## 7.5 Who can afford frontier AI? Costs and concentration

Chapter 4 described how the computation used to train leading models has grown. Here the question is what that growth costs and who can pay. @cottier2024rising, researchers at Epoch AI and Stanford, estimated the cost of training frontier models by spreading the price of the hardware over its useful life and adding the energy used (Going deeper box below). On this basis the cost of the most compute-intensive training runs had grown about 2.4 times a year since 2016 (preprint). GPT-4 had the largest amortised hardware and energy cost in their data set, at about $40 million, and Google's Gemini Ultra about $30 million. In their fuller breakdown of development costs, accelerator chips and staff were the largest components, with energy only 2–6%. If the trend continued, the largest training runs would cost more than a billion dollars by 2027, a level the authors argue would limit frontier development to the best-funded organisations.

Training also has an environmental cost that varies with hardware, duration and the carbon intensity of the electricity used. The AI Index estimates that training AlexNet in 2012 produced about 0.01 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent, while training xAI's Grok 4 in 2025 produced about 72,816 tonnes; DeepSeek's V3, a large model trained more efficiently, produced about 597 tonnes [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1]. These are model-based estimates, made necessary because, as the Index notes, many developers no longer disclose power use for their newest models.

These figures cover only the final training run. Staff, failed experiments and the often larger cost of serving a model to users are additional, and the 2026 AI Index notes that frontier developers now disclose less about their models than before [@sajadieh2026ai, Introduction]. Building the infrastructure costs far more. According to the IEA, leading cloud providers (the "hyperscalers") and newer specialised providers announced capital expenditure of about USD 715 billion for 2026, a 75% increase, more than the slightly less than USD 600 billion invested in the entire US energy sector in 2024 [@iea2026key, sec. 1]. Because this spending absorbs nearly all the firms' cash, they are turning increasingly to borrowing, which makes the build-out sensitive to financial markets.

The result is concentration. The AI Index reports that industry produced 91.2% of notable AI models in 2025 (93 models against 2 from academia), with OpenAI (20), Google (14) and Alibaba (11) the most prolific developers [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1]. By country, the United States produced 59 notable models and China 35. On the supply side, Nvidia reported that two direct customers alone accounted for 22% and 14% of its total revenue in fiscal year 2026 [@nvidia2026form]. The United States hosts 5,427 data centres, more than ten times any other country, followed by Germany (529), the United Kingdom (523) and China (449) [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1]. Concentration of this kind matters because a few firms' choices about pricing, access, safety practices and openness then shape what everyone else can build.

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: Estimating the cost of a training run**

The simplest estimate multiplies the time a run takes on its chips by a price per chip-hour:

*cost ≈ number of GPU-hours × rental price per GPU-hour*

DeepSeek reported that the full training of its V3 model took 2.788 million H800 GPU-hours. Assuming a rental price of $2 per GPU-hour, it calculated a cost of 2,788,000 × $2 = $5.576 million [@deepseekai2024deepseek]. The company stated explicitly that this excludes "the costs associated with prior research and ablation experiments". Cottier et al. use a different method: they take the purchase price of the hardware, divide it over the hardware's expected working life and charge the training run for the share of that life it used, then add energy [@cottier2024rising]. The two methods answer different questions. A rental-price estimate asks what it would cost someone to repeat the final run; an amortised estimate asks what share of a lab's capital the run consumed. Neither includes salaries, data or failed attempts, which is why headline training costs understate what it takes to build a frontier model.
:::

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: The "DeepSeek moment", January 2025**

In late December 2024 the Chinese company DeepSeek released the weights of DeepSeek-V3, a model with 671 billion parameters (37 billion active for each token), and reported the $5.576 million final-run cost described above [@deepseekai2024deepseek]. In January 2025 it released DeepSeek-R1, a reasoning model, under the permissive MIT licence, together with smaller "distilled" versions built on Alibaba's Qwen and Meta's Llama models [@deepseekai2025deepseekr1]. DeepSeek's chatbot app rose to the top of Apple's app store in the US and UK. On Monday 27 January 2025 Nvidia's shares fell 17%, removing nearly $600 billion from its market value, as investors questioned whether frontier AI would need as many expensive chips as expected [@milmo2025sputnik].

Later evidence complicated the story. When the R1 work was published in *Nature* in September 2025, DeepSeek's supplementary information gave the cost of turning the V3 base model into R1 (the two reinforcement-learning stages plus the creation of fine-tuning data) as about 147,000 H800 GPU-hours, or about $294,000 at the same assumed $2 rental price, and acknowledged using A100 GPUs for preparatory experiments [@guo2025deepseek]. The low headline figures were therefore real but partial: they excluded earlier research and the company's existing hardware. Nvidia's Data Center revenue continued to grow strongly through 2025 [@nvidia2026form]. The episode showed that algorithmic efficiency can reduce the cost of matching the frontier, and it drew wide attention to Chinese open-weight models; it did not show that compute had stopped mattering.
:::

## 7.6 Open-weight models

A trained model is, in the end, a very large file of numbers: its **weights** (also called parameters). A **closed-weight** model is kept on the developer's servers, and users reach it only through an app or an application programming interface (API), so the developer can monitor use, update the model and withdraw it. An **open-weight** model is one whose weights are published for anyone to download, run on their own hardware and modify, for example by further training ("fine-tuning") on their own data. Chapter 3 introduced the distinction; this section examines its consequences.

::: {.box .concept}
**Key concept: Open-weight is not the same as open source**

In software, "open source" means that the source code is available under a licence allowing anyone to use, study, modify and share it for any purpose. For AI, the Open Source Initiative's Open Source AI Definition (version 1.0, October 2024) requires those freedoms plus access to the parameters, the full training code and sufficiently detailed information about the training data for a skilled person to build a substantially equivalent system [@osi2024open]. Many "open" models release only weights under a custom licence. Meta's Llama 2 licence, for example, required any company whose products had more than 700 million monthly active users to request a separate licence from Meta [@meta2023llama]. "Open-weight" is therefore the more accurate general term.
:::

The modern wave of open-weight language models began in earnest when Meta released Llama 2 in July 2023, describing it as "free of charge for research and commercial use" [@meta2023meta]. In September 2023 the French start-up Mistral AI released Mistral 7B, a 7.3-billion-parameter model, under the permissive Apache 2.0 licence, claiming it outperformed the larger Llama 2 13B on all the benchmarks it reported [@mistral2023mistral]. Chinese developers became central to the open ecosystem: Alibaba's Qwen family and DeepSeek's models were widely adopted, and the IEA reports that in 2025 "China's models overtook those from developers in the United States in terms of total downloads on open-source platforms" [@iea2026key, Box 10.1]. In August 2025 OpenAI released two open-weight reasoning models, gpt-oss-120b and gpt-oss-20b, under Apache 2.0 [@openai2025gptoss].

How good are open models compared with closed ones? The AI Index tracks the gap on the Arena leaderboard, where people compare anonymous answers from two models and vote for the better one. In May 2023 the best closed model led the best open model by 174 points (15.2%); by August 2024 the gap had narrowed to 7 points (0.5%); by March 2026, after new closed reasoning models, it had widened again, with Claude Opus 4.6 at 1,503 points against GLM-5 at 1,454, a gap of 49 points (3.4%) [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 2]. On this measure the best open models trail the best closed ones by a small margin, although the gap has widened and narrowed as new models appear.

The most cited framework for weighing benefits and risks is by @kapoor2024position, 25 researchers from academia, industry and civil society. They identify five properties that distinguish open models, such as greater customisability and weak monitoring of use, and argue that these produce benefits for innovation, competition, transparency and the distribution of decision-making power. Researchers can inspect open weights, which closed models do not allow; businesses and governments can run models on their own computers without sending sensitive data to a vendor; and developers in countries without frontier labs can adapt models to local languages. The US government's 2025 AI Action Plan made similar points, adding that open models "have geostrategic value" because they may become global standards [@whitehouse2025winning].

The risks follow from the same properties. Once weights are published they cannot be recalled, and any safety training can be removed. @lermen2023lora showed that with "a budget of less than $200 and using only one GPU" they could fine-tune Meta's Llama 2-Chat 70B so that it refused harmful requests only about 1% of the time on two standard tests (preprint). @kapoor2024position argue that the key question is **marginal risk**: how much an open model adds to what a malicious actor could already do with search engines, existing tools or closed models. Their review found that "current research is insufficient to effectively characterize the marginal risk" across most misuse scenarios.

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Should the weights of the most capable models be released openly?**

*For openness by default.* @kapoor2024position argue that open models spread power beyond a few companies, enable independent safety research and support competition, and that most claimed misuse risks have not been shown to exceed those of existing technologies. In July 2024 the US National Telecommunications and Information Administration concluded that "current evidence is not sufficient to definitively determine" that restrictions on open-weight models were warranted, and recommended that government "actively monitor" risks instead [@ntia2024dual].

*For caution at the frontier.* @seger2023opensourcing (preprint) accept that open-sourcing has historically brought large net benefits, but argue that for some highly capable future models the risks may be extreme enough that such models "should not be open-sourced, at least not initially". They examine alternatives, such as ways of sharing models without publishing their weights. The ease of removing safety training [@lermen2023lora] supports their concern that release decisions are irreversible.

*What would settle it.* Both sides agree that the answer depends on evidence about marginal risk at particular capability levels. Careful evaluations of what open models add to specific harms, repeated as models improve, would allow decisions to rest on measured risk rather than on assumptions. Chapter 12 discusses such dangerous-capability evaluations.
:::

Open-weight models also interact with the rest of this chapter. They lower the cost of entry for firms and countries that cannot afford frontier training, which partly offsets concentration. They also complicate export controls: the January 2025 diffusion rule controlled the weights of certain advanced closed-weight models but left published weights outside its scope, because published information cannot be recalled [@bis2025framework].

## 7.7 Compute governance and sovereign AI

Since data and algorithms are hard to observe and easy to copy, some researchers argue that hardware is an especially practical lever for governing AI. @sastry2024computing, a group of 19 authors including Yoshua Bengio, argue that AI-relevant compute is a good point of intervention because it is "detectable, excludable, and quantifiable" and is produced through "an extremely concentrated supply chain" (preprint). Detectable means that large data centres and chip shipments are hard to hide; excludable means that access can be denied; quantifiable means that chips and computations can be counted. **Compute governance** is the use of these properties by public authorities to gain visibility into AI development, allocate resources and enforce rules.

Examples already exist. Export controls are one. Another is the use of training compute as a regulatory threshold: the EU AI Act presumes that a general-purpose AI model has "high impact capabilities", and is therefore subject to additional obligations, when the computation used for its training exceeds 10^25 floating-point operations [@eu2024regulation, Art. 51(2)]. Chapter 13 discusses these thresholds and their limits in detail. @sastry2024computing also warn that poorly designed compute governance carries risks to privacy, economic harms and an undesirable centralisation of power, and they propose guardrails such as collecting only the minimum information needed.

The same logic of control over compute underlies **sovereign AI**, the effort by governments to secure national access to AI infrastructure and models rather than depending entirely on foreign providers. The 2026 AI Index notes that "AI sovereignty emerged as a central organizing principle" in national policies during 2025 [@sajadieh2026ai, Introduction]. Strategies differ in scale and emphasis:

- **European Union.** In February 2025 the European Commission launched InvestAI, aiming to mobilise €200 billion for AI investment, including a €20 billion fund for four "AI gigafactories", each with around 100,000 of the latest AI chips [@ec2025eu].
- **India.** In March 2024 the Indian cabinet approved over Rs 10,300 crore over five years for the IndiaAI Mission, including a plan for public–private compute capacity of more than 10,000 GPUs [@pib2024cabinet].
- **United Kingdom.** The AI Opportunities Action Plan of January 2025 recommended expanding the country's public AI Research Resource "by at least 20x by 2030" and creating "AI Growth Zones" to speed up data-centre construction, while noting that sovereign compute would "almost certainly be the smallest component" of the country's total compute [@dsit2025ai].
- **United States.** The July 2025 AI Action Plan called for exporting the "full AI technology stack" (hardware, models, software, applications and standards) to allied countries, so that they would not become dependent on rivals' technology [@whitehouse2025winning].

The scale differences are large. US private AI investment reached $285.9 billion in 2025, against $12.4 billion in China, although the AI Index cautions that the Chinese figure omits much state funding [@sajadieh2026ai, Report highlights]. A single country's public programme of 10,000 GPUs is small next to the 17.1 million H100-equivalents of global capacity estimated in Section 7.1. For most countries, then, "sovereignty" means securing reliable access, building local data centres, or adapting open-weight models to national languages and needs. Training a frontier model from scratch remains out of reach.

Sovereign strategies also interact with the chokepoints described in Section 7.2. A country can build data centres on its own soil and still depend on Nvidia's designs, TSMC's fabs and ASML's machines, and those dependencies are shaped by US export policy, as the UAE arrangement of 2026 illustrates [@bis2026enhanced]. Open-weight models offer a partial route to independence at the model layer, which is one reason why governments as different as the United States and China have, for their own reasons, encouraged them [@whitehouse2025winning; @iea2026key, Box 10.1]. The physical layers of the stack are harder to replicate, and they are where the decisions of a few firms and governments continue to set the terms for everyone else.

## Summary

- Modern AI runs on accelerators such as GPUs and TPUs, which perform many simple calculations in parallel and depend on fast high-bandwidth memory; Nvidia supplies over 60% of estimated global AI compute.
- The chip supply chain contains severe chokepoints: ASML is the only maker of EUV lithography machines, and TSMC fabricates virtually every leading AI chip.
- Since October 2022 the United States has used export controls to restrict China's access to advanced chips; the 2025 global diffusion rule was abandoned before it took full effect, and in 2026 policy shifted to case-by-case licensing for some chips and favourable deals with partners. Their effectiveness is debated.
- The IEA estimates that data centres used 485 TWh (about 1.5% of world electricity) in 2025 and projects about 950 TWh (about 3%) by 2030 in its Base Case, with a range of 833–1,008 TWh across its cases; local impacts, as in Ireland, can be much larger.
- Water use is mainly local and mainly for cooling; estimates vary widely with location and method.
- Training costs for the largest runs have grown about 2.4-fold a year, and infrastructure spending by a few cloud providers runs to hundreds of billions of dollars, concentrating frontier development in industry and in a few firms.
- Open-weight models (Llama, Mistral, Qwen, DeepSeek, gpt-oss) trail the best closed models by a small margin and spread access widely, but their release is irreversible and their safety training can be removed cheaply; the key unresolved question is their marginal risk.
- Compute governance uses the detectability and concentration of hardware to regulate AI, and sovereign AI strategies seek national access to compute, usually by building infrastructure and adapting open models rather than training frontier systems.
