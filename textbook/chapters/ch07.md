# Chapter 7: Chips, Data Centres, Energy and Open Models

The government of Alberta, a Canadian province, set out to attract data centres. Requests to connect new data centres to its grid soon reached 16 gigawatts (GW), more than the province's entire peak electricity demand. In 2025 the grid operator imposed a temporary cap of 1.2 GW to protect the reliability of the system [@iea2026key, sec. 3; @aeso2025aeso]. A gigawatt is a little more than the output of the Three Mile Island nuclear unit that Microsoft has contracted to restart, about 835 megawatts [@iea2026key, sec. 5.1].

Every answer from a chatbot is computed on physical machines, in buildings that draw electricity from real grids, using chips made in a handful of factories with critical tools that, in the case of the most advanced lithography machines, only one company makes [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1; @asml2026annual]. That physical layer explains why AI is expensive, why a few firms dominate it and why chips have become an instrument of foreign policy [@bis2022implementation]. It also explains why releasing a model's weights is contested, because published weights escape the controls that apply to hardware [@bis2025framework].

## 7.1 How AI accelerators work

Training and running a neural network consists mostly of multiplying large matrices (Section 2.6), and the quantities are vast. By the rule of thumb derived in Section 4.1, pre-training the Chinese open-weight model DeepSeek-V3 took about 3.3 × 10^24^ floating-point operations (FLOP). That figure is the author's calculation from the 37 billion active parameters and 14.8 trillion training tokens reported by its developer [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3]. How fast a chip gets through such work depends as much on how quickly it can be fed with data as on its arithmetic units.

### Inside a GPU

A general-purpose central processing unit (CPU) devotes much of its silicon to running varied instructions quickly one after another. A graphics processing unit (GPU) instead contains many simpler processors that apply the same operation to many numbers at once, which suits the arithmetic of neural networks [@goodfellow2016deep, sec. 12.1.2]. Nvidia's H100, announced in March 2022, packs 80 billion transistors onto a die of 814 mm² made on a customised TSMC process called 4N, according to the company [@nvidia2022hopper; @andersch2022nvidia]. The H100 SXM5 version, a module mounted directly on a server board rather than a plug-in card, has 132 streaming multiprocessors (SMs), each with four **tensor cores**, 528 in all, which Nvidia's engineers describe as "specialized high-performance compute cores for matrix multiply and accumulate (MMA) math operations" [@andersch2022nvidia]. A tensor core multiplies small blocks of two matrices and adds the result to a running total as one operation, rather than as many separate instructions. Per SM and per clock cycle, the H100's tensor cores deliver twice the matrix throughput of its predecessor, the A100.

GPUs are one kind of **AI accelerator**, a chip specialised for the arithmetic of machine learning. Others were designed from the start for neural networks. Google's first Tensor Processing Unit (TPU), deployed in its data centres from 2015, was built around a large grid of 8-bit multiply-accumulate units. In a 2017 paper Google's engineers reported that on Google's own inference workloads it was on average about 15 to 30 times faster than the contemporary GPU and CPU it was compared with [@jouppi2017indatacenter]. The comparison was with an Nvidia K80; later GPUs narrowed the gap, and Google reported its fourth-generation TPU as 1.2 to 1.7 times faster than Nvidia's A100 in systems of similar size [@jouppi2023tpu].

### Memory bandwidth: the usual bottleneck

The weights of a large model do not fit in the small, fast memory on the processor die, so they sit in **high-bandwidth memory (HBM)**. HBM consists of stacks of memory chips placed in the same package as the processor and joined to it by thousands of wires. The H100 SXM5 has five HBM3 stacks connected through ten 512-bit memory controllers, a 5,120-bit interface [@andersch2022nvidia]. Nvidia's product specification gives 80 GB of memory and a bandwidth of 3.35 terabytes per second (TB/s) [@nvidia2026h100].

The balance between arithmetic and memory speed decides how fast real workloads run. Engineers measure it as **arithmetic intensity**, the number of operations a workload performs for each byte it reads from memory [@gholami2024ai]; a "roofline" plot shows a chip's attainable speed rising with intensity until it hits the ceiling of peak arithmetic [@williams2009roofline]. The H100 can perform about 990 trillion 16-bit operations per second in ordinary ("dense") matrix arithmetic, so it runs at full speed only on work with an intensity of roughly 300 operations per byte (calculation by the author from the specification). Generating text one token at a time falls far short of that. Each 16-bit weight occupies two bytes and is used for one multiplication and one addition per token, so a single user's request performs only about one operation per byte. A 70-billion-parameter model stored at 16 bits occupies 140 GB, more than one H100's 80 GB, so it must be split across at least two GPUs. Reading the weights once at one H100's bandwidth would take about 42 milliseconds; spread over two GPUs, it takes about 21 milliseconds per token for a single user, however many arithmetic units sit idle. Long inputs add to the traffic, because at each step the model also rereads the intermediate results it has stored for every earlier token, its "KV cache" [@kwon2023efficient]. Serving systems therefore process many users' requests together ("batching") so that each weight fetched from memory is used many times.

This imbalance has grown. In an analysis published in *IEEE Micro*, Gholami and colleagues found that over the past 20 years peak server hardware FLOPS rose about 3.0 times every two years, against 1.6 times for memory bandwidth and 1.4 times for interconnect bandwidth. They conclude that memory bandwidth has become the main bottleneck for decoder models such as chatbots, particularly when serving them [@gholami2024ai]. The same problem was visible in the first TPU, whose designers estimated that equipping it with the faster memory of the GPU it was compared with would have tripled its achieved performance [@jouppi2017indatacenter].

### Number formats

Fewer bits per number mean more numbers per second through the same memory, more operations per unit of chip area and less energy per operation. The price is less range (how large and small a number can be) and less precision (how finely values are distinguished). A floating-point number has a sign bit, exponent bits that set its range and mantissa bits that set its precision [@micikevicius2022fp8; @kalamkar2019study]. Table 7.1 compares the formats used in AI.

| Format | Sign, exponent, mantissa bits | Largest normal value | Main use in AI |
|---|---|---|---|
| FP32 | 1, 8, 23 | 3.40 × 10^38^ | Master copy of weights; accumulation |
| FP16 | 1, 5, 10 | 65,504 | Mixed-precision training since 2017 |
| BF16 | 1, 8, 7 | 3.39 × 10^38^ | Training without loss scaling |
| FP8 (E4M3) | 1, 4, 3 | 448 | Weights and activations |
| FP8 (E5M2) | 1, 5, 2 | 57,344 | Gradients, which need more range |

: Table 7.1: Floating-point formats used in AI computing. More exponent bits give more range, more mantissa bits give more precision. Largest normal values computed by the author from the bit layouts. Source: [@micikevicius2017mixed; @kalamkar2019study; @micikevicius2022fp8]. {#tbl:07-01}

Each step down the table was made usable by a training technique. In 2017, researchers from Baidu and Nvidia showed that networks could be trained with weights, activations and gradients stored in 16-bit FP16, provided a 32-bit master copy of the weights accumulated the updates. Without it, the smallest updates would be rounded to zero. They also multiplied the loss by a scaling factor so that small gradients stayed within FP16's range [@micikevicius2017mixed]. The bfloat16 (BF16) format, first used mainly within Google's ecosystem, keeps FP32's 8 exponent bits and so its range, giving up precision instead. A 2019 preprint by Intel and Facebook researchers found that BF16 training matched FP32 results across image, speech, language and recommendation tasks without changing hyperparameters [@kalamkar2019study].

Eight-bit floating point (FP8) followed. A 2022 preprint by Nvidia, Arm and Intel engineers defined two encodings, E4M3 and E5M2, and reported that FP8 training matched 16-bit results on models of up to 175 billion parameters [@micikevicius2022fp8]. Unlike the A100, the H100 supports FP8 in its tensor cores, which, according to the company, "halves data storage requirements and doubles throughput compared to FP16 or BF16" [@andersch2022nvidia]. DeepSeek-V3's developers describe it as the first validation of FP8 training on an "extremely large-scale model". They scaled values in small blocks so that occasional extreme values did not swamp the others. They also worked around a limitation of the Hopper architecture's tensor cores, which add up FP8 products using only about 14 bits of precision [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3]. The limitation is shared by the H100 and is not a product of export controls, although their chip, the H800, was a Hopper GPU that could be sold to China only until the US tightened its export rules in 2023 (Section 7.3) [@nvidia2023form]. They reported that the relative loss error of FP8-trained test models, trained on about a trillion tokens, stayed below 0.25% of BF16 baselines [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3]. Nvidia's next architecture, Blackwell, added 4-bit floating point with scaling factors applied to small blocks of numbers [@nvidia2024blackwell].

Precision is also the main trap in reading chip specifications. Nvidia quotes the H100's 16-bit performance as 1,979 TFLOPS "with sparsity", a figure that depends on a feature exploiting zero-valued weights; for its newer systems it states that dense performance is half the sparse figure [@nvidia2026h100; @nvidia2026hgx]. Google described its 2025 Ironwood TPU pod as offering "over 24x the compute power" of El Capitan, then the world's largest supercomputer [@vahdat2025ironwood]. El Capitan's 1.742 exaflops were measured with the High-Performance Linpack (HPL) benchmark, which times the solution of a huge system of equations in double-precision (64-bit) arithmetic [@top5002025june; @top5002026linpack], whereas Google's chart notes indicate that Ironwood's figures refer to 8-bit arithmetic, so the comparison sets unlike quantities against each other.

### Interconnects and systems

Training a frontier model requires thousands of accelerators to exchange partial results constantly, so the links between chips limit performance as well as the chips themselves. Within a server, Nvidia's NVLink connects GPUs directly. The H100 has 18 NVLink links giving 900 GB/s in total, seven times the 128 GB/s of the standard PCIe Gen 5 connection [@andersch2022nvidia]. Between servers, data travels over networks such as InfiniBand. DeepSeek-V3, for example, was trained on 2,048 H800 GPUs arranged in nodes of eight, linked by NVLink within each node and InfiniBand between nodes [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3].

The Blackwell GPU, announced in March 2024, joins two dies, each as large as the lithography machines can expose in one pass (the "reticle limit"), through a 10 TB/s chip-to-chip link into a single GPU (Table 7.2). Its GB200 NVL72 rack combines 72 such GPUs and, according to the company, delivers 1.4 exaflops of "AI performance" with 30 TB of fast memory [@nvidia2024blackwell]. That figure counts 4-bit operations and assumes sparsity; the dense figure is half as large, and neither is comparable with the 16-bit numbers in Table 7.2 [@nvidia2026gb200]. According to the company, Blackwell's successor, Rubin, carries up to 288 GB of HBM4 memory per GPU with up to 22 TB/s of bandwidth, which the company describes as nearly triple Blackwell's [@aubrey2026inside]; Nvidia said in August 2026 that the Vera Rubin platform was in full production [@nvidia2026nvidia].

Google has taken a different route to scale. Its TPU v4 supercomputer of 4,096 chips uses optical circuit switches that can reconfigure the network's topology at small cost, according to Google's engineers [@jouppi2023tpu]. Ironwood, announced in April 2025, scales a pod to 9,216 chips [@vahdat2025ironwood].

The International Energy Agency (IEA) estimates that over the past decade leading AI processors improved performance per watt by roughly 30–40% a year, with gains slowing as transistor scaling matures [@iea2026key, sec. 2.2]. Google states that Ironwood doubles the performance per watt of its previous TPU [@vahdat2025ironwood]. But each chip draws more power. An H100 SXM is rated at up to 700 W, against 400 W for the A100 [@andersch2022nvidia]. Measurements cited by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory found that eight-GPU H100 servers running AI training averaged 74% of their rated power [@shehabi2024united].

The 2026 AI Index, drawing on Epoch AI's data, puts worldwide AI computing capacity at roughly 17.1 million "H100-equivalents" in 2025, meaning the number of H100 chips that would supply the same computing power. Capacity has grown about 3.3 times a year since 2022 [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1; @epochai2026global]. Nvidia chips account for over 60%, with Google and Amazon supplying much of the remainder and Huawei a small but growing share [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1]. The commercial weight of this demand is visible in Nvidia's accounts. In its fiscal year ending January 2026 the company reported revenue of $215.9 billion, of which sales to the data-centre market contributed $193.7 billion, up from $47.5 billion two years earlier [@nvidia2026form].

## 7.2 The semiconductor supply chain and its chokepoints

A chip passes through several specialised industries before it reaches a data centre, and the AI Index describes high barriers to entry at every layer, with each stage dominated by very few firms [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1]. Figure 7.1 shows the stack, from the machines that make chips at the bottom to the applications people use at the top.

Schematic by the author, based on @sajadieh2026ai, @trendforce2025eda, @asml2026annual, @tsmc2026annual and @bis2022eda.](figures/fig-07-01.svg){#fig:07-01}

### Design software

A modern chip with tens of billions of transistors can be designed only with **electronic design automation (EDA)** software, and three firms dominate it. According to the market-research firm TrendForce, Synopsys and Cadence of the United States and Siemens EDA, part of Germany's Siemens, held about 74% of the world market in 2024 [@trendforce2025eda]. EDA has already served as a lever of policy. From October 2022 the United States controlled exports of software for designing chips with the newest "gate-all-around" transistors [@bis2022eda]. In May 2025 the Commerce Department told Cadence that it needed a licence to export its design software to China, then rescinded the requirement on 2 July 2025 [@cadence2025form].

### Lithography

Chips are made by projecting circuit patterns onto light-sensitive coatings on silicon wafers, layer after layer; the finest features need the shortest wavelengths. **Extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) lithography** uses light with a wavelength of 13.5 nanometres, and ASML of the Netherlands states that it is "currently the world's only manufacturer of EUV lithography systems" [@asml2026annual]. ASML recognised revenue on 48 EUV systems in 2025, out of 327 lithography systems sold, on total net sales of €32.7 billion. The next generation, "High-NA" EUV with a larger lens aperture for finer features, shipped its first production-ready system in 2025, and ASML expects it to support high-volume manufacturing from 2027.

ASML in turn depends on others. Carl Zeiss SMT is its sole supplier of lenses, mirrors and other critical optics, under an exclusive arrangement. ASML warns that the number of systems it can build "is limited by the production capacity" of Zeiss [@asml2026annual]. ASML needs export licences for EUV systems and for certain deep-ultraviolet (DUV) immersion systems, the previous generation of tools. China nonetheless remains a large market for older tools, and customers there accounted for 29.1% of ASML's 2025 net sales, down from 36.1% in 2024.

### Fabrication

Above lithography sits **fabrication**. Firms such as Nvidia, AMD and Google design chips but do not make them; they send their designs to foundries. The Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC) dominates the leading edge. According to the 2026 AI Index, it "fabricates virtually every leading AI chip", including Nvidia's Blackwell GPUs and AMD's MI300X [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1]. AI has reshaped TSMC's business. High-performance computing, the category that includes AI accelerators, supplied 58% of its 2025 revenue, up from 43% in 2023 [@tsmc2026annual].

The concentration runs in both directions. TSMC's ten largest customers provided 78% of its 2025 revenue, and the largest alone almost a fifth [@tsmc2026annual]. TSMC is building fabs in Arizona, Japan and Germany, yet the AI Index still describes the AI hardware supply chain as dependent on one foundry in Taiwan [@tsmc2026annual; @sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1]. That dependence is also a security risk. The consultancy Rhodium Group estimated in 2022 that a Chinese blockade of Taiwan would put "well over two trillion dollars in economic activity at risk", mostly by halting Taiwan's trade with the world, before counting sanctions or a military response [@vest2022global].

### Memory and packaging

After fabrication, chips are **packaged**. The processor dies and their HBM stacks are combined in one package that supplies power, removes heat and carries data between them. Nvidia's annual report states that it uses TSMC's CoWoS ("chip on wafer on substrate") technology for this step, in which the dies are mounted side by side on a thin slice of silicon that carries thousands of fine connections between them. TSMC describes CoWoS as a service that integrates processor dies and HBM stacks "to enhance HPC products with superior compute power and memory bandwidth" [@nvidia2026form; @tsmc2026annualreport]. Packaging and testing are also done by specialist firms such as ASE Group in Taiwan and Amkor Technology in the United States. The leading HBM makers are SK Hynix and Samsung of South Korea and Micron of the United States [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1].

These later stages became the binding constraints of the AI build-out. The IEA reports that packaging capacity for high-end chips limited production in 2025. From the second half of 2025, memory became the tighter limit [@iea2026key, sec. 1]. Leading GPU systems are expected to carry more than 1,000 GB of memory by 2027, against less than 150 GB in 2023. Producing HBM takes about three times as much wafer capacity per gigabyte as conventional memory, and memory prices rose by an order of magnitude between late 2024 and early 2026 [@iea2026key, sec. 1]. The IEA expects the shortage to last "through at least the end of 2027" [@iea2026key, Executive Summary].

Disruptions elsewhere propagate quickly. The IEA described how a conflict in the Middle East in 2026 disrupted supplies of helium from Qatar, which had supplied roughly a third of the world's helium. Helium is used to cool and clean chip-making equipment and has, according to the IEA, no substitute with equivalent properties [@iea2026key, Box 1.1]. Power equipment is also scarce. Lead times for transformers average two to three years, and gas turbine deliveries can take around five years [@iea2026key, sec. 1]; orders for gas turbines rose 70% in 2025 [@iea2026key, Executive Summary].

::: {.box .concept}
**Concept: Chokepoint**

A chokepoint is a stage in a supply chain that very few suppliers control and that others cannot easily replace. In AI hardware the clearest examples are design software (three firms), EUV lithography (one supplier, itself dependent on one optics maker), leading-edge fabrication (mainly one foundry), and HBM and advanced packaging (three memory makers and limited packaging lines) [@trendforce2025eda; @asml2026annual; @sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1; @iea2026key, sec. 1]. Chokepoints give whoever controls them leverage, which is why export controls target them [@bis2022implementation; @bis2024foreignproduced]. They also create shared vulnerability. A shortage, accident or conflict at one point can delay production worldwide, as the helium disruption of 2026 showed, and the leverage cuts both ways, since China controls chokepoints of its own in processed minerals (Section 7.3) [@iea2026key, Box 1.1; @cset2024notice].
:::

## 7.3 Export controls and the geopolitics of compute

The most important firms in the chain are based in the United States or allied countries. That has allowed the US government to use **export controls**, legal requirements to obtain a licence before selling certain goods abroad, to limit which countries can obtain advanced AI chips and chip-making tools. The US rules are written by the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1; @bis2022implementation]. Figure 7.2 summarises the main steps.

### Drawing the line

The first major rule, effective on 7 October 2022, imposed licence requirements on advanced computing chips, on computers containing them and on certain chip-manufacturing equipment, mainly aimed at China [@bis2022implementation]. It defined a controlled chip by two tests together: processing performance (bit length multiplied by trillions of operations per second) of 4,800 or more, and an aggregate chip-to-chip data transfer rate of 600 GB/s or more. A chip that fell below either line was free to ship. Chip designers responded with China-specific products designed to comply [@nvidia2026form].

The October 2023 revision dropped the transfer-rate test. It replaced it with **total processing performance** (TPP): twice the peak multiply-accumulate rate in trillions per second, multiplied by the bit length of the operation. Chips with a TPP of 4,800 or more are controlled. So are chips with a TPP of 1,600 or more whose "performance density" (TPP per square millimetre of die) is 5.92 or more, and a second tier catches somewhat less dense chips [@bis2023implementation]. The rule counts dense, not sparse, performance. By the H100's dense 8-bit figure, its TPP is about 1,979 × 8 ≈ 15,800 and its density about 19 per mm², far above both lines (calculation by the author). Nvidia told investors that the revised rule required licences for exports to China and several other countries of chips "including but not limited to the A100, A800, H100, H800, L40, L40S, and RTX 4090" [@nvidia2023form]. The A800 and H800, sold in China under the first rule, were now caught. In December 2024 BIS added further controls on chip-making equipment and, for the first time, on certain high-bandwidth memory [@bis2024foreignproduced].

The rules have kept chasing the bottleneck that matters. In April 2025 the US government told Nvidia that its H20, a chip designed for the Chinese market, needed a licence. The requirement covered "any other circuits achieving the H20's memory bandwidth, interconnect bandwidth, or combination thereof" [@nvidia2026form]. That wording reflects the lesson of Section 7.1 that, for running models, memory bandwidth can matter more than peak arithmetic. Nvidia took a $4.5 billion charge for excess H20 inventory and purchase obligations. In August 2025 it received licences for certain H20 sales, generating about $60 million in revenue. According to Nvidia's annual report, US officials said they expected the government to receive 15% or more of the revenue from licensed sales of its products, but no regulation codifying this was published. Its revenue from customers headquartered in China (including Hong Kong) fell from $25.0 billion in fiscal 2025 to $19.7 billion in fiscal 2026 [@nvidia2026form].

### The diffusion rule and after

In January 2025 the outgoing Biden administration published a broader **Framework for Artificial Intelligence Diffusion**. It extended licence requirements for advanced chips to most of the world and sorted countries into tiers with different levels of access. It also added a new control on the weights of certain advanced closed-weight AI models [@bis2025framework]. On 13 May 2025, two days before the compliance date, the new administration announced that it would rescind the rule, which it said "would have stifled American innovation", instructed officials not to enforce it and promised a replacement [@bis2025department]. In July 2026 the worldwide licence requirement still appeared in the regulations, but BIS stated that it was enforcing it only for certain destinations, mainly countries of concern such as China [@bis2026enhanced]. It had clarified in May 2026 that exports to companies headquartered in China, or whose ultimate parent is, still needed a licence wherever those companies were located [@bis2026guidance].

Policy then moved in several directions at once (Figure 7.2).

- *Sales to China.* In January 2026 BIS changed its review policy for exports to China of Nvidia's H200 and equivalent chips from a "presumption of denial" to "case-by-case review", subject to independent testing in the United States [@bis2026revision]. Nvidia reported that by the quarter ending July 2026 it had made only a fraction of the permitted shipments, partly because the Chinese government had discouraged customers from buying its data-centre products [@nvidia2026formq]. Its outlook for the following quarter assumed no data-centre revenue from China [@nvidia2026nvidia].
- *Ownership rules and the trade truce.* The **Entity List** names foreign firms that need a licence to receive US goods. In September 2025 BIS extended its restrictions automatically to any company at least 50% owned by listed entities [@bis2025expansion], then suspended that rule for a year from 10 November 2025 as part of a trade truce in which China agreed to suspend sweeping new export controls on rare earths [@bis2025one; @whitehouse2025fact]. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said in September 2026 that the truce would be extended to 10 January, according to the Associated Press [@ap2026trump]; the suspension of the ownership rule was due to lapse on 9 November 2026 unless extended [@bis2025one].
- *Partner deals.* A July 2026 rule gave the United Arab Emirates' government and approved companies licence-free access to advanced computing items [@bis2026enhanced].
- *Allied controls.* The Netherlands added metrology and inspection equipment to its controls in January 2025, and in November 2025 the European Union extended Dutch licence requirements, including for DUV immersion lithography, to all member states [@asml2026annual].

China holds chokepoints of its own. In December 2024 its commerce ministry announced that exports to the United States of gallium and germanium, materials used in some semiconductors, would "in principle" not be permitted [@cset2024notice]; it suspended that ban in November 2025, until November 2026, as part of the same truce [@evertiq2025china].

### Do the controls work?

The arguments come largely from interested parties. Supporters point to signs that Chinese developers are short of compute; the IEA describes China's model developers as "compute-constrained due to a lack of access to high-end chips" [@iea2026key, Box 10.1]. Dario Amodei, chief executive of the US developer Anthropic, argued in January 2025 that export controls "serve a vital purpose" and that, well enforced, they "are the only thing that can prevent China from getting millions of chips" [@amodei2025deepseek]. His company competes with Chinese developers and has lobbied for tighter controls [@anthropic2025securing]. On the other side, Nvidia's chief executive Jensen Huang, whose company had lost much of its Chinese market, called the controls "a failure" in May 2025, according to Reuters, which noted that they had pushed Chinese buyers towards domestic designers such as Huawei [@reuters2025nvidia].

The evidence shows workarounds that are real but limited. DeepSeek trained V3 on H800s, which the first rule had left uncontrolled [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3; @nvidia2023form]. Smuggling has continued. In December 2025 the US Justice Department announced that a Houston company had pleaded guilty to exporting at least $160 million worth of H100 and H200 chips in breach of the rules [@doj2025us]. Renting chips in data centres abroad was not itself covered; a bill to bring such remote access under export controls passed the House of Representatives in January 2026 but had not become law [@congress2026remote]. Chinese substitutes are growing from a low base, and estimates of their output vary widely. A US Commerce official told Congress in June 2025 that Huawei would be able to make just 200,000 of its Ascend AI chips that year, according to Bloomberg [@bloomberg2025us]; Bloomberg later reported, citing unnamed sources, that Huawei planned up to 1.6 million Ascend chip dies in 2026, including dies already in stock [@bloomberg2025huawei]. Even if each Huawei chip matched an H100, either figure would be small next to worldwide capacity of 17.1 million H100-equivalents (Section 7.1). Meanwhile the 2026 AI Index reports that the performance gap between the best US and Chinese models "has effectively closed" [@sajadieh2026ai, Report highlights]. None of this settles the question, because nobody can observe what Chinese labs would have achieved without the controls.

## 7.4 Data centres, electricity and water

A **data centre** is a building that houses servers, networking equipment, power supplies and cooling systems; much of their electricity still goes to ordinary cloud computing, streaming and storage rather than AI. Few operators publish measured figures for AI workloads, so most numbers here are estimates whose methods and boundaries differ. The most systematic global estimates come from the IEA, an intergovernmental energy agency, in its April 2025 special report *Energy and AI* [@iea2025energy] and its April 2026 update, *Key Questions on Energy and AI* [@iea2026key].

### How much electricity

According to the 2026 update, global data-centre electricity consumption grew by 17% in 2025, reaching 485 terawatt-hours (TWh) [@iea2026key, Executive Summary]. A terawatt-hour is a billion kilowatt-hours; 485 TWh was about 1.5% of the world's electricity use [@iea2026key, sec. 3]. Consumption by data centres that focus on AI grew by about 50%. The IEA's Base Case sees total data-centre consumption roughly doubling to about 950 TWh by 2030, around 3% of global electricity demand, with AI-focused data centres tripling their use [@iea2026key, Executive Summary and Annex A].

Figure 7.3 shows these figures with the IEA's alternative cases. The Lift-Off Case assumes stronger adoption and resolved bottlenecks in grids, turbines and chips; the High Efficiency Case assumes Base Case demand with faster efficiency gains; the Headwinds Case assumes slower adoption and investment [@iea2026key, sec. 1]. The 2030 spread, from 833 to 1,008 TWh, is a range of projections under assumptions, not a forecast.

Source: @iea2026key, Annex A, Table A.1 (data published April 2026).](figures/fig-07-03.svg){#fig:07-03}

The global share is modest, but the local share can be large. The IEA notes that data centres accounted for as much as 20–30% of demand in the states, metropolitan areas or regions where they cluster [@iea2026key, sec. 3]. Ireland is the clearest national case. Official statistics show that data centres consumed 23% of the country's metered electricity in 2025, up from 5% in 2015 [@cso2026data]. Growth is also concentrated by country. In the IEA's tables the United States accounts for 183 of the world's 416 TWh in 2024 and for 426 of 945 TWh in the 2030 Base Case, with China second and Europe well behind (Figure 7.4) [@iea2026key, Table A.4]. Seen against the whole economy, the IEA expects data centres to account for slightly less than 10% of the growth in global electricity demand to 2030 [@iea2026key, sec. 1].

An independent US estimate comes from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL), whose December 2024 report for the Department of Energy adds up the installed base of servers, storage, networking and cooling [@shehabi2024united; @doe2024doe]. US data-centre use was flat at about 60 TWh a year from 2014 to 2016, then rose to 176 TWh (4.4% of US electricity) in 2023 as GPU-accelerated servers spread. That is about 14% above the IEA's estimate of 154 TWh for the same year, a gap that reflects different boundaries and methods [@iea2026key, Table A.4]. In LBNL's data, inference accounted for nearly 60% of AI server energy in 2023. For 2028 the report gives a range of roughly 325 to 580 TWh, or 6.7% to 12% of US electricity. The top of the range is almost twice the bottom, and the authors stress that "the lack of data availability significantly limits the analysis" [@shehabi2024united].

The only figures from a provider's own infrastructure come from Google. In a preprint by its staff, Google measured the median text prompt to its Gemini Apps in May 2025 at 0.24 watt-hours (Wh). Counting only the active accelerators, on a sample of highly utilised machines, as many earlier studies did, gave 0.10 Wh. The company reports that energy per prompt fell 33-fold in a year [@elsworth2025measuring]. A median says nothing about the long tail of long or complex prompts, and the measurement cannot be checked from outside. OpenAI's chief executive Sam Altman wrote in June 2025 that "the average query uses about 0.34 watt-hours", without describing the method [@altman2025gentle].

Outside estimates show how sensitive the figure is to assumptions. Epoch AI, a research institute, estimated about 0.3 Wh for a typical GPT-4o query. It assumed 500 output tokens and H100 GPUs running at 10% computational utilisation; different plausible assumptions gave anything from about 0.1 to 4 Wh. Long inputs change the picture. By the same method a 10,000-token document would cost about 2.5 Wh [@you2025much]. The widely quoted older figure of about 3 Wh comes from a 2023 commentary by Alex de Vries [@devries2023growing; @you2025much]. Epoch traces it mainly to an assumption of 2,000 output tokens per query, together with older A100 servers and peak rather than average power.

Other kinds of output cost far more. In a peer-reviewed study of openly available models, Luccioni, Jernite and Strubell found that generating an image used on average more than 60 times as much energy as generating text [@luccioni2024power]. The IEA adds that video generation can require "hundreds to thousands of times more energy" than a short text query [@iea2026key, sec. 2.1].

Efficiency and demand are moving in opposite directions. The IEA estimates that energy per AI task has fallen "by at least an order of magnitude annually in recent years" [@iea2026key, Executive Summary]. Best-in-class hyperscale facilities now achieve a power usage effectiveness (PUE, total facility energy divided by IT energy) of about 1.1 to 1.2, against 1.5 to 1.7 in older or enterprise sites. Moving from 1.6 to 1.2 cuts total energy use by about a quarter [@iea2026key, sec. 2.2]. Yet the IEA also notes that cheaper AI encourages more use, an instance of the Jevons paradox, so total consumption is set to rise even with rapid efficiency gains [@iea2026key, sec. 2].

### Where the power comes from, and emissions

Grid connection waiting times can reach five to ten years in many jurisdictions, and requests to connect are a poor guide to what will be built. The IEA warns that such queues are "not a reliable indicator of future demand, as only a limited and uncertain share of the pipeline may ultimately be realised" [@iea2026key, sec. 1]. Local opposition is growing. In the second quarter of 2025 alone, 20 US data-centre projects worth an estimated $98 billion were blocked or delayed amid concerns about their environmental and price impacts [@iea2026key, sec. 1]. Those price concerns have a basis. In PJM, which runs the grid across much of the eastern United States, the independent market monitor concluded in June 2025 that record capacity prices, which feed into consumers' bills, were "almost entirely the result of large load additions from data centers" [@appa2025pjm].

The tech sector signed around 40% of all corporate renewable power-purchase agreements, long-term contracts to buy electricity from particular generators, worldwide in 2025 [@iea2026key, Executive Summary]. In the United States some developers are building gas-fired generation on site; the IEA estimates that 15–27 GW of such capacity might power data centres by 2030 [@iea2026key, Executive Summary]. On-site turbines bring local air pollution. At xAI's Colossus data centre in Memphis, aerial photographs commissioned by the Southern Environmental Law Center, an environmental legal group, showed 35 gas turbines in April 2025, while the company had applied for permits for 15; the county health department approved 15 in July 2025, a decision the group appealed [@selc2026inside].

Nuclear deals have multiplied. Microsoft agreed in 2024 to buy power from a restarted Three Mile Island unit, and Amazon Web Services agreed in 2025 to take power from the Susquehanna plant. Agreements for small modular reactor capacity for data centres had reached about 45 GW by January 2026, although the first commercial units are not expected before about 2030 [@iea2026key, sec. 5.1]. Whatever the mix, the IEA projects that emissions associated with data centres will roughly double, reaching around 350 million tonnes of carbon dioxide in 2035, about 2% of the electricity sector's emissions [@iea2026key, Executive Summary].

Company emissions figures depend on an accounting choice. **Market-based** emissions subtract the clean electricity a company has contracted to buy, while **location-based** emissions use the average carbon intensity of the grids that actually supply its buildings. Google's figures show how far apart the two can be. According to the company, its data centres' electricity use grew by 38% in 2025, while its market-based emissions from purchased electricity fell by 3%, to about 2.8 million tonnes. On the location basis the same emissions rose to about 15.1 million tonnes, nearly three times the 2019 level [@google2026environmental, pp. 90, 93]. Both follow recognised rules; they answer different questions, and headlines usually quote the lower one. Google's reported 44-fold cut in emissions per prompt likewise combines efficiency gains with clean-energy purchasing [@elsworth2025measuring].

Training emissions vary with hardware, duration and the grid. The AI Index estimates that training AlexNet in 2012 produced about 0.01 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent, xAI's Grok 4 in 2025 about 73,000 tonnes and DeepSeek-V3 about 597 tonnes [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1]. These are model-based estimates, made necessary because, as the Index notes, many developers no longer disclose power use for their newest models.

Mistral AI published a life-cycle assessment carried out with the consultancy Carbone 4 and ADEME, the French ecological transition agency. According to the company, a 400-token response from its Mistral Large 2 model caused 1.14 grams of emissions and used 45 millilitres of water, including a share of hardware manufacturing [@mistral2025our].

### Water

**Water** is the less visible resource. Data centres use water mainly for cooling, often by evaporation, and indirectly through the water used to generate their electricity. **Withdrawal** is water taken from a source; **consumption** is the part that is not returned, mostly because it evaporates. LBNL estimates that US data centres directly consumed 21.2 billion litres in 2014 and 66 billion litres in 2023; hyperscale facilities alone could consume 60 to 124 billion litres in 2028 [@shehabi2024united].

Estimates for AI specifically are rougher. @li2025making estimated that training GPT-3 in Microsoft's US data centres could have directly evaporated about 700,000 litres of fresh water. Counting both cooling water and water used to generate electricity, they projected that global AI demand might account for 4.2 to 6.6 billion cubic metres of water withdrawal in 2027. The 2026 AI Index reports estimates that a year of GPT-4o inference consumes about 1.3 to 1.6 million cubic metres, at the high end more than the annual drinking-water needs of 1.2 million people [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1].

Per-query water figures differ by two orders of magnitude, partly because of what they count and partly because models, response lengths and sites differ. Google's 0.26 millilitres for a median Gemini text prompt covers only cooling water at its own data centres [@elsworth2025measuring]; Mistral's 45 millilitres per response also includes upstream impacts such as hardware manufacturing [@mistral2025our]. Google reported consuming about 41 billion litres across its data centres and offices worldwide in 2025, 34% more than in 2024 [@google2026environmental, p. 35]. It had chosen air cooling for a new site at Waltham Cross in England because local water sources were under stress [@google2025environmental]. The same litre matters more in a drought-prone basin than beside a large river, so national totals say little about local impact. A peer-reviewed study of the 9,347 data centres it could identify worldwide found that a quarter were in places where, under the hydrological conditions of 2001–2010, water demand would have exceeded supply for at least one month a year; under projected future conditions the share rises to a third [@rosa2026one].

## 7.5 Who can afford frontier AI? Costs and concentration

Section 4.4 sets out what the largest training runs cost and how fast that cost has grown. Much of that account rests on a preprint by Cottier and colleagues at Epoch AI and Stanford. In their data set GPT-4 had the largest amortised hardware and energy cost, about $40 million in 2023 dollars. Extrapolating their growth rate, the authors expected the largest training runs to cost more than a billion dollars by 2027, so that only the best-funded organisations could afford them [@cottier2024rising]. Later estimates bear this out. Epoch's database puts the amortised cost of xAI's Grok 4 run in 2025 at about $388 million (Table 4.3) [@epochai2026data], and the 2026 International AI Safety Report states that leading models cost "hundreds of millions of dollars to develop" [@bengio2026international].

::: {.box .deeper}
**Going deeper: Estimating the cost of a training run**

The simplest estimate, used by DeepSeek for its own headline figure, multiplies the time a run takes on its chips by a price per chip-hour [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3]:

*cost ≈ number of GPU-hours × rental price per GPU-hour*

DeepSeek reported that pre-training V3 took 2.664 million H800 GPU-hours on its 2,048-GPU cluster; extending the model's context window and post-training added 124,000, for a total of 2.788 million. At an assumed rental price of $2 per GPU-hour this comes to 2,788,000 × $2 = $5.576 million. The company stated that this excludes "the costs associated with prior research and ablation experiments" [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3]. The amortised method of Cottier and colleagues (Section 4.4) instead charges the run for its share of the hardware's working life, plus energy [@cottier2024rising]. A rental-price estimate asks what it would cost someone to repeat the final run; an amortised estimate asks what share of a lab's capital the run consumed. Neither includes salaries, data, failed attempts or the full purchase price of the cluster, which is why headline training costs understate what it takes to build a frontier model.

The GPU-hours can be checked against the compute estimate of Section 4.1. Spreading about 3.3 × 10^24^ operations over the 2.664 million pre-training GPU-hours (about 9.6 × 10^9^ GPU-seconds) implies an average of about 340 trillion useful operations per second per GPU. Taking the H100's dense peaks in Table 7.2 as a guide, that is about a third of the 16-bit peak and a sixth of the 8-bit peak, a reminder of how far real training falls short of a chip's specification. (The calculation is the author's, from the GPU-hours reported by DeepSeek [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3] and the H100 figures in Table 7.2; the compute rule ignores attention and other overheads.)
:::

Building the infrastructure costs far more than any training run. Leases add to these sums. Microsoft reported that at the end of June 2026 it had signed additional leases, "primarily for datacenters", worth $329.1 billion that had not yet begun. Some were subject to conditions, and they would start between fiscal years 2027 and 2033 [@microsoft2026form]. According to the IEA, the hyperscalers and newer specialised providers announced capital expenditure of about $715 billion for 2026, a 75% increase, more than was invested in the entire US energy sector in 2024. The spending will absorb almost all of their cash generation in 2026; in 2025 these normally cash-rich companies issued around $200 billion in debt, which ties the build-out to financial markets [@iea2026key, sec. 1].

Running models, rather than training them, is where costs are falling fastest. Epoch AI found that the price of reaching a given level of benchmark performance fell by between 9 and 900 times a year, depending on the level chosen [@cottier2025llm]. Cheaper tokens have not reduced total spending, because use has grown faster, and inference already accounted for most AI server energy in LBNL's 2023 estimate [@shehabi2024united].

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Can the spending pay for itself?**

*The case that it can.* Demand is growing fast. The IEA reports that major model providers saw a threefold increase in active users and a fivefold increase in revenue in a year [@iea2026key, Executive Summary]. Nvidia's data-centre revenue quadrupled in two years (Section 7.1), and the hyperscalers have so far paid for most of their investment from operating cash rather than borrowing [@iea2026key, sec. 1]. Falling prices per token widen the range of uses worth paying for.

*The case for caution.* The IEA's projections imply about $3.9 trillion of data-centre investment between 2026 and 2030, which it calls "too large to be funded solely from the balance sheets of AI companies". It notes that investors show "increasing signs of concern that this surge in spending is running ahead of AI monetisation" [@iea2026key, sec. 1]. Some demand is financed by suppliers. In September 2025 Nvidia said it intended to invest up to $100 billion in OpenAI "progressively as each gigawatt is deployed" of Nvidia systems [@nvidia2025openai], an arrangement in which the supplier's money returns as orders. In December 2025 the Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee judged that many valuations remained "materially stretched, particularly for technology companies focused on Artificial Intelligence", and that debt was playing a quickly growing role [@boe2025fpc].

In the author's view, the question turns on whether revenue grows fast enough before today's chips are superseded, and outsiders must rely largely on company announcements, which deserve the same scepticism as other company claims; the IEA likewise stresses the uncertain prospects for monetisation [@iea2026key, sec. 1].
:::

The AI Index reports that industry produced 91.2% of notable AI models in 2025, with OpenAI, Google and Alibaba the most prolific developers [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1]. On the supply side, two direct customers alone accounted for 22% and 14% of Nvidia's revenue in fiscal 2026 [@nvidia2026form]. The United States hosts more than ten times as many data centres as any other country [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 1]. A few firms' choices about pricing, access, safety practices and openness therefore shape what everyone else can build. At the same time, the AI Index notes, frontier developers now disclose less about their models than before [@sajadieh2026ai, Introduction].

::: {.box .case}
**Case study: The "DeepSeek moment", January 2025**

In late December 2024 the Chinese company DeepSeek released the weights of DeepSeek-V3, with 671 billion parameters (37 billion active for each token), and reported the $5.576 million final-run cost described above [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3]. In January 2025 it released DeepSeek-R1, a reasoning model, under the permissive MIT licence, together with smaller "distilled" versions built on Alibaba's Qwen and Meta's Llama models [@deepseekai2025deepseekr1]. On Monday 27 January 2025 Nvidia's shares fell 17%, removing nearly $600 billion from its market value, as investors questioned whether frontier AI would need as many expensive chips as expected, the Guardian reported [@milmo2025sputnik].

Later evidence complicated the story. The R1 work was published in *Nature* in September 2025. Its supplementary information gave the cost of turning the V3 base model into R1 as about 147,000 H800 GPU-hours, or about $294,000 at the same assumed $2 rental price. It also acknowledged using A100 GPUs for preparatory experiments [@guo2025deepseek]. The low headline figures were real but partial, because they excluded earlier research and the company's existing hardware. Amodei, whose company competes with DeepSeek, argued that V3 was "an expected point on an ongoing cost reduction curve" rather than a break from it [@amodei2025deepseek]. Nvidia's data-centre revenue continued to grow strongly through 2025 [@nvidia2026form]. The episode showed that engineering for efficiency, including FP8 training, can reduce the cost of approaching the frontier, and it drew wide attention to Chinese open-weight models; it did not show that compute had stopped mattering.
:::

## 7.6 Open-weight models

A trained model is a very large file of numbers, its **weights** (or parameters). A **closed-weight** model stays on the developer's servers and is reached through an app or an application programming interface (API), so the developer can monitor use, update the model and withdraw it. An **open-weight** model's weights are published for anyone to download, run and modify, for example by further training ("fine-tuning") on their own data. Between the two lie options such as staged release to researchers. At the open end, EleutherAI's GPT-NeoX was released with weights, data and code and no use restrictions; BigScience's BLOOM released all three subject to licence restrictions on use [@bengio2026international].

::: {.box .concept}
**Concept: Open-weight is not the same as open source**

In software, "open source" means that the source code is available under a licence allowing anyone to use, study, modify and share it for any purpose. For AI, the Open Source Initiative's Open Source AI Definition (version 1.0, October 2024) requires those freedoms plus access to the parameters and the full training code. It also requires information about the training data detailed enough for a skilled person to build a substantially equivalent system [@osi2024open]. Most "open" models release only weights, often under a licence with conditions, so "open-weight" is the more accurate general term.
:::

### What is released, and on what terms

Open releases of large language models accelerated after Meta's LLaMA, released to researchers in February 2023 and described in a preprint [@touvron2023llama], and especially after Llama 2 in July 2023, which Meta described as "free of charge for research and commercial use" [@meta2023meta]. Its licence nonetheless required any company with more than 700 million monthly active users to request a separate licence [@meta2023llama]. In September 2023 the French start-up Mistral AI released Mistral 7B under the permissive Apache 2.0 licence [@mistral2023mistral]. Chinese developers, led by Alibaba's Qwen family and DeepSeek, then became central; the IEA reports that in 2025 "China's models overtook those from developers in the United States in terms of total downloads on open-source platforms" [@iea2026key, Box 10.1]. In August 2025 OpenAI released its first open-weight models since GPT-2 in 2019, gpt-oss-120b and gpt-oss-20b [@openai2025gptoss; @bengio2026international]. ### How far behind?

In November 2024 Epoch AI estimated that the best open language models had lagged the best closed ones by 5 to 22 months on benchmarks (90% confidence interval), "around one year" [@cottier2024how]. The 2026 International AI Safety Report, drawing on Epoch's later capability index, judged that the gap had narrowed and that the leading closed models were "less than one year ahead" [@bengio2026international]. By May 2026 Epoch's own estimate was that open-weight models had trailed the best closed ones by an average of about four months since January [@epochai2026open]. On the LMArena leaderboard, where people vote between anonymous answers from two models, the AI Index finds that the best closed model's lead over the best open one shrank from 174 points in May 2023 to 7 points in August 2024. By March 2026, after new closed reasoning models, it had widened again to 49 points, between Claude Opus 4.6 and GLM-5 [@sajadieh2026ai, ch. 2]. Arena scores are ratings, so only differences between them mean anything. A 49-point gap implies that voters would prefer the closed model in about 57% of head-to-head comparisons (calculation by the author from the rating formula).

### Benefits

An influential framework for weighing benefits and risks comes from @kapoor2024position, 25 researchers from academia, industry and civil society. They identify five properties that distinguish open models, such as greater customisability and weak monitoring of use, and argue that these produce benefits for innovation, competition, transparency and the distribution of decision-making power. Businesses and governments can run models on their own computers without sending sensitive data to a vendor, and developers in countries without frontier labs can adapt models to under-resourced languages [@bengio2026international]. The US government's 2025 AI Action Plan made similar points, adding that open models "have geostrategic value" because they may become global standards [@whitehouse2025winning].

### Risks

The risks follow from the same properties. Once weights are published they cannot be recalled; hosting platforms can remove copies, but motivated actors can still obtain them, and developers cannot make users install fixes [@bengio2026international]. Safety training can be removed cheaply. In a paper presented at an ICLR 2024 workshop, @lermen2023lora showed that with "a budget of less than $200 and using only one GPU" they could fine-tune Meta's Llama 2-Chat 70B so that it refused harmful requests only about 1% of the time on two standard tests. The problem is not unique to open weights. @qi2023finetuning removed the safety behaviour of OpenAI's GPT-3.5 Turbo through the company's own fine-tuning service, using only 10 examples at a cost of less than $0.20. The difference is that a provider can monitor its service and roll out fixes, whereas a downloaded model is beyond reach.

Kapoor and colleagues argue that the key question is **marginal risk**: how much an open model adds to what a malicious actor could already do with search engines, existing tools or closed models. Their worked examples point in both directions. For automated searches for software vulnerabilities, they judged the marginal risk of open models to be low at present, partly because defenders can use the same tools. For digitally altered intimate images made without consent, they concluded that open image models "pose considerable marginal risk at present", because such models need few resources, can be fine-tuned on pictures of specific people and are hard to police once released. They also cite a 2023 study finding that most computer-generated child sexual abuse material had been made with the openly available Stable Diffusion family of image models [@kapoor2024position].

Technical defences are an open research problem. Tamirisa and colleagues, in a paper at ICLR 2025, presented a training method, TAR, designed so that safeguards survive "hundreds of steps of fine-tuning" by an attacker [@tamirisa2024tamper]. The International AI Safety Report notes that filtering harmful material out of training data can resist adversarial fine-tuning about ten times more effectively than defences added after training, and that such techniques are now used in some releases. It also notes that other research has disabled such safeguards, and that their robustness against real attacks remains hard to evaluate [@bengio2026international].

::: {.box .debate}
**Debate: Should the weights of the most capable models be released openly?**

*For openness by default.* @kapoor2024position argue that open models spread power beyond a few companies, enable independent safety research and support competition, and that most claimed misuse risks have not been shown to exceed those of existing technologies. In July 2024 the US National Telecommunications and Information Administration concluded that "current evidence is not sufficient to definitively determine" that restrictions on open-weight models were warranted, and recommended that government "actively monitor" risks instead [@ntia2024dual]. Before releasing gpt-oss, OpenAI fine-tuned it to be as capable as possible in biology and cybersecurity. According to a paper by the company's researchers, presented at ICLR 2026, even this version performed below OpenAI's closed o3 model, which it had judged to be under its own "High" risk threshold [@wallace2025estimating]. That is a developer's evaluation of its own product, without independent replication.

*For caution at the frontier.* In a preprint, @seger2023opensourcing accept that open-sourcing has historically brought large net benefits. They argue, however, that for some highly capable future models the risks may be extreme enough that such models "should not be open-sourced, at least not initially". Cheap removal of safeguards [@lermen2023lora] makes release decisions irreversible, and documented harms from open image models show that marginal risk is not only hypothetical [@kapoor2024position].

In the author's assessment, both sides accept that the answer turns on marginal risk at particular capability levels [@kapoor2024position; @seger2023opensourcing], which makes it an empirical question. Independent evaluations of what open models add to specific harms, repeated as each generation improves, would narrow the disagreement, as would evidence on whether tamper-resistant training survives determined attackers; Chapter 12 discusses such dangerous-capability evaluations.
:::

Open weights lower the cost of entry for firms and countries that cannot afford frontier training, partly offsetting concentration. They also complicate export controls. The January 2025 diffusion rule controlled the weights of certain advanced closed-weight models but left the weights of open-weight models outside its scope, on the ground that the most advanced of them were less powerful than the leading closed models [@bis2025framework]. A model trained on restricted chips can be downloaded anywhere the day it is published.

## 7.7 Compute governance and sovereign AI

Data and algorithms are hard to observe and easy to copy, so some researchers argue that hardware is an especially practical lever for governing AI. In a preprint, @sastry2024computing, a group of 19 authors including Yoshua Bengio, argue that AI-relevant compute is "detectable, excludable, and quantifiable" and is produced through "an extremely concentrated supply chain". **Compute governance** uses these properties to give public authorities visibility into AI development, allocate resources and enforce rules. Sastry and colleagues also warn of risks to privacy, economic harms and centralisation of power, and propose guardrails such as collecting only the minimum information needed.

Proposals range from paperwork to hardware. Heim and colleagues argue in a preprint that cloud providers could act as record keepers and verifiers. In their view, information the providers already hold, without seeing customers' data, can reveal whether a workload is large-scale training and how much compute it used [@heim2024governing]. In another preprint, Shavit proposed a more ambitious scheme in which chip firmware would occasionally save snapshots of the weights being trained, so that inspectors could later verify what a training run did. Monitoring of the chip supply chain would ensure that no actor could amass untracked chips [@shavit2023what].

Training compute is already used as a regulatory threshold. The EU AI Act presumes that a general-purpose AI model has "high impact capabilities", and is subject to additional obligations, when the computation used for its training exceeds 10^25^ floating-point operations [@europeanparliament2024regulation, Art. 51(2)]. DeepSeek-V3's pre-training used about 3.3 × 10^24^ operations (Section 4.1), a third of the threshold, yet its developers report that it performed comparably to leading closed models of its time [@deepseekai2024deepseekv3]. Efficiency gains of this kind are one reason fixed compute thresholds are contested; Chapter 13 discusses thresholds and their limits.

The same logic of control over compute underlies **sovereign AI**, the effort by governments to secure national access to AI infrastructure and models rather than depending entirely on foreign providers. The 2026 AI Index notes that "AI sovereignty emerged as a central organizing principle" in national policies during 2025 [@sajadieh2026ai, Introduction]. Strategies differ in scale and emphasis:

- **European Union.** In February 2025 the European Commission launched InvestAI, aiming to mobilise €200 billion for AI investment, including a €20 billion fund for four "AI gigafactories", each with around 100,000 of the latest AI chips [@ec2025eu]. Consortia were to be selected in early 2027 [@eurohpc2026eurohpc].
- **India.** In March 2024 the Indian cabinet approved over Rs 10,300 crore over five years for the IndiaAI Mission, including a plan for public–private compute capacity of more than 10,000 GPUs [@pib2024cabinet]. By February 2026 more than 38,000 GPUs had been made available through the mission [@pib2026democratising].
- **United Kingdom.** The AI Opportunities Action Plan of January 2025 recommended expanding the country's public AI Research Resource "by at least 20x by 2030" and creating "AI Growth Zones" to speed up data-centre construction [@dsit2025ai].
- **United States.** The July 2025 AI Action Plan called for exporting the "full AI technology stack" (hardware, models, software, applications and standards) to allied countries, so that they would not become dependent on rivals' technology [@whitehouse2025winning].

US private AI investment reached $285.9 billion in 2025, against $12.4 billion in China, although the AI Index cautions that the Chinese figure omits much state funding [@sajadieh2026ai, Report highlights]. Even India's 38,000 GPUs are small next to the 17.1 million H100-equivalents of global capacity, and Microsoft alone spent $115.9 billion on equipment in its 2026 fiscal year (Table 7.4). For most countries "sovereignty" therefore means reliable access, local data centres or open-weight models adapted to national languages, not training frontier models from scratch.

Sovereign strategies also run into the chokepoints of Section 7.2. A country can build data centres on its own soil and still depend on Nvidia's designs, TSMC's fabs, Korean and American memory and ASML's machines, dependencies shaped by US export policy, as the UAE arrangement of 2026 illustrates [@bis2026enhanced]. Open-weight models offer a partial route to independence at the model layer, which is one reason governments as different as the United States and China have encouraged them [@whitehouse2025winning; @iea2026key, Box 10.1]. For most countries, then, sovereignty over models is within reach, while sovereignty over the hardware that runs them is not.

In the author's summary of the evidence above, each layer of the stack runs into a scarce input that few suppliers control. Memory bandwidth limits how fast chips serve models, HBM and packaging capacity limit how many accelerators are made, and grid connections and turbines limit where they can run. Export controls are possible because the hardware chokepoints sit mostly in the United States and allied countries, and the rules have followed the bottleneck from arithmetic to memory. Efficiency gains, from FP8 training to cheaper queries, have so far been outrun by demand, so electricity use and capital spending keep rising. Open weights spread access to models far more widely than access to hardware, so the decisions of a few firms and governments over chips, power and capital still set the terms for everyone else.

- International Energy Agency (2026). *Key Questions on Energy and AI*. The most detailed public analysis of data-centre electricity, with transparent scenarios, data tables and a clear account of supply-chain bottlenecks. [@iea2026key]

- Kapoor, S., et al. (2024). *Position: On the Societal Impact of Open Foundation Models*. The standard framework for weighing the benefits and marginal risks of open-weight models. [@kapoor2024position]
- Seger, E., et al. (2023). *Open-Sourcing Highly Capable Foundation Models*. A careful statement of the case for caution, best read alongside Kapoor et al. [@seger2023opensourcing]
- Sastry, G., et al. (2024). *Computing Power and the Governance of Artificial Intelligence*. Explains why hardware is a lever for governing AI, and what could go wrong. [@sastry2024computing]
