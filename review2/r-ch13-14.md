# Second review: Chapters 13 (law and policy) and 14 (forecasting, proposals, ending)

Reviewer scope: `textbook/chapters/ch13.md` (310 lines) and `ch14.md` (282 lines), read in full; bib/ch13.bib and ch14.bib entries for every claim checked; figures 13.1, 13.2, 14.1–14.4 and Tables 13.1–13.4 and 14.1–14.5 checked in the built PDF (pp. 397–463). Today is 3 November 2026. I did not read `review/`.

## 1. Verdict: publish after minor fixes

For these two chapters, the answer to "Can I really put this out like that?" is: almost. A small set of fixes is needed first, and none of them requires restructuring.

The reasons that decide it:

1. **Accuracy on very recent law is unusually high.** I checked 58 claims against primary or close-to-primary sources (Table in §6). Most of them concern events after mid-2026. They include the Digital Omnibus (Reg. 2026/1744) dates and content, EO 14409 and EO 14434, *xAI v. Weiser* and Colorado SB 26‑189, the Third Circuit's *Thomson Reuters v. Ross* opinion, the Munich *GEMA v. Suno* judgment, the *Bartz* final approval, the Euronews report on enforcement against OpenAI and Anthropic, the eight state AG suits, and the FRI and ESPAI 2024 forecasting data. All of them check out, down to quotations and footnotes. I found no fabricated or misdated legal event.
2. **The legal analysis is precise where it counts.** The AI Act walk-through (classification under Art. 6(3), Arts 9–15, internal-control conformity assessment under Art. 43(2), the FRIA limits in Art. 27, the Art. 73 deadlines, the fines in Arts 99/101) matches the regulation's text. The copyright section reads the European cases carefully and correctly separates input from output.
3. **The proposals are clearly fenced off from reportage** (ch14:183, Table 14.5, preface). Each proposal states its strongest objection. The three developed proposals (14.2, 14.3, 14.6) are argued with real evidence and real self-criticism.
4. **One material mischaracterisation must be fixed.** The FRONTIER Act is presented as a pure pre-emption bill. It is a bipartisan federal frontier-AI oversight bill that pre-empts state law in the same subject areas. This skews the chapter's US narrative.
5. **The ending works for Chapter 14 but only partly for the book.** The "bottom line" is honest and testable. But it closes on a new thesis (correlated model families) rather than drawing together what thirteen chapters established. One of its key sentences misstates the opening evidence, and the misstatement is stale against the chapter's own data.

## 2. Blocking issues

**B1. The FRONTIER Act is described as pre-emption only (ch13:148).**
- Quote: "The legislative route followed with the FRONTIER Act (H.R. 9925), introduced on 23 July 2026 by Representative Jay Obernolte. It would bar states from imposing new substantive obligations on AI developers in three 'covered subject areas'…"
- Problem: The bill is bipartisan (Obernolte with Trahan, Franklin, Peters, Houchin and Subramanyan). It creates federal obligations tiered by developer size: model cards, risk-management frameworks, independent audits, incident reporting and ongoing assessments. Pre-emption is the counterpart to that federal standard, not the bill's whole content. As written, a reader concludes that Congress's only move is to strip state law. That reinforces the chapter's framing ("Federal action in 2026 continued to favour voluntary arrangements", ch13:142, and the conclusion at ch13:301) beyond what the evidence supports. The book's own source describes the bill, so the error lies in the summary, not the research.
- Evidence: Benton/press release, 24 July 2026 (https://www.benton.org/headlines/reps-obernolte-trahan-introduce-bipartisan-frontier-act-strengthen-oversight-advanced-ai); Washington Examiner, "Bipartisan AI oversight bill introduced in House"; LegiScan HB9925.
- Fix: "…introduced on 23 July 2026 by Representatives Jay Obernolte and Lori Trahan with bipartisan co-sponsors. It would impose federal duties on large frontier developers, including risk frameworks, independent audits and incident reports, and in exchange bar states from imposing new obligations in three 'covered subject areas'…". Add a clause to ch13:301 noting that a federal frontier bill is pending.

No other blocking issues were found in my scope.

## 3. Major issues

**M1. The premise of the proposals overstates the gap in the AI Act (ch14:183).**
- Quote: "Proposals 14.1 to 14.4 address risks that grow with adoption rather than capability, which … rules triggered by a single model's training compute (Section 13.2) do not reach [art. 51(2)]."
- Problem: Article 51(2) is only the compute *presumption*. Under Art. 51(1)(b) and Annex XIII, the Commission can designate a systemic-risk model on criteria that explicitly include reach: number of registered end-users, and a presumption at 10,000 registered EU business users. Art. 3(65) also defines systemic risk partly "due to their reach". The law therefore does reach adoption, though at model level rather than fleet level. The proposals' real point, that correlated behaviour across *deployments* is not evaluated, survives, but the premise as stated is wrong in law. Chapter 13 itself says "The EU can designate models on other grounds" (ch13:116).
- Fix: "…which compute triggers do not reach, and which the EU's reach-based designation criteria (Annex XIII) address only at the level of a single model, not of correlated fleets."

**M2. The link from proposals 14.7–14.10 back to Chapter 13 is not supported (ch14:183).**
- Quote: "Proposals 14.7 to 14.10 concern the obligations and records of agents acting over time, where Chapter 13 found liability law thinnest."
- Problem: Section 13.7 discusses product liability, tort suits over chatbots, AG suits and Section 230. It never discusses agents acting over time, agent-to-agent contracts, memory, or posthumous agents. The cross-reference claims a finding the earlier chapter does not make.
- Fix: Either add two sentences to 13.7 on agent-made commitments (who is bound, and what record exists), or reword this sentence to say "where Chapter 13's liability regimes say little".

**M3. Proposals 14.8–14.10 are named but not argued (ch14:258).**
- Problem: Three of the ten proposals are compressed into one paragraph. The text says "Table 14.5 carries their detail", but the table gives only one research question and one objection for each. For readers this reads as a list of coined terms ("cognitive custody", "covenant inheritance") rather than proposals, which dilutes the three proposals that are well argued. About a third of Chapter 14 is now the author's proposals, which unbalances a chapter titled "Forecasting the Next Ten Years".
- Fix: Keep 14.1–14.7 (or just the three developed ones plus 14.1). Move 14.8–14.10 to a short "Further open problems" box, or cut them. If kept, give each at least the evidence–mechanism–objection structure that 14.2 gets.

**M4. The book's closing sentence misstates its own opening evidence (ch14:271).**
- Quote: "…and researchers' answers moved by seventy years with the wording of a question [@grace2025thousands]."
- Problem: The ~70-year gap (2047 against 2116) is between two *different questions* (HLMI and FAOL), which ch14:5 itself calls "two questions". It is not a shift in the same question's wording. The true wording effect (fixed-years against fixed-probabilities) is 17 against 34 years (ch14:45). Moreover, the 2024 round, reported by the chapter at ch14:45 and in Fig. 14.1, narrowed the HLMI–FAOL gap to 56 years. In the passage meant to be the book's last word on forecasting, the number is both imprecise and out of date.
- Fix: "…and researchers' answers differed by seven decades (56 years in the latest round) between two framings of almost the same milestone."

**M5. The ending closes Chapter 14 but not the book (ch14:271–273).**
- Problem: The preface promises "the bottom line that ends the book". The final paragraph's thesis is that reliability and organisational adoption will decide the decade, and that correlated model families are the main risk. It is well hedged and testable, which is good. But it does not connect to the governance findings of Chapter 13 ("timetables proved soft", courts settling what statutes left open), to the risk evidence in Chapters 11–12, or to the labour evidence in Chapter 8. A reader finishing the book gets the author's research agenda, not a synthesis. It is not a defect of fact, but a serious publisher would ask for a closing page.
- Fix: Add 150–250 words before "The author's bottom line" that name the three or four things the book's evidence most firmly establishes, then the bottom line. Alternatively, retitle the last section "What to watch, and the author's view" so that it does not promise more than it gives.

**M6. The Omnibus summary omits the Machinery Regulation carve-out (ch13:52–74, Table 13.1 line 64).**
- Problem: In the final text, products under the Machinery Regulation are channelled through that regime, and the AI Act applies to them directly only in part. This was one of the most contested trilogue outcomes. Table 13.1's "High-risk, Annex I … 2 Aug 2028" row and the "in order of consequence" list therefore overstate how uniformly Annex I still applies. The restored simplified registration for Art. 6(3) self-exempted systems is also missing.
- Evidence: Stephenson Harwood, "The Digital Omnibus on AI: ten key takeaways" (July 2026), item 10; Hunton, "EU Digital Omnibus on AI enters into force".
- Fix: Add one bullet: "Machinery. AI in machinery is regulated mainly through the Machinery Regulation rather than directly under the Act."

## 4. Minor issues

- **ch13:46.** "Each use needs prior authorisation by a judicial or independent administrative authority" omits the Art. 5(3) urgency exception: use may begin without authorisation if it is requested within 24 hours. Add "except in duly justified urgency, when it must be sought within 24 hours".
- **ch13:25.** "As a regulation, it binds all 27 member states directly without national implementing laws" is imprecise. The Act requires national laws designating authorities and setting penalties (Arts 70, 99), and the chapter itself reports the lag in doing so (ch13:122). Fix: "applies directly, although member states must still designate authorities and set penalty rules".
- **ch14:167, Table 14.4.** "In April 2026 NIST's … CAISI … estimated that DeepSeek V4 … lagged the frontier by about eight months". The CAISI evaluation was published on 1 May 2026 (https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2026/05/caisi-evaluation-deepseek-v4-pro), and the bib URL says 2026/05. Change to "In May 2026".
- **ch13:240.** The transition to the DOJ statement ("It noted that the Department of Justice had filed…") hides the legally important point. In footnote 7 the Third Circuit *distinguished* the DOJ's position because ROSS's tool was not generative and was a direct substitute. Say so in one clause. The current wording attributes the description of the DOJ filing to Bloomberg only.
- **Table 13.4.** It omits *GEMA v. Suno* (Munich, 31 Jul 2026), which the text treats as half of the Munich line (ch13:250, 254). Add a row.
- **ch13:190.** The citation key `xinhua2025china` points to a China Daily page, and the text correctly says "China Daily". Rename the key to avoid confusion in the numbered source list.
- **ch13:194.** "Two other Asian democracies have passed horizontal AI statutes": "other" has no antecedent (the preceding paragraph is about the UK), and Japan's Act is a promotion statute with no duties beyond cooperation, as the paragraph itself shows. Suggest: "South Korea and Japan have each passed an economy-wide AI statute, at very different strengths."
- **ch13:244.** The dismissal of Register Perlmutter is reported without the litigation that followed (she obtained interim reinstatement in 2025, and the matter went to the Supreme Court). I could not verify the status as of October 2026. Either add "a dismissal she challenged in court" or drop the sentence; it is tangential.
- **ch13:294.** Demirer et al. figures (25%, 21%) are attributed to the 2024 working paper while the citation is the JPE article (Crossref confirms doi 10.1086/744118, 18 Sep 2026). NBER summaries and revisions variously give 26% and 20–22%. Cite the published version's numbers, or say "in the working-paper version".
- **Fig. 13.2.** It mixes signature dates (Texas, SB 53, RAISE) with entry-into-force dates (China anthropomorphic AI "Jul 2026", Korea "Jan 2026"). Label the event type or make the dates consistent. The lane text is about 6 pt at A5 and is barely legible at print size.
- **Fig. 14.2.** The single y-axis "AI capability and real-world impact" conflates the two quantities whose divergence is the chapter's main point (ch14:141: the views "disagree less about current capabilities than about how quickly capability turns into change"). The "fast acceleration" curve also leaves the fan, which contradicts "each scenario marks out a region of the fan". Either label the axis "real-world impact" or note the conflation in the caption.
- **ch14:21.** This is the densest paragraph in the chapter: a FrontierMath definition embedded mid-sentence, then five separate findings. Split it after "Table 14.1" and move the benchmark definition to a parenthesis or footnote.
- **ch14:107.** The "about 17 hours" 50% horizon (May 2026) is cited to FRI and METR, but the FRI review gives only the 3.1-hour 80% figure. Make sure the METR page is the cited source for 17 h.
- **ch13:212, 214.** "21 signatories" (CoE convention) and "delegations from 163 countries" (Geneva Dialogue) are unverifiable from accessible sources (the coe.int treaty office returns 403). Keep them only if the logged primary page supports them.
- **Voice markers.** "In the author's view/judgement/assessment/reading" appears 13 times in Chapter 14 and 6 in Chapter 13. The labelling is principled, but in 14.4–14.5 it becomes a tic. Where a sentence is plainly analysis, one marker per paragraph is enough.

## 5. What works

- **The Chapter 13 opening.** The Omnibus delay and the Colorado retreat are concrete, dated and immediately make the point that "every rule below is dated".
- **The hiring-tool walk-through (13.2).** It is the clearest lay explanation of AI Act high-risk compliance I have seen. It correctly identifies who does what, when the FRIA applies, and when it does not.
- **The tables.** Table 13.1 (old against new dates, with article references), Table 13.3 (EU/CA/NY frontier regimes) and Table 13.4 (case status) are exactly what a reader needs, and they are correct.
- **The European copyright synthesis (ch13:254).** "What the model itself retains" as the common thread across Getty, GEMA and LAION is an original and defensible reading.
- **Chapter 14's honesty about its own evidence.** The bias in interim reviews towards finding underestimates (ch14:37) and the arithmetic-not-forecast caveat on Table 14.2 are both good. The Brier worked example is correct (0.125 and 0.205, and 0.955/0.835 for the Olympiad).
- **Proposals 14.2, 14.3 and 14.6.** The 50.2 Hz case is verified (Döring, about 14 GW; SysStabV), as is the UK random-delay rule (SI 2021/1467 reg. 11: 600 s default, 1800 s capability, owner cancel, DSR exception). Each proposal names its own strongest objection, and the signature-capacity design anticipates the incentive to sandbag. This is how speculative proposals should be written in a nonfiction book.
