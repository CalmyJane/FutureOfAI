# Second review: Chapters 9 and 10

Reviewer scope: `textbook/chapters/ch09.md` (Information, Media and Democracy) and `textbook/chapters/ch10.md` (Education, Health and Everyday Life), with their bibliographies, figures 9.1–9.4 and 10.1–10.3, and Tables 9.1–9.3 and 10.1–10.3. Typeset pages 268–330 of the A5 PDF were spot-rendered. I read every line of both chapters. I used the evidence logs only as leads. Every check below was made against the source itself: full text from Europe PMC, arXiv or publisher PDFs, official pages, Crossref, or news reports.

## 1. Verdict

**Publish after minor fixes.**

The reasons that decide it:

1. **Factual accuracy is very high.** I checked about 75 load-bearing claims against their sources: 71 were confirmed exactly or within rounding. One was clearly wrong (the sample size of the Ghana Rori trial) and two were partly wrong. Ten I could not verify. No number that carries an argument was invented or distorted.
2. **The handling of method is better than most published trade nonfiction and much academic writing.** The chapters separate intention-to-treat from effects on users. They work out a treatment-on-treated sanity check for the Maryland trial (2.5 SD, rightly called implausible). They flag differential attrition in the Nigeria trial with exact counts, and note the effect of experimenter-made tests (Cheung and Slavin). They distinguish non-inferiority from superiority in MASAI. They note that Figure 9.3's gap is twice a one-sided effect, apply base rates to detectors, and keep correlation apart from cause on companions and loneliness. Each chapter builds on its "Going deeper" boxes.
3. **Balance is good.** Debate boxes give both sides fairly. Industry sources are labelled as such (Meta, OpenAI, Google, Vara funding, OpenAI co-authorship). Contested regulation (the DSA, the TAKE IT DOWN Act) is given critics from both the right and civil liberties groups.
4. **What remains is fixable in days.** It consists of one wrong sample size, a few time-sensitive sentences that are already stale against the book's "late October 2026" claim, one overstated attribution to a named political organisation, one citation that does not support its sentence, and some figure and number points that depend on which version of a paper was used.
5. **Legal risk is low.** Statements about named people and companies (Kramer, Georgescu, Epic, X/xAI, Character.AI, OpenAI, Fidesz) are attributed and qualified ("alleged", "according to", "estimated"). The one exception is the plural "National Republican committees" (see B3).

## 2. Blocking issues (fix before publication; each is small)

**B1. The Ghana Rori trial's sample is overstated by about double.** `ch10.md:29` (Table 10.1) reads "Ghana, 11 schools, grades 3–9; about 1,000 students", and `ch10.md:38` says "was used by about 1,000 pupils in grades 3 to 9".

- Problem: "about 1,000" comes from the preprint's abstract. The paper's own participation section says: "Initially, 637 students in grades 3-8 participated in the baseline assessment and out of that group, 477 students also completed the endline. The attrition of 160 students was primarily due to inconsistent school attendance." The 0.37 effect is therefore estimated on 477 pupils, after 25% attrition. In a book that criticises the Nigeria trial for its attrition, leaving this out is inconsistent and inflates the apparent strength of the evidence.
- Evidence: arXiv 2402.09809, Section 3.2 (PDF read).
- Fix: change the table cell to "11 schools, grades 3–8; 477 pupils analysed (637 at baseline)", and add one sentence on attrition to the paragraph.

**B2. The Brazil sentence is stale.** `ch09.md:128` reads: "Brazil, whose presidential run-off falls on 25 October 2026, went furthest."

- Problem: the book says it is current to late October 2026, and the run-off has now taken place. A reader in 2027 will take the future tense as an error.
- Fix: rewrite in the past tense ("Brazil, which held its presidential run-off on 25 October 2026, …"). Either add whether the 72-hour ban was tested, or cut the date. The same sentence pattern applies to "In the 2026 US midterms" in the same paragraph: the source is a Reuters piece from March 2026, and the midterms were held on 3 November 2026. Make it clear that this describes the campaign as of spring 2026.

**B3. Overstated attribution to named political organisations.** `ch09.md:128` reads: "National Republican committees ran ads, labelled in small print, in which AI versions of Democratic candidates spoke".

- Problem: the cited Reuters article (via Star-Advertiser) names one national committee: the NRSC's ad of James Talarico, with "AI generated" in "easy-to-miss font". The other cases are a county committee (Loudoun County) and a candidate's campaign (Mike Collins). Using the plural for national party bodies goes beyond the source and is the one place in my scope where wording about a named organisation is looser than its evidence.
- Evidence: https://www.staradvertiser.com/2026/03/28/breaking-news/ai-deepfakes-blur-reality-in-2026-us-midterm-campaigns/
- Fix: "The National Republican Senatorial Committee ran an ad, labelled in small print, in which an AI version of the Democratic Senate candidate James Talarico spoke, and other Republican campaigns did likewise".

**B4. A citation does not support its sentence.** `ch10.md:196` reads "an Italian fine on OpenAI against which a Rome court upheld the company's appeal in 2026 [@edpb2024opinion; @garante2024chatgpt]".

- Problem: `garante2024chatgpt` is the Garante's 2024 release that closed its investigation. It cannot document a 2026 court ruling. Chapter 13 cites the right sources (`garante2026tribunale`, `papini2026openai`).
- Fix: add those keys to the `ch10` bib and cite them here.

## 3. Major issues

**M1. The Hackenburg et al. (Science 2025) numbers depend on the version used, and one pair does not add up.** This affects `ch09.md:146`, Table 9.2 (`ch09.md:159–160`) and Figure 9.3 Panel A.

- What is consistent across versions: the arXiv preprint (2507.13919) gives "+2.94pp, +41%" for GPT-4o and "+3.60pp, +52%" for GPT-4.5. It gives 36%/42% retained at one month, 9 minutes, personalisation "+0.43pp … never exceeds 1pp" and maximal persuasion of 15.9 pp. I confirmed all of these.
- The problem: the book's static effects of 6.1 and 6.9 points come from the LSE accepted manuscript, which the evidence log says may differ from the published text. A 41% gain on 6.1 is 2.5 pp, not 2.94. On the preprint's figures the GPT-4o static effect would be about 7.2. The preprint also gives 29.7% of claims inaccurate, where the book gives 30.0%.
- Fix: check the published *Science* version (DOI 10.1126/science.aea3884), which is the one cited, and use its numbers throughout.
- Figure 9.3 Panel A also needs attention. It sets same-model static bars (GPT-4o, GPT-4.5) against the average conversation over all 19 models (9.4). It also shows the "maximal persuasion" figure, a cross-fitted model estimate rather than an observed arm, as a bar like the others. Label that bar "estimated" and either show the same-model conversation bars or say in the caption that the average spans all models.

**M2. The corrected Costello sample is not verifiable from public sources.** This affects `ch09.md:144` and Table 9.2 ("2,044 (corrected)").

- Confirmed: Crossref has the Expression of Concern (11 June 2026, now titled "RESCINDED") and an erratum dated 22 October 2026.
- Not confirmed: I could not find "2,044" anywhere I could reach. The original paper and Retraction Watch both give 2,190.
- Fix: because the erratum appeared only 10 days before the book's currency date, quote the erratum's exact wording and page, or write "about 2,000 after correction".

**M3. The Kalla and Broockman baseline is described only in part.** `ch09.md:134` reads "In primaries and ballot-measure campaigns, where party labels give voters no cue, the field experiments they reviewed did find persuasive effects".

- Problem: the abstract names the two exceptions that matter for AI campaigning: candidates who take unusually unpopular positions and target persuadable voters heavily, and early contact measured immediately, whose effect decays. The decay is directly relevant to the durability figures in Table 9.2.
- Fix: add one sentence giving both exceptions.

**M4. Units are inconsistent in Section 9.5.** `ch09.md:134` sets a convention: "'points' are points on such scales, not percentage points". Hackenburg and Margetts report "4.83 vs. 6.20 percentage points" (PNAS abstract), and `ch09.md:140` says "percentage points" while Table 9.2 (`ch09.md:155`) says "points".

- Fix: pick one usage and apply it. This matters because readers will compare across rows despite the caption's warning.

**M5. Some figures and sentences are near the edge of the currency claim and have drifted.**

- `ch09.md:59`: Google's SynthID page, updated 29 October 2026, lists Apple among current partners. "With Apple to follow" may now be out of date.
- `ch09.md:246` ("here xAI"): Ofcom's October 2026 statement calls the company "SpaceXAI, formerly xAI". Check the provider's legal name, since the passage assigns a legal duty to it.
- `ch09.md:199`: the Penske dismissal was without prejudice, according to TheWrap. Add "without prejudice" so the reader does not take it as a final ruling.

**M5a. Possible balance and legal point on the Epic Sepsis Model.** The Epic case study (`ch10.md:124`) says the model "was implemented at hundreds of US hospitals although its performance had not been adequately evaluated" in the author's own voice.

- This tracks the wording of the JAMA Internal Medicine abstract, but it is a statement about a named vendor.
- Fix: attribute it ("Wong and colleagues noted that…"), and optionally add Epic's public response of 2021 that local threshold tuning was expected.

**M6. Chapter 9 is long and repeats itself in places.** It runs to about 10,000 words of body text.

- Altay and colleagues' "preaches to the choir" is quoted twice (`ch09.md:25` and `ch09.md:121`).
- Kapoor and Narayanan is summarised three times (9.1, 9.4, and the Debate box).
- The 2026 election round-up (`ch09.md:126–128`) reads as a news digest, and it is the part that will date fastest.
- Fix: cut about 10% from Section 9.4 and the Debate box's "Largely, yes" paragraph. Trimming the election round-up also reduces the currency risk in B2.

## 4. Minor issues

- **Robocall recipients.** `ch09.md:3` says "an estimated tens of thousands of Democratic voters". The figure is taken from Barrington et al.'s introduction, a secondary source. The New Hampshire Attorney General's estimate was 5,000–25,000, and AP says "thousands". Soften the wording or cite the primary source.
- **What Pew's 5% measures.** `ch09.md:195` says "News sites accounted for 5% of the sources the summaries cited". Pew's wording is "5% of AI summaries – and 5% of standard search results – linked to news websites", which is a share of summaries, not a share of cited sources. Align the wording.
- **Table 10.1 caption.** `ch10.md:34` says "with random or matched assignment". No study in the table uses matched assignment; the closest is Bastani, where classes were assigned to arms to balance observables. Say that instead.
- **Rori sentence** (`ch10.md:38`). After B1, note that the 0.37 is on the analysed sample of 477 pupils.
- **Kestin crossover** (`ch10.md:36`). The analysed post-test counts differ by arm (N = 142 for the AI arm, 174 for in-class), so "each student took one lesson in each format" holds for eligibility but not for every analysed comparison. A clause would fix this.
- **Nickow citation key.** The key `nickow2020impressive` points to the 2024 *AERJ* article. This does not show in print, but rename the key to avoid future mismatches. The same applies to `zhang2025rise`, which points to a 2026 paper.
- **Fig. 9.3 caption.** It says "with 95% confidence intervals where reported". Panel A's static bars have no whiskers; say so.
- **Page layout.** On page 284 of the PDF, Table 9.2 continues and then leaves about two-thirds of the page blank before Figure 9.3. Check float placement.
- **Accessibility section is thin and dated** (`ch10.md:198–206`). Its main evaluation is a CHI 2024 study with 16 participants, and Be My Eyes dates from 2023. Either add a 2025–26 evaluation or shorten the section and say plainly that the evidence is sparse.
- **Romania case box** (`ch09.md:222`). It ends without saying that the election was rerun in May 2025. One clause would close the story for readers.
- **House Judiciary report** (`ch09.md:227`). "The report is a partisan document" is a fair description, but it is editorial. "issued by the committee's Republican majority" (already stated) plus "rests on the committee's own selection of documents" is enough.
- **Bastani percentages.** The text should keep the "points obtained" wording throughout, so that "17% lower" is not read as percentage points. It already does this at `ch10.md:48`; keep it consistent elsewhere.

## 5. What works

- Both opening vignettes (the New Hampshire robocall; Nigeria versus Turkey) set up each chapter's argument: what is measured, and how the tool is set up, decides the verdict.
- The "Going deeper" boxes on text watermarks, C2PA manifests, effect sizes and education trials, and medical evidence ladders are accurate and teach well. The arithmetic checks out: z = 4 gives about 3×10⁻⁵; a shift of 0.3 SD moves a student to the 62nd percentile; 98.5% specificity on 10,000 people gives 150 false alarms; the detector base-rate example gives 91% false alarms.
- The figures are faithful to their sources. In Figure 9.1, the AUC and perplexity points (0.998/6.3 to 0.696/33.9) and the z = 4 detection rates (82% and 35%) match Kirchenbauer et al. Figure 9.4 matches Pew. Figure 10.2 matches the FDA list exactly: I re-parsed the September 2026 list and got 1,614 devices, year counts of 6 (2015) to 335 (2025), and 1,553 K / 40 DEN / 21 P submissions.
- The chapters use evidence honestly when it cuts against the narrative: the null result in Kenya (Penda Health), the null at the AMIE threshold, MASAI's non-significant reduction, and the confound in the Maryland trial.
- Corrections to the literature are tracked to the week, including the Costello Expression of Concern and erratum, and the Pew digital-twin study.
- The prose is plain, precise and unhyped. The author's judgements are marked as such, about 10 to 12 times per chapter. That is slightly frequent but acceptable.
